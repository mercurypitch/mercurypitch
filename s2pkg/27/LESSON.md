## Three words you'll meet

- **Seventh chord**: a triad with one more third stacked on top. Its four notes are its root,
  third, fifth and seventh. [Play: C, E, G and B]
- **Major seventh**: seven letters, both ends counted, and 11 semitones, a semitone short of the
  octave: C up to B. [Play: C up to B]
- **Minor seventh**: seven letters too, and 10 semitones, a semitone narrower: C up to B♭. [Play: C
  up to B♭]

## Step 1 of 4: How do you build a seventh chord?

Stacking thirds as Lesson 12 did, add one more to a triad and you have a seventh chord, named by
its triad and its seventh. On do, C, E and G take a B, a major seventh above C. [Play C, E and G,
then C, E, G and B] A major triad with a major seventh is a major seventh chord.

On so, G, B and D take an F, a minor seventh above G. [Play G, B and D, then G, B, D and F] A major
triad with a minor seventh is a dominant seventh chord.

(Held until the cold open's reveal, the paragraph above shows with it, its play included. Until

## Step 2 of 4: Does an extra note just make a chord richer?

[Play the major seventh chord, then the dominant seventh] Both are a major triad with one more note.
Now hear where one of them goes. [Play the dominant seventh, then I]

> **Common mix-up:** "Adding a note just makes a chord richer."
> **Why it seems right:** one open textbook, pairing each seventh chord with a mood as a way to
> learn its sound, suggests hearing the major seventh chord as "happy and jazzy".
> **The evidence:** the same book suggests hearing the dominant seventh as "unresolved", "like they
> strongly need to move to another chord". It says the added note adds dissonance, which
> strengthens the pull of V towards I.
> **The better idea:** what a seventh does depends on the chord it joins. On V, it pulls home.

**Try:** play each seventh chord, then the dominant seventh going home.
**Notice:** on the ring, the added corner sits one bead from a corner of I.

(Held until the cold open's reveal, the step's text above, its plays, the card, Try and Notice show

## Step 3 of 4: Why does the dominant seventh pull home?

Lesson 23's last step ended on a pair: B and F, ti and fa, a tritone apart, which counts as
restless. V holds ti, and its added note, fa, makes the pair. [Play B and F, then C and E] In V7 to
I, ti tends to rise a semitone to do, and a seventh tends to fall a step, here fa to mi: the tritone
closes to a major third. [Play V7, then I] With F♯ instead, B and F♯ are seven semitones apart, a
perfect fifth, which counts as settled.

**Try:** play B and F, then V7 going home.
**Notice:** B and F sit straight across the ring, then each moves one bead, to C and E.

(Held until the cold open's reveal, the step's text above, its plays, Try, Notice and Go deeper
show with it. Until then the step shows its heading and "Choose an answer at the top, then hear both
chords, to unlock this step." FACTS 1a, 40.)

_Toy: the ring with the V7 room, its B to F diagonal drawn across the ring's centre, and the I room;
the step's two plays show with its held text._

## Step 4 of 4: Which note names a chord?

**Predict:** Merc plays C, E and G, then moves C up an octave: E, G and C. What is the chord now?
[ Still C major ] [ E minor ]

(Until the cold open's choice, both choices and both plays are disabled and the hint reads "Choose

Reveal: Still C major, its root still C; E minor is E, G and B. (Lesson 23's intervals changed
names upside down, and Lessons 10 and 12 called the root the bottom note, which made E minor a fair

That holds in root position. The bottom note is the bass: with C major's third in the bass, it's in
first inversion, written C/E; with its fifth, second. A seventh chord's third inversion has its
seventh in the bass.

**Try:** turn V7 over three times.
**Notice:** the bass changes; the room stays.

_Toy: the ring with two chips, I and V7, a mark on the corner in the bass, Turn over and the

wait for this step's choice, which itself waits for the cold open's._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Figures.** Older music writes an inversion as numbers with the bass note, the intervals above
>   it: 6 for a first inversion, 6 over 4 for a second. One open textbook says musicians shortened
>   the figures "to save time and supplies (paper and ink were very expensive before the industrial
>   revolution)".

## Wrap

- Stack one more third on a triad and you have a seventh chord, named by its triad and its seventh.
  On do it's a major seventh chord.
- On so the added note is fa: the dominant seventh, V7. Its ti and fa are Lesson 23's tritone, and
  in V7 to I they tend to close inwards to do and mi.
- A chord is named by its root, whatever is in the bass: root position, then first, second and, for
  a seventh chord, third inversion.
