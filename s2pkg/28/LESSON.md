## Step 1 of 4: What makes a phrase sound finished?

Each phrase ends on a cadence, as in Lesson 14. The first, bars 1 to 4, stops on V: re in Tavi's
part over so in Merc's. That's an imperfect cadence: it sounds unfinished and calls for more. [Play
bars 1 to 4] The second, bars 5 to 8, goes from V to I and lands on do: a perfect cadence, which
sounds final. [Play bars 5 to 8] American books call these a half cadence and an authentic cadence.

## Step 2 of 4: How can a song fake its ending?

The way the song kept going, bars 7 and 8 went from V to vi: an interrupted cadence, also called
deceptive or false. It seems to end, then keeps going; it's considered weak, for its "hanging" feeling. Bars
9 and 10 then end it V to I. [Play bars 7 to 10]

The plagal cadence, IV to I, is often added after an ending, as a tag. Hymns set "Amen" to it so
often that it's also called the Amen cadence. [Play the ending, then the Amen]

> **Common mix-up:** "When a phrase sounds finished, the song is over."
> **Why it seems right:** a perfect cadence does sound final.
> **The evidence:** cadences end phrases all through a song, like punctuation. And an interrupted
> cadence seems to end, then keeps going.
> **The better idea:** a cadence is a full stop. It ends a sentence, not always the story.

**Try:** play bars 7 and 8 each way.
**Notice:** Tavi's part sings ti to do every time; Merc's part changes.

## Step 3 of 4: How is a song put together?

In sections, each written as a capital letter. One plan is A A B A: a section, the same again, a
contrasting one, the bridge, then A once more. It's also called 32-bar song form, since in older
songs that use it each section is eight bars. This song's bridge opens on vi, moves mostly in
quarter notes, and ends on V, unfinished, so A comes back. [Play B, then A] Other songs alternate
verses, whose words change, and a chorus, whose words repeat and hold the title or the hook.

**Try:** tap a letter to hear its section.
**Notice:** each A ends on I; B ends on V.
