## Step 1 of 4: Why raise te?

Natural minor's seventh note, te, sits a tone under do. [Play so, te, do] Raised a semitone, it's
ti, a semitone under do: Lesson 4's leading note. [Play so, ti, do] One open textbook says natural
minor's ending lacks major's sense of closure, which ti rising to do partly creates.

The chord before home, the chord on so, changes too: so, te and re, E, G and B, make a minor chord;
so, ti and re, E, G♯ and B, a major one. [Play the chord on so, then home]

(Held until the cold open's reveal, the paragraph above shows with it, its play included. Until
then its place reads "Choose an answer at the top, then hear both endings, to unlock this
paragraph." FACTS 1a, 36.)

Natural minor with te raised to ti is harmonic minor.

**Try:** play so, te, do, then so, ti, do.
**Notice:** the last step shrinks from a tone to a semitone.

_Toy: Lesson 10's minor staircase on A, in your clef's octave, with Raise te; the chord on so's
three beads beside it; and your staff, which writes each figure as it plays, G♯ with its own sharp.
Raise te and the beads wait for the cold open's choice, under the steps' line "Choose an answer at
the top to unlock the rest of this figure."_

## Step 2 of 4: What does the raised note do to the scale?

[Play A harmonic minor, up and down] Harmonic minor climbs tone, semitone, tone, tone, semitone,
then three semitones, then a semitone. The wide step is le to ti, F to G♯. [Play le to te, then le
to ti] F to G♯ spans two letters, a second, but it's a semitone wider than a major second: an
augmented second, Lesson 23's word. No other step in the scale is wider than a tone, and one open
course calls this one a challenging interval to sing.

**Try:** play le to te, then le to ti.
**Notice:** le to ti is the widest stair: three semitones.

_Toy: the staircase in harmonic minor, its le-to-ti stair outlined, beside your staff, which writes
the scale as it plays, G♯ with its sharp._

## Step 3 of 4: Is there more than one minor scale?

Tavi: "Natural minor, harmonic minor. Which one is the minor key?"

Either way the key is A minor, and its scale has a third form too. Melodic minor raises le to la as
well, going up only: la to ti is a tone, and the wide step is gone. Coming down, both fall back, and
it's natural minor again. [Play A melodic minor, up and down] Going up, it's one note from A major:
me, C, where major has mi, C♯. [Play A major, then melodic minor, going up]

> **Common mix-up:** "There is one minor scale."
> **Why it seems right:** a minor key has one key signature, and it's natural minor's.
> **The evidence:** inside that key, the sixth and seventh change with the music: te often rises to
> ti before home, and le rises too on the way up. One open textbook likens the three forms to
> flavours of ice cream, and says a piece is simply "in minor".
> **The better idea:** one minor key, whose sixth and seventh can move.

**Try:** switch on Raise le, then play the scale up and down.
**Notice:** le and te rise on the way up and fall back on the way down.

_Toy: the staircase in harmonic minor, with Raise le; switched on, it shows melodic minor, le and
te lifting going up and settling coming down, beside your staff, which writes the scale going up,
then coming down as a figure of its own._

## Step 4 of 4: Why do C major and A minor share a key signature?

[Play C major, then A minor] A minor's notes are C major's, started on la. Same notes, same
signature: none. Keys sharing a signature are relative keys; the minor's do sits three semitones
below the major's. A is la in C major and do in A minor: do follows home. Lesson 21's "no sharps or
flats, C major" now names only the major key's home. Lesson 10's C major and C minor share do:
they're parallel. [Play C minor] C minor's signature has three flats: B♭, E♭ and A♭.

Tavi: "Same notes, different home. The signature gives the notes; the house shows where home is."

**Try:** play both scales and watch the house.
**Notice:** the signature stays; only home moves.

_Toy: your staff, with the house on do and a small staff in the other clef beside it, which shows
the same key signature and no notes: none for C major and A minor, and C minor's three flats,
written with their own signs as the scale plays, then moved into the signature on both staves._

## Wrap

- A minor key is one key, and its sixth and seventh can move: natural minor keeps le and te.
- Harmonic minor raises te to ti, which leans up to do and turns the chord on so major. Melodic
  minor raises le too, going up only, and closes the wide step that leaves.
- C major and A minor share a key signature: they're relative keys, the same notes with home moved
  from C to A.
