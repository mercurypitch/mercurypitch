Hold a note against a drone: how can you hear when the two are in tune?

By the end you'll know what to listen for against a drone, why the piano's fifth and third sound different there, and you'll hold do, so and mi over one.

This lesson is about two notes held together, and your voice. Tap to turn the sound on: nothing plays until you press play. We ask for the microphone only if you choose to sing.

Choose an answer to unlock the slide.

Your guess

It slows, then stops. Each throb is a beat, and two close notes beat as many times a second as the difference between their frequencies: 224 against 220 four times, and 220 against 220 not at all. Against a drone, you hear in tune when the beats stop. (Pushed closer, two notes might sound busier, which made “speeds up” a fair guess.)

Four words you'll meet before the first step

Drone, harmonic, pure, equal-tempered

Drone

A note that keeps sounding, unchanged, while other notes sound with it.

Harmonic

One of the frequencies inside a note, at 1, 2, 3 or more times the note's own.

Pure

Tuned to an exact ratio of small whole numbers: 3 to 2 for a fifth, 5 to 4 for a major third.

Choose an answer at the top to hear this.

Equal-tempered

Tuned with every semitone the same size, 100 cents: the piano's tuning here, and the app's.

What is that throbbing?

Hear the slide at the top to unlock this step.

Two close notes beat: their sound swells and fades as their waves fall in and out of step, a throb in loudness, not Lesson 8's beat that you tap. The closer the notes, the slower the beats: 221 against 220 beats once a second, 220.5 once every two seconds.

It works an octave apart too: 441 Hz beats once a second against the drone's second harmonic, 440 Hz.

TryDrag the top note until the throb stops, then do it again on the octave.

NoticeThe slower it throbs, the closer you are. In tune, it's still.

2Step 2 of 4

Do two different notes throb too?

Predict

Merc plays the piano's fifth above the drone, then its major third. Which throbs faster?

Choose an answer at the top first.

Your guess

The major third, much faster. (The fifth is the wider interval, which made it a fair guess.)

Harmonics beat too (Lesson 13). In a pure fifth, 3 to 2, the top note's second harmonic meets the drone's third at 660 Hz: nothing beats. The piano's fifth is a hair narrow: they sit 0.75 Hz apart and beat slowly. Its third, 0.8 % wider than pure, beats almost nine times a second, near 1,100 Hz.

TryPlay so and mi both ways, then all three, pure.

NoticeTuned pure, do, mi and so, Lesson 10's major chord, ring still.

3Step 3 of 4

Which should I sing: the piano's note, or the pure one?

Hear Step 2's two intervals to unlock this step.

TaviShould I sing the piano's major third, or the pure one?

Either lands in Sing it's band, 35 cents either side of the piano's note. A pure major third is 386 cents, 14 below the piano's 400; a pure fifth is 2 above the piano's 700. One a cappella quintet's major thirds averaged 392 cents, between the two, with examples of both.

TryDrag mi from the piano's tick down to the pure one.

NoticeBoth sit in the band, but only the pure one is still.

4Step 4 of 4

To hold my part, should I shut out the other one?

Common mix-up

“To hold your part, shut out the other voice.”

Why it seems right: in one study, singers were more accurate alone than with a partner, and more accurate singing the same part than a different one.

The evidence: the same singers, hearing each other, adjusted “to mitigate a partner's error and preserve harmonic intervals”. Together, the harmony between them was closer than when each sang alone.

The better idea: listen to the other part. The beats between you tell you how close you are.

A singer's wide wave swings the top note's harmonics past the drone's: the even throb weakens under a flutter. Against a drone, a straight note lets you hear the beats best. That's a way to listen, not a rule about singing: in Sing it, if a wave comes, that's fine.

TryPlay the piano's fifth with a wave, then straight.

NoticeStraight, the throb is even. With the wave, it's much fainter and harder to follow.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • Two close notes beat as many times a second as their difference, and stop when they're in tune.
  • Above a drone, the piano's fifth beats slowly and its major third fast; pure, both are still.
  • To hold a part, listen to the other one: the beats between you tell you how close you are.
