Your guess

Merc's part. Tavi sang the same notes both ways, ti to do. To end the song, Merc's part leapt up from D to G, and both parts landed on do: V to I, a perfect cadence. To keep going, it stepped up to E, the root of vi: V to vi, an interrupted cadence. To most listeners an ending on do sounds finished, as in Lesson 4: a song that went on seems to have missed do, which made “Tavi's part” a fair guess.

Three words you'll meet before the first step

Cadence, section, form

Cadence

The end of a phrase, where the tune or its chords come to rest, fully or partly.

Section

A stretch of a song with one job, such as a verse, a chorus or a bridge.

Form

The order of a song's sections, written as letters, such as A A B A.

What makes a phrase sound finished?

Each phrase ends on a cadence, as in Lesson 14. The first, bars 1 to 4, stops on V: re in Tavi's part over so in Merc's. That's an imperfect cadence: it sounds unfinished and calls for more. The second, bars 5 to 8, goes from V to I and lands on do: a perfect cadence, which sounds final. American books call these a half cadence and an authentic cadence.

2Step 2 of 4

How can a song fake its ending?

Hear both ways at the top to unlock this step.

The way the song kept going, bars 7 and 8 went from V to vi: an interrupted cadence, also called deceptive or false. It seems to end, then keeps going; it's considered weak, for its “hanging” feeling. Bars 9 and 10 start A again.

The plagal cadence, IV to I, is often added after an ending, as a tag. Hymns set “Amen” to it so often that it's also called the Amen cadence.

Common mix-up

“When a phrase sounds finished, the song is over.”

Why it seems right: a perfect cadence does sound final.

The evidence: cadences end phrases all through a song, like punctuation. And an interrupted cadence seems to end, then keeps going.

The better idea: a perfect cadence is a full stop. It ends a sentence, not always the story.

TryPlay bars 7 and 8 each way.

NoticeTavi's part sings ti to do every time; Merc's part changes.

3Step 3 of 4

How is a song put together?

In sections, each written as a capital letter. One plan is A A B A: a section, the same again, a contrasting one, the bridge, then A once more. It's also called 32-bar song form, since in older songs that use it each section is eight bars. This song's bridge opens on vi, moves mostly in quarter notes, and ends on V, unfinished. Then A comes back. Other songs alternate verses, whose words change, and a chorus, whose words repeat and hold the title or the hook.

NoticeEach A ends on I; B ends on V.

How do I read my part?

Each part has a staff of its own: Tavi's in the treble clef, Merc's in the bass clef, as in Lesson 15. The sharp after each clef is G major's key signature, as in Lesson 21: every F is F♯ sharp. Do is G. Count 1, 2, 3, 4: a half note lasts two beats, a whole note four. Breathe at the breath marks: at this slow tempo, each phrase takes a quick breath halfway.

    Three lines to keep

    • A cadence ends a phrase: perfect, V to I, sounds final; imperfect, on V, unfinished; interrupted seems to end, then keeps going; plagal, IV to I, is the Amen.
    • Sections, written as letters, make a song's form: A A B A.
    • Your part is a tune of its own, read in your own clef.
