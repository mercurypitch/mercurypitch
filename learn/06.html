Four words you'll meet before the first step

Interval, step, leap, transpose

Interval

The distance between two notes, which this lesson counts in semitones.

Step

A move to the next letter up or down: in a major scale, a semitone or a tone.

Leap

A move that skips at least one letter, like D up to G.

Transpose

To move a whole tune up or down, every note by the same interval.

What shape is a tune?

Here's the tune's first line on a chart like MercuryPitch's: each note at its height, one row per semitone. Left to right: two notes the same, a big jump up, two the same, a small step up, two the same, a step down. The tune's contour is which way it goes from each note to the next: up, down or the same.

TryPlay the line, then tap any note to hear it.

NoticeThe ups, downs and repeats make a shape you could draw with a finger.

2Step 2 of 5

How far apart are the notes?

Contour says which way. An interval says how far, in semitones. From the first “twin-kle” to the second, the tune leaps up 7 semitones, C to G. On “lit-tle star” it steps down 2, a tone. In the second line, “I won-der” steps down just 1, a semitone. A step goes to the next letter, so in this tune it's always a semitone or a tone. Anything bigger is a leap. The first two lines hold one leap and six steps.

TryTap a gap between two notes to hear it. Then play ten rounds of Step or leap?

NoticeA leap skips notes the tune could have stepped through: C up to G passes D, E and F.

What happens if I slide the whole tune?

TaviIf I slide the line up, won't the gaps stretch?

Lift the line off the rows and slide it to a new start. Every note moves by the same number of semitones, so every interval stays the same. That's transposing, and it's why “Twinkle” was still “Twinkle” from F♯ sharp. The do-re-mi names stay too: from any start, the first line is do do so so la la so. Only the letters change, as do moved with home in Lesson 4.

TrySlide the line to three starts and play each one, then switch the names to do-re-mi.

NoticeThe gap numbers and the do-re-mi never change. Only the letters under the notes do.

Common mix-up

“A tune is a list of notes, so changing the notes changes the tune.”

Why it seems right: on a piano, a tune is a row of particular keys to press.

The evidence: change every note but keep the intervals, and it's still “Twinkle”. Keep five notes but swap two intervals, and it isn't. In one study, people heard familiar folk tunes that kept their rhythm and their ups and downs. Versions with new sizes were recognised less often than ones that kept the sizes too.

The better idea: a tune is a pattern of intervals, so it can start on any note.

TryPress Swap sizes, play the line, then swap back.

NoticeThe contour, the up-and-down shape, survives the swap. The tune doesn't.

Do the distances have names?

Musicians name an interval by counting letters, both ends included: C up to G runs C, D, E, F, G, five letters, so it's a fifth. The number of semitones gives the rest of the name: a fifth of seven semitones is a perfect fifth. To name an interval, think of a tune you know: hear a leap, and ask which tune starts that way.

TryPlay each tune, then its interval on its own. Then play eight rounds of Name it.

Notice“Should auld” and “twin-kle, twin-kle” both leap up, and the fifth is a tone wider.

Go deeper
  • Relative pitch. Hearing notes by their distances from each other is called relative pitch. Most adults remember a tune mainly by those distances, not by its exact notes, and know it sung high or low (Plantinga and Trainor, 2005). Absolute pitch is something else: naming or singing a note with no other note to go by (Takeuchi and Hulse, 1993).

Three lines to keep

  • A tune is a pattern of intervals, the distances from note to note. Move every note by the same interval and it's the same tune: that's transposing.
  • A step goes to the next letter, a semitone or a tone; anything bigger is a leap. The first two lines of “Twinkle” hold six steps and one leap.
  • You can name an interval by a tune you know: a perfect fifth opens “Twinkle, Twinkle”, and a perfect fourth opens “Auld Lang Syne”.
