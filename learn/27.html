Your guess

F. Stacked in C major, as Lesson 12 built its chords, the next third above D is F, the key's fa: G, B, D and F is V's seventh chord, the dominant seventh, written V7. Three words you'll meet before the first step

Seventh chord, major seventh, minor seventh

Seventh chord

A triad with one more third stacked on top. Its four notes are its root, third, fifth and seventh.

Major seventh

Seven letters, both ends counted, and 11 semitones, a semitone short of the octave: C up to B.

Minor seventh

Seven letters too, and 10 semitones, a semitone narrower: C up to B♭ flat.

How do you build a seventh chord?

Stacking thirds as Lesson 12 did, add one more to a triad and you have a seventh chord, named by its triad and its seventh. On do, C, E and G take a B, a major seventh above C. A major triad with a major seventh is a major seventh chord.

Does an extra note just make a chord richer?

Choose an answer at the top, then hear both chords, to unlock this step.

Both are a major triad with one more note. Now hear where one of them goes.

Common mix-up

“Adding a note just makes a chord richer.”

Why it seems right: one open textbook, pairing each seventh chord with a mood as a way to learn its sound, suggests hearing the major seventh chord as “happy and jazzy”.

The evidence: the same book suggests hearing the dominant seventh as “unresolved”, “like they strongly need to move to another chord”. It says the added note adds dissonance, which strengthens the pull of V towards I.

The better idea: what a seventh does depends on the chord it joins. On V, it pulls home.

TryPlay each seventh chord, then the dominant seventh going home.

NoticeOn the ring, the added corner sits one bead from a corner of I.

3Step 3 of 4

Why does the dominant seventh pull home?

Choose an answer at the top, then hear both chords, to unlock this step.

Lesson 23's last step ended on a pair: B and F, ti and fa, a tritone apart, which counts as restless. V holds ti, and its added note, fa, makes the pair. In V7 to I, ti tends to rise a semitone to do, and a seventh tends to fall a step, here fa to mi: the tritone closes to a major third. With F♯ sharp instead, B and F♯ sharp are seven semitones apart, a perfect fifth, which counts as settled.

TryPlay B and F, then V7 going home.

NoticeB and F sit straight across the ring, then each moves one bead, to C and E.

Go deeper
  • No fifth. Sung in four parts, with every note of V7 moving the way it tends to, the I it lands on has, in one open textbook's words, “three roots and one third—no fifth”. The book calls that normal.

4Step 4 of 4

Which note names a chord?

Predict

Merc plays C, E and G, then moves C up an octave: E, G and C. What is the chord now?

Choose an answer at the top first.

Your guess

Still C major, its root still C; E minor is E, G and B. (Lesson 23's intervals changed names upside down, and Lessons 10 and 12 called the root the bottom note, which made E minor a fair guess.)

That holds in root position. The bottom note is the bass: with C major's third in the bass, it's in first inversion, written C/E; with its fifth, second. A seventh chord's third inversion has its seventh in the bass.

TryTurn V7 over three times.

NoticeThe bass changes; the room stays.

Go deeper
  • Figures. Older music writes an inversion as numbers with the bass note, the intervals above it: 6 for a first inversion, 6 over 4 for a second. One open textbook says musicians shortened the figures “to save time and supplies (paper and ink were very expensive before the industrial revolution)”.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • Stack one more third on a triad and you have a seventh chord, named by its triad and its seventh. On do it's a major seventh chord.
  • On so the added note is fa: the dominant seventh, V7. Its ti and fa are Lesson 23's tritone, and in V7 to I they tend to close inwards to do and mi.
  • A chord is named by its root, whatever is in the bass: root position, then first, second and, for a seventh chord, third inversion.
