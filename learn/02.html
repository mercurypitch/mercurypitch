Predict before you listen

Seven letters leave seven gaps on the way round. Does every gap hold one more note?

Choose an answer to unlock the play button.

Your guessTwelve steps, not fourteen. If every gap held a note, seven letters and seven more notes would make fourteen. There are twelve, so only five of the gaps hold one, and two hold none. Which two, and why? That's this lesson's story. (The letters run in order like the alphabet, so one note per gap was a fair guess.)

1Step 1 of 6

What's the very next note after A?

Start on A and step up to the very next note. You land on the bead between A and B. That step is a semitone, the smallest step a piano has, and the bead is a real note, one of the five with no letter of its own.

TryTap A3, then the bead after it, then B3.

NoticeTo your ear, the bead sits halfway between A and B, so A to B is two of these small steps.

2Step 2 of 6

How many steps until we're back at A?

Keep taking semitone steps and count. From A3 you reach A4, one turn up the spiral, in twelve steps: seven letters, and five notes between them. In hertz each step is a little bigger than the last. To your ear, though, every step is the same size, so each one turns the spiral by the same amount. And twelve of them double the frequency, from 220 Hz to 440 Hz, which is exactly what an octave is.

TryPress Climb and watch the beads light. Then press Look down.

NoticeFrom above, one turn is a ring of twelve beads, evenly spaced like the hours on a clock.

3Step 3 of 6

Isn't B to C the same step as C to D?

Unroll the spiral and every bead lands on a key: seven white keys and five black ones in every octave. Now look at B and C: there's no black key between them.

Predict

Which step is bigger, B to C or C to D, or are they the same?

Choose an answer to unlock the play buttons.

Your guess

C to D is the bigger step, two semitones; B to C is one. E to F is one too.

Common mix-up

“B to C looks just as far as C to D, so it must be the same step.”

Why it seems right: on the keyboard every white key is drawn the same width.

The evidence: B to C is exactly as big as C to the black key above it: half of C to D.

The better idea: B and C are already neighbours. Every neighbouring pair of keys, white or black, is one semitone apart.

TryPress Straighten to draw every key the same width.

NoticeB and C touch, just like C and the black key above it.

4Step 4 of 6

So where do the other five come from?

Two semitones make a tone, or whole step, like C to D with the black key between them.

Now the opening question has its answer. Five pairs of neighbouring letters (A–B, C–D, D–E, F–G, G–A) are a tone apart, and each of those gaps holds one note. B–C and E–F are only a semitone apart, so there's no gap to fill. Seven letters plus five: twelve notes.

TryPress Lift out the five. Then play ten rounds of Semitone or tone? below.

NoticeA tone always skips over one key; a semitone never skips any.

5Step 5 of 6

Why does one key have two names?

The five need names, and they borrow them from their neighbours. A sharp (♯sharp sign) means one semitone up; a flat (♭flat sign) means one semitone down. So the key just above C is C♯ sharp, and the key just below D is D♭ flat: the same key, two names. Musicians call that enharmonic. Both are right: the music around it decides which one is written. MercuryPitch's pitch line shows the sharp names, typed with a #hash sign, like C#4C sharp 4.

Common mix-up

“The black keys are the sharps and flats; the white keys are the plain notes.”

Why it seems right: every black key does carry a sharp name and a flat name.

Predict

One semitone up from E: a black key or a white key?

Choose an answer to unlock the play button.

Your guessThe evidence: one semitone up from E is F, a white key, so E♯ sharp is F.

The better idea: a sharp or a flat is a direction, not a colour: it can land on any key.

TrySwitch the names to Both. Then tap F and press Flat.

NoticeOne semitone down from F lands on E, a white key too: F♭ flat is another name for E.

6Step 6 of 6

How big is a semitone, exactly?

We measure it in cents. In the tuning MercuryPitch uses, every semitone is a hundred cents wide, so the twelve semitones of an octave make 1,200 cents, and a tone is 200. MercuryPitch's Singing tab measures in cents too. Its Input monitor names the nearest note, and a bar shows how far above or below it you are, up to 50 cents either way. Fifty cents is halfway; past that, the next note is nearer.

TryDrag the marker slowly from A3 up to A♯ sharp 3.

NoticeAt 50 cents the nearest note changes from A3 to A#3A sharp 3, just as the Input monitor does.

Go deeper
  • Why twelve equal steps exactly double the frequency. Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the same number, the twelfth root of 2, about 1.0595. Twelve of those multiply to exactly 2: an octave. So the gap in hertz grows as you go up (A3 to A♯ sharp 3 is 13.08 Hz, A4 to A♯ sharp 4 is 26.16 Hz), while every semitone is still 100 cents. Your ear hears multiplying, not adding (Lesson 1).
  • Where the cent comes from. The cent comes from Alexander Ellis, who used it in 1885 to compare the scales of different musical cultures. One cent multiplies the frequency by about 1.000578.
  • A second meaning. Singers also say a note is “sharp” when it's too high and “flat” when it's too low: the same directions, for a miss of any size.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • Seven letters and five notes in the gaps: twelve notes, twelve equal semitones to the octave.
  • A semitone is one key to the very next, white or black; a tone is two. B–C and E–F are semitones.
  • One key can have two names, like C♯ sharp and D♭ flat. MercuryPitch measures in cents: 100 to a semitone, in the tuning it uses.
