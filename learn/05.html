Four words you'll meet before the first step

Air pressure, diaphragm, onset, support

Air pressure

The push of air from your lungs on your vocal folds: more pressure, a louder note.

Diaphragm

A dome-shaped muscle under your lungs. It tightens to breathe in.

Onset

The very start of a note, the moment the sound begins.

Support

Singers' word for how they manage the breath as they sing. There's no one agreed meaning.

    What singers say and do. Researchers tracked how six opera baritones' chests and bellies moved. Judging from that, the diaphragm refilled their lungs, but the singers' own accounts of their breathing bore little resemblance to it (Watson and Hixon, 1985).

    Belly in, belly out. Some singers keep the belly out, others draw it in (Sundberg, 1992). In one study, most of the trained classical singers drew it in just before singing; that study measured breathing, not the sound (Salomoni and colleagues, 2016).

    Holding back has a name: inspiratory checking. In four professional singers, some tightened the diaphragm briefly near a phrase's start; one kept it working throughout (Leanderson and colleagues, 1987).

    1Step 1 of 5

    Why does pushing move the note?

    How hard your breath pushes on your vocal folds is its pressure. It mainly sets how loud the note is, and it nudges the pitch: push harder and the note gets louder and creeps higher. Loud and high are different things (Lesson 1), but in a voice one push moves both. So singers learn to keep each note's pressure steady, and loud and high apart.

    TryPress Hold the note, slide Push up and down, then keep it still.

    NoticeMore push, and the strip grows and the line climbs out of the band. Keep Push still, and so does the line.

    2Step 2 of 5

    So what does my diaphragm actually do?

    Not the pushing. Your diaphragm mostly works on the breath in: it tightens, and air flows into your lungs. It can't push air out.

    Predict

    After a big breath, you start a quiet note. What must your body do with the air?

    Your guess

    Hold it back, with your breathing muscles, never your throat. Full lungs spring back by themselves, often harder than a quiet note needs. So at first your breathing-in muscles keep working, holding some of that push back. As the air goes, your belly and rib muscles take over the pushing. (If you said push: full lungs already push.)

    TaviSo full lungs push by themselves, and at first I just hold some back.

    Common mix-up

    “Support means pushing the air out hard, or squeezing.”

    Why it seems right: singers often talk about support, and it sounds like effort.

    The evidence: pushing harder makes a note louder and higher (Step 1). Squeezing your throat makes a pressed sound, the sound people make lifting something heavy, and a pressed sound needs more air pressure, not less.

    The better idea: a steady note needs steady air, not more of it: breathe in low, start easily, and let the air flow out unforced.

    TryPress Play the breath, then switch between Quiet and Loud.

    NoticeWhat the note needs stays level, while the lungs' own push falls. So first the body holds back, then it adds. A loud note needs less holding back.

    3Step 3 of 5

    How should I breathe in?

    Start tall but easy. If you're standing, keep your knees unlocked. Let your jaw, tongue and neck go loose. That's where singing teachers usually begin. Then breathe in quietly and let the breath drop low, shoulders still, taking as much as feels easy. If your belly moves out, that's your diaphragm at work. Singers breathe in different ways; this is a calm place to start.

    • Lesson 3's care lines still apply: stop if your voice feels scratchy or hoarse.

    4Step 4 of 5

    How should a note start?

    A voice has three main ways to start a note, its onset. Air first: a puff of breath, like a soft “h”, then the sound. A catch: the vocal folds are pressed shut, and the note starts as they open, a little like a cough. Or together: the folds meet the air just as it starts to flow. For a long, easy note, many singing teachers aim for the third, the easy start. It's often found by beginning with a soft “h”, then keeping it only in your mind.

    TryPlay the three starts. Then sing a quiet “hoo” three times, with a breath between, then “oo”, with the “h” only in your mind.

    NoticeWith the “h”, air leads the sound. Keep the ease, lose the puff, and they start together.

    5Step 5 of 5

    What does a steady note look like?

    Sung notes are rarely perfectly still: they can begin with a short slide, a scoop. And steady doesn't mean dead straight: a small vibrato, a regular wave in the pitch, can sit inside the band. Steady means the line settles into the band and stays, for a comfortable, fixed length: 5, 10 or 15 seconds, never “as long as you can”.

    TryPress Play, then Slow, and watch the first second.

    NoticeThe line scoops up, wavers, then settles into the band with a small, even wave: a steady note.
