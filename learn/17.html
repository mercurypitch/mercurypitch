What is a quarter note a quarter of?

A whole note: an open head with no stem, which in 4/4 fills a bar of four beats. Halve it for two half notes, open heads with stems. Halve again for four quarter notes, their heads filled in; then eight eighth notes, each with a flag, and sixteen sixteenths, with two flags. Flags in one beat join into a beam. In 4/4 the top 4 counts four beats a bar, and the bottom 4 says a quarter note gets the beat. In Britain these are the semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver and semiquaver.

TryPlay the halvings, then choose a beat and press Halve or Join.

NoticeHowever you split it, the bar still lasts four beats.

2Step 2 of 5

So what sets how long a note lasts?

Tavi's guess was half right. At one tempo, every quarter note lasts as long as the next. But a note value is a share of a whole note, and the tempo sets the clock. In Lesson 8, 60 BPM meant one beat a second and 120 meant two. So in 4/4 at 60 BPM, a quarter note lasts 1 second and a half note 2 seconds. At 120 BPM, they last half a second and 1 second. Change the tempo and every note stretches or shrinks together, while the page stays the same.

Common mix-up

“A note value is a length of time.”

Why it seems right: at any one tempo, every quarter note does last the same time.

The evidence: the same written bar lasts half as long at 120 BPM as at 60.

The better idea: a note value is a share of a whole note. The tempo turns the shares into seconds.

TryPlay the bar at 60 BPM, then drag Tempo to 120 and play it again.

NoticeEvery stopwatch reading halves, and the notes never change.

3Step 3 of 5

How do you count the short ones?

Lesson 8 counted the beats: 1 2 3 4. Shorter notes get words between the numbers. Eighth notes split each beat in two, counted “1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and”. Sixteenths split it in four: “1 e and a”, with “a” said “uh”. A note longer than a beat holds its count. Counts you don't say are written in brackets, so a half note on beat 3 counts “3 (4)”. The second bar counts “1, 2 and, 3 e and a, 4”.

TrySwitch on Count, play the bars and say each count as it lights. Play them again: the counts fade.

NoticeThe numbers always land on the clicks. “And”, “e” and “a” fall between them.

Go deeper
  • Takadimi. Some teachers count with syllables instead: “ta” on each beat, “di” halfway through it, and “ta ka di mi” for four sixteenths. The 1996 paper that set it out notes that the syllables are like those of North Indian tabla playing.
  • “1 e and a”. The same paper calls it the common system, one that grew up as music was brought into American state schools.

4Step 4 of 5

What does a silence look like?

Every note value has a rest: a written silence just as long. There's a whole rest, a half rest, and so on down to a sixteenth rest. The beat doesn't stop for a rest, so the count carries on in your head, in brackets as before: quarter, quarter rest, quarter, quarter rest counts “1 (2) 3 (4)”. A half note on beat 1 counts “1 (2)” too. The difference is what you hear on beat 2: the half note keeps sounding, and the rest is silent.

TryTap a beat to turn its note into a rest, and listen.

NoticeThe clicks carry on through every silence.

5Step 5 of 5

How do you write a note longer than its shape?

Three beats has no shape of its own: a half note lasts two, a whole note four. A dot after a note makes it half as long again, so a dotted half lasts three beats and a dotted quarter one and a half. A tie is a curve that joins two notes of the same pitch into one sound, as long as both together. A tie can even cross a bar line. Pitch matters for a tie, so the bars move onto the staff from Lesson 15, with every note on A.

TryPress Dot, then Tie, and listen each time.

NoticeTied notes sound once, for both their lengths.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • Note values are shares of a whole note: halves, quarters, eighths and sixteenths. Each has a rest just as long.
  • The tempo turns the shares into seconds. In 4/4 at 60 BPM a quarter note lasts 1 second; at 120 BPM, half a second.
  • Count the beats with numbers and the halves between them with “and”. A dot makes a note half as long again, and a tie joins two notes into one sound.
