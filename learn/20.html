What share of their tries came out right. How long they practised didn't go with how well they played, and nor did how many tries they made; the share of tries played right did (Duke, Simmons and Cash, 2009). One small study: a link, not proof.

For a run, it suggests a tempo slow enough to land every note, then a little faster. (Practising longer feels as if it should help, which made “how long” a fair guess.)

What's left when fa and ti go?

Merc's run uses five of the major scale's notes. Take Lesson 4's staircase and leave out fa and ti. What's left, do, re, mi, so and la, is the major pentatonic. Fa and ti took both semitones with them, mi to fa and ti to do: no step is a semitone now. But the steps aren't even. They climb 2, 2, 3, 2 and 3 semitones: mi to so and la to do are three each.

TryPress Leave out fa and ti, then slide the staircase to start on D.

NoticeTwo short risers go and two tall ones appear. From D, the five are Merc's: D, E, F♯ sharp, A and B.

Go deeper
  • Not everywhere. In one study of 304 recordings from around the world, pentatonic scales were among eight features expected to be universal that weren't; scales of seven notes or fewer with uneven steps nearly were. Where they occur, their step patterns tend to be alike.

2Step 2 of 4

Is there a minor pentatonic too?

Yes, from the same five notes. Start them on la, A in C major: A, C, D, E and G. Same notes, a new home, and now the steps climb 3, 2, 2, 3 and 2. In this series minor is sung from do (Lesson 10): A becomes do, and the five are do, me, fa, so and te. On C: C, E♭ flat, F, G and B♭ flat, Lesson 10's natural minor scale without re and le.

TrySwitch Major · Minor and play each.

NoticeThe same five notes, but the house moves from C to A, and the first step is now a tall one.

Go deeper
  • Blues. MercuryPitch's blues scale adds one note to the minor pentatonic, between fa and so; its Ear Lab calls it Fi.

3Step 3 of 4

How do I make a run faster without smudging it?

Fast is the goal: a guide for new singing teachers builds agility with fast scales and arpeggios on “oo” and “ah”. But for the pianists, the share of tries played right went with better playing; practice time didn't. In Lesson 9, occasional singers' intervals came out more accurate the slower they sang. Together they suggest a plan, though no study here tested it on singers: a tempo slow enough for every note to land in its band, repeated, then nudged up.

Common mix-up

“Speed comes from practising it fast.”

Why it seems right: you want a fast run, and practising fast looks like the direct way.

The evidence: in one study of 17 pianists, neither practice time nor the number of tries went with how well they played the next day; the share of tries played right did.

The better idea: a tempo where every note lands, then nudge it up.

TryPlay the run at 0.6 seconds a note, then 0.3, then Play it smudged.

NoticeFaster, the beads crowd closer, each in its band; smudged, notes slip out of their bands, the cue to drop back a notch.

How do short notes help?

Notes sung short and separate, with a gap after each, are staccato. Joined smoothly, they're legato. The same guide says staccato is sung much like legato, but without carrying the sound from note to note. It uses staccato exercises to train onsets, the starts of notes (Lesson 5): in a staccato run, each note has its own.

TryPlay the run staccato, then legato.

NoticeEach staccato bead starts on its own, with a gap before the next.

    Three lines to keep

    • The major pentatonic, do, re, mi, so and la, is the major scale without fa and ti: no semitones, but steps of 2, 2, 3, 2 and 3.
    • The minor pentatonic is the same five notes started on la. Sung from do, it's do, me, fa, so and te.
    • For speed, keep your tries right: a tempo where every note lands, then nudge it up.
