Your guess

Their heights. In ‘ee’, the peaks are on the 1st bar and the 10th. In ‘ah’, the first peak moves up to the 3rd bar, and the second drops to the 5th. The bars themselves stay where they are, and so does the line: Merc's note never changed. (The peaks did move along the strip, so ‘their places’ was a fair guess.) A vowel is a shape of your throat and mouth. The shape picks which of those frequencies come out strongest: that's this lesson's story.

Four words you'll meet before the first step

Harmonic, spectrum, resonance, formant

Harmonic

One of the frequencies inside a sung note, at 1, 2, 3 or more times the note's own.

Spectrum

A note's recipe: how loud each of its harmonics is. Here, a row of bars.

Resonance

A frequency that your throat and mouth pass on most strongly. Their shape sets where it falls.

Formant

A peak in the spectrum, where a resonance boosts the harmonics near it.

1Step 1 of 5

What's inside one note?

A sung note isn't one frequency. Your vocal folds vibrate at the note's frequency (Lesson 3). The sound they make holds a whole stack of harmonics, at 1, 2, 3 and more times that frequency. Merc's A3 is 220 Hz, so its harmonics are 220, 440 and 660 Hz, and on up. The pitch you hear comes mostly from their spacing. How loud each one is shapes the timbre: whatever makes two sounds differ when their pitch and loudness are the same.

TryPress Add a harmonic a few times, then All.

NoticeEach new harmonic stands out as it joins, but the spacing stays 220 Hz, so the note stays A3, and so does the line.

2Step 2 of 5

Then what makes ‘ee’ different from ‘ah’?

Your throat and mouth, the vocal tract, sit between your vocal folds and the air. They act as a filter: some frequencies pass through boosted, others come out weaker. Each shape of the tract has its own resonances, the frequencies it passes on most strongly. The harmonics nearest them come out boosted, as peaks called formants. ‘Ee’ and ‘ah’ are two shapes, so their peaks fall on different bars. In English, listeners tell vowels apart mainly by the first two.

TryTurn on Resonances, then play ‘ee’ and ‘ah’.

NoticeEach peak sits under a resonance's curve. The curves move from vowel to vowel. The bars don't.

Go deeper
  • High notes and words. On a high enough note, the note itself can rise past the first resonance. In one study, eight classically trained sopranos, near the top of their range, raised that resonance to match the note they sang. The researchers say this makes the voice louder and more even, at the cost of the words: the vowels crowd together. It helps explain why words sung high by sopranos are hard to make out.

3Step 3 of 5

What moves a peak?

Two moves do most of it. Opening your mouth wider raises the first resonance, so the first peak can move to a higher harmonic. Where your tongue narrows the mouth, near the front for ‘ee’ and further back for ‘oo’, moves the second resonance most. Rounding or spreading your lips changes them too. Your jaw, tongue and lips make the vowel. The note stays with your vocal folds.

TryShape ‘ee’, then ‘ah’, without making a sound, and feel your mouth open. Then play ‘ee’ and ‘oo’.

NoticeFrom ‘ee’ to ‘oo’, the first peak stays on the 1st bar, and the second drops from the 10th to the 5th.

4Step 4 of 5

Are five vowels five different notes?

No. Sung on one note, ‘ee’, ‘eh’, ‘ah’, ‘oh’ and ‘oo’ all have the note's harmonics, at the same frequencies. So on Merc's strip the bars stay put, and Merc's line stays level. Only the bars' heights change. Your vocal folds set the note and your vocal tract shapes the vowel, and you can often move each one almost without the other. In speech, too, you can usually swap one word in a sentence and keep its tune.

Common mix-up

“Different vowels are different notes.”

Why it seems right: vowels really do sound different. And in speech, ‘ee’ and ‘oo’ tend to come out higher in pitch than ‘ah’, in many languages.

The evidence: a singer can hold one note at one loudness while the mouth opens and the tongue moves. That makes most of English's vowels. On one note, every vowel keeps the same harmonics; only their loudness changes.

The better idea: a vowel is a shape, not a note. Keep the note level, and let only the vowel change.

TryPlay the five vowels on one note.

NoticeThe line stays level, while the tallest bars change.

5Step 5 of 5

Does everyone's ‘ah’ have the same peaks?

No, and that's one reason nothing here checks your vowel. A vocal tract is typically 15 to 20 centimetres long, a bit shorter in women and children. In a simple tube, a shorter length resonates higher. One researcher measured the vowels of ten British broadcasters. The five women's average ‘ah’ had its first two peaks at about 910 and 1,320 Hz. The five men's had them at about 650 and 1,160 Hz. Merc's lower voice is modelled on those men, and the higher voice on those women.

TrySwitch between Lower and Higher on ‘ah’.

NoticeThe higher voice's bars are twice as far apart, and its tallest bar is the 2nd, not the 3rd.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • A sung note is a stack of harmonics, and its pitch comes mostly from their spacing.
  • Your throat and mouth boost the harmonics near their resonances. Those peaks, the formants, are mainly how a listener tells one vowel from another.
  • A new vowel moves the peaks, not the note: keep the note level and let your mouth do the rest.
