Four words you'll meet before the first step

Phrase, legato, swell, cadence

Phrase

A musical thought that ends at a point of rest.

Legato

Singing smoothly, with no break in the sound, as the notes and words change.

Swell

One note growing louder, then softer. Classical singers call it messa di voce, Italian for “placing the voice”.

Cadence

The end of a phrase, where the tune or its chords come to rest, fully or partly.

What's a phrase?

A phrase is a musical thought that ends at a point of rest. Phrases vary in length, and musicians don't always agree where one ends. In this lesson, each line of the song's words is one: eight beats, ending on a long note where the words pause. You've met its parts already: its beat (Lesson 8), its notes from the major scale (Lesson 4) and its leap from do up to so (Lessons 6 and 9). This lesson puts them together in one song, then shapes it.

Where do I breathe?

At the end of a phrase, where the words pause. A song's score can mark the place with a breath mark, which looks like a comma and usually sits at a phrase's end. The breath takes its time from the note before it, so the beat doesn't change. A breath is a pause in the sound, so a breath inside a phrase breaks its legato. Merc's take 1 snatched one there, between “little” and “star”.

TryPlay take 1, then take 2, and watch the breath marks.

NoticeTake 1's breath takes its time from “little”, inside the phrase. Take 2's takes it from “star”, at the phrase's end.

Common mix-up

“If every note is in tune, the song is finished.”

Why it seems right: a pitch line shows only the notes, and every note can sit in its band.

The evidence: Merc's two takes put every note in its band and still differ: where the breath falls, and how “star” grows and fades. One guide for new singing teachers lists intonation as one area of technique among many, beside phrasing and legato.

The better idea: sing the notes, then shape the phrase: plan its breaths and its swell.

3Step 3 of 5

How do I make a note grow and fade?

With a swell: start the note soft, let it grow, then let it soften again, all on one breath. Classical singers have prized it for centuries. In Lesson 5, a harder push made a note louder and higher. A swell asks for louder without higher. In one study, nine adults with healthy voices and no professional singing training swelled one note on “ee”. Most kept its pitch almost steady. Here, grow only to about speaking volume.

TryPlay the swell, then Lesson 5's push.

NoticeBoth grow louder, and the strip under each grows with them. Then the push's line climbs out of the band, and the swell's stays in it. Both are drawings, not recordings.

4Step 4 of 5

How does a phrase end?

With a cadence. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on, as in Lesson 12: I on do, IV on fa and V on so. “What you are” lands on do as the chords go from V to I: a perfect cadence. To most listeners, ending on do sounds finished, as in Lesson 4. “World so high” stops on re, over V: an imperfect cadence. It sounds unfinished and calls for more, so the song goes on.

TryPlay “How I wonder what you are”, then “Up above the world so high”, each with its chords.

NoticeAfter “are”, the song could stop. After “high”, it waits for more.

5Step 5 of 5

Why listen back?

You hear yourself differently from everyone else. As you speak, your voice reaches your ears through your skull as well as through the air, so you hear more bass in it than others do. A recording carries only the air's part, which is one reason your recorded voice can sound strange. Listen back for your plan, not the sound of your voice: where did each take breathe, and did “star” grow and fade? Sing it records your takes on this device, and nothing scores them.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • Breathe at a phrase's end, where the words pause. The breath takes its time from the note before, so the beat holds.
  • A swell grows one note louder, then softer, on a level line. Grow only to about speaking volume.
  • Every note in tune is the start. Shape each phrase, then listen back for your plan.
