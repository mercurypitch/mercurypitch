Which single note turns a major chord into a minor one?

  • 17 minutes

  • Sound on

TaviIf one note moves a semitone, can the whole chord change?

MercChoose one, and we'll move it.

Lesson 1's spiral seen from above, so one turn is a ring of twelve notes, every black key named by its flat. Do, mi and so, C 4, E 4 and G 4, are circled alike and joined by a thin triangle. With the keyboard, the arrow keys pick a note and Enter plays it.

Predict before you listen

Do stays home. Which note, moved down a semitone, makes the chord minor?

Choose an answer to unlock the two moves.

Your guess

Mi. Move mi down a semitone and C, E, G becomes C, E♭ flat, G: C minor. Move so down instead and you get C, E, G♭ flat, which is neither major nor minor. Mi is the chord's third, three letters up from do: C, D, E. That one note decides major or minor: that's this lesson's story. (Moving so changed the chord too, which made “so” a fair guess.)

Four words you'll meet before the first step

Third, triad, quality, natural minor

Third

An interval three letters wide, both ends counted: C up to E, or C up to E♭ flat.

Triad

Three notes stacked in thirds, sounding together. The bottom one is the root.

Quality

What kind of triad it is. This lesson's two are major and minor.

Natural minor

The minor scale that minor keys are built on.

1Step 1 of 5

How big is a third?

In Lesson 6 you named an interval by counting letters, both ends included. Three letters make a third: C, D, E. The semitones finish the name: C up to E is 4 semitones, a major third. Lower the top note one semitone, to E♭ flat. It's still three letters, so still a third, but only 3 semitones: a minor third. A minor interval is always a semitone smaller than the major one.

2Step 2 of 5

What if I stack another third on top?

Stack a second third on the first and you have a triad. Its bottom note is the root, the middle one the third and the top one the fifth. On C, stack a major third, then a minor third: C, E, G. The gaps are 4 and then 3 semitones. Root to fifth is 7 semitones, a perfect fifth, the leap in “twin-kle, twin-kle”. That's C major, sung do-mi-so.

TryPress Stack a third, then play the triad note by note, and all together.

NoticeThe two gaps, 4 and 3, add up to the perfect fifth's 7.

3Step 3 of 5

Why does moving mi make it minor?

Slide the middle bead down one semitone: C, E♭ flat, G. The gaps swap to 3, then 4, and the fifth stays perfect: C minor, sung do-me-so (“me” sounds like “may”). Moving so down instead shrinks the fifth to 6 semitones. That's no longer perfect, and the chord is neither major nor minor. Major and minor triads share a perfect fifth and are named after their third. In the Royal Conservatory of Music's 2019 voice syllabus, telling them apart by ear is the first-level chord test.

TrySwitch between Major and Minor, move the chord to a new root with Root higher, then play ten rounds of Major or minor?

NoticeOnly the third moves; the root and fifth stay put.

Go deeper
  • Why do stays home. Move do down a semitone instead and C, E, G becomes B, E, G. Stacked in thirds, that's E, G, B: E minor. It's minor too, but its root is now E, not C. Keeping do home keeps the chord on C.

4Step 4 of 5

Is minor the sad one, then?

TaviMajor sounds happy and minor sounds sad. Isn't that the rule?

In listening tests, many Western listeners do lean that way. One open textbook even suggests “happy” and “sad” as a way to remember the two sounds. But a third isn't the only thing in the music. Play the chord's pattern in minor, slowly, then fast: the same three notes, just quicker.

Common mix-up

“Minor means sad and major means happy, always.”

Why it seems right: in one experiment in Sweden and Finland, researchers changed four composed pieces in several ways. For their 46 listeners, mostly musically trained, switching between major and minor moved happy and sad ratings more than any other change, tempo included.

The evidence: “always” is the trouble. In another study, non-musicians judged plain melodies: tempo counted for more than major or minor. In two remote communities in northwest Pakistan, listeners who said they knew no Western music rated the major and minor versions about equally pleasant. UK listeners rated the major ones higher.

The better idea: a major and a minor chord differ by one note, the third. Tempo, the rest of the music and what you grew up hearing all shape how happy or sad a piece sounds.

TryPlay Minor, slow, then Minor, fast. Then switch to Major and play both again.

NoticeAt either speed the three notes are the same; only the speed changes.

Go deeper
  • Happy and sad at once. Researchers asked listeners to press one button whenever they felt happy and another whenever they felt sad. With mixed cues, such as a fast tempo in a minor key, they spent longer pressing both at once than when the cues agreed (Larsen and Stastny, 2011).
  • Tempo first. In one study, five-year-olds went by the tempo alone; six- to eight-year-olds used major and minor too, as adults did (Dalla Bella and colleagues, 2001).
  • The other way round. With single chords, the results suggest that listeners from the same two communities in Pakistan heard minor as more positive than major, the reverse of UK listeners. The authors suggest it's because each culture's music uses one of the two chords more often (Lahdelma and colleagues, 2021).

Is there a minor scale too?

Predict before you listen

Merc plays the C major scale, then C natural minor. How many of the seven notes change?

Choose an answer to unlock the two scales.

Your guess

Three. Mi, la and ti each drop a semitone, to me, le and te (“may”, “lay”, “tay”). The steps now run tone, semitone, tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone. Do stays home, and do, me and so are the minor chord, inside its scale. (One note changed the chord, so “just one” was a fair guess.)

TrySpread the chord into its scale, play C major, then C minor, and switch the names to do-re-mi.

NoticeDo, re, fa and so never move. Me, le and te each sit one row lower.

Go deeper
  • Do or la? This lesson sings minor from do. MercuryPitch's Ear Lab does too, and writes the syllables Do, Re, Me, Fa, Sol, Le, Te. Some teachers, especially in choirs and with children, sing minor from la instead: la-do-mi.

Three lines to keep

  • A third is three letters wide. A major third is 4 semitones and a minor third 3: one semitone apart.
  • Stack two thirds and you have a triad: major is 4 then 3, do-mi-so; minor is 3 then 4, do-me-so. Only the third moves. Many Western listeners hear major as happier, but tempo, the rest of the music and what you grew up hearing count too.
  • The natural minor scale lowers mi, la and ti to me, le and te; do stays home.
