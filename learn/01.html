A3 to A4

1Step 1 of 6

A note is something shaking

Every note you've ever heard starts with something shaking: a guitar string, a speaker cone, the vocal folds in your throat. Pluck a string and it swings back and forth, pushing on the air. Each push travels outward as a wave, all the way to your ear.

TryPluck the string. Then turn on slow motion.

NoticeSlowed 220 times, the string swings once a second: there and back is one swing.

2Step 2 of 6

Count the swings

Count the swings in one second and you have the note's frequency. We measure it in hertz: swings per second. This string swings 220 times a second, so its note is 220 Hz.

TryDrag the dial from 110 to 880 Hz.

NoticeFaster swings make a higher note, and the bead climbs. Your ear hears speed as height.

3Step 3 of 6

Loud is not high

Common mix-up

“A high note is just a loud note.”

It seems right because we use “high” and “up” for volume too (“turn it up”). But try it: make the swing bigger and the note gets louder, but no higher. Make it faster and the note climbs, however soft it is.

Loud is how far it swings. High is how fast.

TryMove the “how far” slider, then the “how fast” one.

NoticeOnly “how fast” moves the bead. That's why MercuryPitch draws your voice as a line that climbs when you go higher, soft or loud.

4Step 4 of 6

Twice as fast

Back to the puzzle. 440 is exactly twice 220. Lay the two waves on top of each other: every other peak of the fast one lands right on a peak of the slow one, again and again, forever. Two for one: the simplest fit there is. That jump is called an octave.

TryPress Play both, then slide the ratio from 1 to 3.

NoticeAt exactly 2 the waves meet on every slow peak, two for one. At 1.26 (277 Hz, a different note) they drift in and out of step. At 3 they meet again, three for one, but that's a different note too. Of all the fits, two for one is the simplest: that's the octave.

The octave keeps going: 110, 220, 440, 880. Each doubling sounds like the same size of jump, even though the gap in hertz doubles every time. From 110 to 220 is 110 Hz; from 440 to 880 is 440 Hz. Your ear hears multiplying, not adding.

5Step 5 of 6

Pitch is a spiral

So pitch isn't really a straight line. It's more like a spiral: go up an octave and you come back round, right above where you started. Musicians name the notes with seven letters, A B C D E F G, and then the pattern repeats.

TryTurn the spiral, and tap any bead to hear it.

NoticeEvery A sits on the same side, one turn apart. Between the letters there are five more notes without a letter of their own. Twelve in all: that's Lesson 2.

6Step 6 of 6

Names and numbers

The number tells you which turn of the spiral a note is on. It goes up at C, not at A, so B3 is followed by C4. The A that orchestras tune to is A4, 440 Hz, and middle C on a piano is C4. A3 and A4 share a letter because A4 swings exactly twice as fast: they're an octave apart, same spot on the spiral, one turn higher. Unroll the spiral and you get the piano: the same seven letters, octave after octave.

TryUnroll the spiral into a keyboard; tap A3, then A4.

NoticeThey sit seven white keys apart and still sound like the same note.

Cadenza: your solo, and any octave counts

Sing it

When two people sing the same tune, they're often an octave apart, and it still sounds like one tune. So when you match a note, an octave up or down counts.

Three lines to keep

  • A note is something shaking; its frequency is swings per second, in hertz.
  • Faster is higher; bigger is louder.
  • Double the frequency and the same note comes back an octave higher, so pitch is a spiral, and the piano is that spiral unrolled.
