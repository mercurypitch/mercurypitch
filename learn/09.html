How do you land a note you have to leap to?

By the end you'll know how to aim a leap at its landing note, which leaps to try first, and how to tell when you've landed. Then you'll sing a slow four-note ladder, or a smaller one if the top feels high.

Four words you'll meet before the first step

Leap, third, arpeggio, landing zone

Leap

A move that skips at least one letter, like do up to so (Lesson 6).

Third

Three letters, both ends counted. A major third is 4 semitones, a minor third 3.

Arpeggio

A chord's notes sung one at a time, such as do, mi, so and the top do.

Landing zone

The band around the note a leap aims for, 35 cents either side (Lesson 3).

Why is a leap harder than a step?

A step goes to the next letter, right beside the note you're on. A leap skips the notes between. For a higher note, your vocal folds stretch longer and tighter (Lesson 3). A step asks for a little of that, and a leap for a lot, all in one move. And in one study, hundreds of people in the United States and India passed short tunes along by singing them. Over many passes, the tunes drifted towards small intervals.

TryPlay the step, then the leap. Under the chart, watch the folds, slowed 220 times.

NoticeThe step makes the folds about an eighth faster. The leap to so makes them one and a half times as fast.

2Step 2 of 5

Can I aim a leap, then?

Yes: leaps tend to miss the same way. In one study, students with no music training copied leaps of a major third, a fourth and a fifth. Even the accurate singers landed them short, by 50 to 100 cents on average: half a semitone to a semitone. Those who sang off landed further short. Singing a note back means planning it from the note you expect to hear, so hear the landing note first. Then leap, land, listen and adjust: Lesson 3's loop, with a jump in it.

TryPress Hear it first, then Leap.

NoticeThe violet line, an illustration of a typical copy, lands under so's band. While you hear so first, its band lights: aim there.

Common mix-up

“You jump to the top note and hope.”

Why it seems right: a leap is quick, and it skips every note on the way.

The evidence: in one study, copied leaps tended to come out short, not just anywhere. In another, students who said they imagine sounds vividly tended to sing more of their notes in tune.

The better idea: hear the landing note first, aim at its band, then check where you landed and adjust.

3Step 3 of 5

What's the ladder for?

Do, mi and so make a major chord: three notes that can sound at once. Sung one at a time, up to the top do, they make an arpeggio: a ladder. Do to mi is a major third, mi to so a minor third: together, the perfect fifth from do to so. So to the top do is a perfect fourth, and do to do the octave. Exams use it too. Before a singer sings a new tune from the page, the Royal Conservatory of Music's examiners play do, mi and so, then the tune's first note.

TryPress Climb, then Leap to so and Leap to the top do.

NoticeEach leap lands on a rung the climb touched.

4Step 4 of 5

Which leap first?

Small ones, and slowly. The Royal Conservatory's voice exams ask for minor and major thirds first. They add the fifth, then the fourth, then the octave, one level at a time. Teachers of the Kodály method, a Hungarian way of teaching music, found that children struggled to sing semitones in tune and to manage a wide range. The method's revised exercises start with so and mi alone, a minor third. Slow helps too: in one study of “Happy Birthday”, occasional singers' intervals came out more accurate the slower they sang.

TryPlay the five leaps in that order, with Slow on, then off.

NoticeSlowed, each arc takes twice as long, so you can follow it into its band.

5Step 5 of 5

How do I know I landed?

Your landing zone is Lesson 3's band, 35 cents either side of the note. Land, listen and compare: under the band? Slide up into it. An octave up or down still counts, as in Lesson 3. The octave is the ladder's biggest leap, and it can wait. If the top do feels high, sing the smaller ladder, do-mi-so-mi-do. It's the first chord the Royal Conservatory's voice exams ask singers to sing.

TryPress Play to watch a leap land 80 cents short, then adjust.

Notice80 cents short, the nearest note is the one just below so. Inside the band, the readout says “in the band”.

    Three lines to keep

    • A leap skips the notes between, and leaps tend to land short. Hear the landing note first, aim at its band, then adjust.
    • An arpeggio is a ladder. Do-mi-so-do climbs a major third and a minor third to so, then a perfect fourth to the top do. It holds the fifth and the octave too.
    • Small leaps first, and slowly, in your comfortable middle. The octave is optional.
