Your guess

The tune, from a note of her own. With nothing played, Tavi still read the steps, up one, up one, back, back, but from F, a step under the page. With one note first, every note landed where it's written: that's what the note is for. Hearing notes by their distances from each other is relative pitch, and reading runs on it. Absolute pitch, often called perfect pitch, would have let her start on G with nothing played. (She missed every written note, so a jumble was a fair guess.)

1Step 1 of 5

Where is do on the page?

Lesson 4's staircase climbs do, re, mi, fa, so. Each stair lands on the next line or space, even the short stair from mi to fa. In G, do is G, marked by the house: in the treble clef, on the line the clef curls round; in the bass clef, in the top space. Each line or space up is the next syllable. In the bass clef, re is the top line, then mi, fa and so climb above the staff, fa on middle C's ledger line.

TryTap the house, then any note, to hear do and that note.

NoticeCount up from the house to name any note's syllable.

2Step 2 of 5

Do I need perfect pitch for this?

TaviSo what did that one note give me?

The written notes. From that note, each next note comes from the one before: relative pitch. Absolute pitch is something else: naming or singing a note with no other note to go by. A 1993 review found the evidence points to it being learned early, up to about age 6 (Takeuchi and Hulse). To land on the written notes, a reader needs one note to start from, not a stored one. As Lesson 9 said, the Royal Conservatory's examiners give it: do, mi and so, then the first note.

Common mix-up

“You need perfect pitch to sing from the page.”

Why it seems right: a good reader seems to pull each note out of the air.

The evidence: Tavi has no absolute pitch, yet with one note first she sang every written note. The Royal Conservatory's exams give that note first.

The better idea: hear do, then count the steps from it.

TryPlay do, hear re inside, then tap re to check.

NoticeOne note is enough to find the next.

3Step 3 of 5

How do I hear the next note before I sing it?

Hear it inside. With Hear it inside on, each note lights a beat before it sounds: in that beat, imagine it. Edwin Gordon, who researched how people learn music, called this audiation. And in Lesson 9's study, students who said they imagine sounds vividly tended to sing more of their notes in tune. That's a link, not proof that practising helps.

TrySwitch on Hear it inside, play do to so, and imagine each note in its silent beat.

NoticeEach note sounds after its beat, so you can check what you imagined.

4Step 4 of 5

What about a leap?

Do, mi and so, the tonic triad, skip a letter each time. In the treble clef they sit on three lines in a row; in the bass clef, in three spaces in a row, mi and so above the staff. Line to line, or space to space, is a leap of a third: do to mi is 4 semitones, mi to so 3, Lesson 9's ladder. Do to so, two lines or two spaces up, is Lesson 9's leap, a fifth.

TryPlay do, hear mi inside, then tap it.

NoticeDo, mi and so are all lines or all spaces, in either clef.

Can I read it without the names?

The names help at first, then they can go. In Sing it, your first phrase shows them, and your second shows only the house. Do is still there to count from. Turn Names off and the notes stay where they are: only the help goes.

Your turn: two phrases from the page. Merc sings do, then do, mi, so, mi, do, in G, or Tavi an octave higher: the tonic triad and the first note, as an exam gives them. Then read and sing at your own pace: first the tune Tavi sang at the top of the page, with the names, then a line you've never heard, with the names off. Before each note, hear it inside. The next note lights once you've held near it for about half a second. Sing on the syllables or on “ooh”. Sing at about speaking volume. For the lower voice, mi and so sit above the bass staff. If so feels high, stop after the first phrase.

Stop immediately if anything feels uncomfortable.

What this does: it measures how far each of your notes sits from the written note, in cents, in any octave, and shows your pitch line after each phrase, not while you sing. It doesn't diagnose anything, and it isn't a test of whether you can sing. Your voice is analysed on this device: nothing is recorded, kept or sent.

After each phrase, your pitch line appears, with a line for each note, such as:

  • “Re · In the band: 20 cents below the written note.”
  • “Mi · About a semitone below, nearer a different note.”
  • “Do · We didn't hear a steady note there.”
  • Added when it applies: “You sang it an octave lower: same note.”

No microphone? Play the start, read and sing anyway, then play Merc's phrases to check. Nothing listens.

    Three lines to keep

    • To sing a tune on its written notes, you need one note to start from, not absolute pitch: hear do, then count the steps from it.
    • The house marks do. Each line or space up is the next syllable, and do, mi and so sit on three lines, or three spaces, in a row.
    • Hear each note inside before you sing it.
