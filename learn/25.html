Your guess

It turns major. G is the chord's third, three letters up from E. E up to G is 3 semitones, a minor third; E up to G♯ sharp is 4, a major third, and the chord becomes E major (Lesson 10). The key is still A minor, and its home chord stays A minor: of the chords, only the one before home changed. And G♯ sharp is ti, a semitone under do, leaning up to it as Lesson 4's leading note does. That's this lesson's story. (The key stays minor, which made “it stays minor” a fair guess.)

1Step 1 of 4

Why raise te?

Natural minor's seventh note, te, sits a tone under do. Raised a semitone, it's ti, a semitone under do: Lesson 4's leading note. One open textbook says natural minor's ending lacks major's sense of closure, which ti rising to do partly creates.

Choose an answer at the top, then hear both endings, to unlock this paragraph.

The chord before home, the chord on so, changes too: so, te and re, E, G and B, make a minor chord; so, ti and re, E, G♯ sharp and B, a major one.

Natural minor with te raised to ti is harmonic minor.

TryPlay so, te, do, then so, ti, do.

NoticeThe last step shrinks from a tone to a semitone.

2Step 2 of 4

What does the raised note do to the scale?

Harmonic minor climbs tone, semitone, tone, tone, semitone, then three semitones, then a semitone. The wide step is le to ti, F to G♯ sharp. F to G♯ sharp spans two letters, a second, but it's a semitone wider than a major second: an augmented second, Lesson 23's word. No other step in the scale is wider than a tone, and one open course calls this one a challenging interval to sing.

TryPlay le to te, then le to ti.

NoticeLe to ti is the widest stair: three semitones.

3Step 3 of 4

Is there more than one minor scale?

TaviNatural minor, harmonic minor. Which one is the minor key?

Either way the key is A minor, and its scale has a third form too. Melodic minor raises le to la as well, going up only: la to ti is a tone, and the wide step is gone. Coming down, both fall back, and it's natural minor again. Going up, it's one note from A major: me, C, where major has mi, C♯ sharp.

Common mix-up

“There is one minor scale.”

Why it seems right: a minor key has one key signature, and it's natural minor's.

The evidence: inside that key, the sixth and seventh change with the music: te often rises to ti before home, and le rises too on the way up. One open textbook likens the three forms to flavours of ice cream, and says a piece is simply “in minor”.

The better idea: one minor key, whose sixth and seventh can move.

TrySwitch on Raise le, then play the scale up and down.

NoticeLe and te rise on the way up and fall back on the way down.

4Step 4 of 4

Why do C major and A minor share a key signature?

A minor's notes are C major's, started on la. Same notes, same signature: none. Keys sharing a signature are relative keys; the minor's do sits three semitones below the major's. A is la in C major and do in A minor: do follows home. Lesson 21's “no sharps or flats, C major” now names only the major key's home. Lesson 10's C major and C minor share do: they're parallel. C minor's signature has three flats: B♭ flat, E♭ flat and A♭ flat.

TaviSame notes, different home. The signature gives the notes; the house shows where home is.

TryPlay both scales and watch the house.

NoticeThe signature stays; only home moves.

Go deeper
  • Do or la? Lesson 10 named another way: some teachers sing minor from la. A minor then starts on la, and its raised seventh, G♯ sharp, is sung si. This series keeps do on home, where G♯ sharp is ti.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • A minor key is one key, and its sixth and seventh can move: natural minor keeps le and te.
  • Harmonic minor raises te to ti, which leans up to do and turns the chord on so major. Melodic minor raises le too, going up only, and the wide step is gone.
  • C major and A minor share a key signature: they're relative keys, the same notes with home moved from C to A.
