Your guess

The middle. In listening tests, people matched a held note with an even wave like this one to a straight note near the middle of its swing. The line swings past both edges of the band: a wave this wide doesn't fit inside it, but its middle does, and that's the note you hear. (Its peaks rose above the band, so the top was a fair guess.)

Four words you'll meet before the first step

Vibrato, rate, extent, straight note

Vibrato

A regular wave in a held note's pitch, up and down, several times a second.

Rate

How many swings a vibrato makes a second.

Extent

How far each swing reaches, up and down, in cents. It's written with ±plus or minus: ±30plus or minus 30 means 30 up and 30 down.

Straight note

A held note with no wave.

What exactly is waving?

A straight note draws a level line. Give the same note a vibrato and the line itself waves, up and down by the same distance each time. That's the pitch moving, and pitch is height. A swell, Lesson 14's, changes the loudness instead: the strip under the line grows, and the line stays level.

TryPlay the straight note, then the one with a wave, then the swell.

NoticeThe wave moves the line; the swell moves only the strip.

2Step 2 of 4

How fast, and how far?

A vibrato has two measures. Its rate is how many swings it makes a second. Its extent is how far each swing reaches from the middle, up and down, in cents. Trained singers have been measured at about 5 to 8 swings a second, usually under ±100plus or minus 100 cents, a semitone either way; averages sit near six. Hertz counts swings a second, as in Lesson 1. Merc's “ooh”, heard since Lesson 1, carries a small vibrato: 5.5 a second, ±14plus or minus 14 cents, inside Lesson 5's band.

TrySet Rate to 6 and Extent to ±30plus or minus 30, then double Extent.

NoticeThe count stays at 6 as the swing widens: rate and extent are separate.

If it swings, why do we hear one note?

Predict

Merc slows the same wave to two swings a second. What will you hear?

Choose an answer at the top first.

Your guess

A note that moves. Slow enough, and your ear follows the pitch up and down. At singers' usual speeds it hears one note, at the middle. (At six a second you heard one note, so “still one note” was a fair guess.)

TaviSo a wave that crosses the band isn't out of tune?

Common mix-up

“A wobbling note is out of tune.”

Why it seems right: the line leaves the band swing after swing.

The evidence: people match an even wave to a straight note near its middle, and in one experiment they judged the pitch of vibrato notes as well as straight ones.

The better idea: Lesson 5 said a small vibrato can sit inside the band. One wider than the band's 35 cents swings past its edges, so on this page the band holds the middle of the line. That middle is the note: if it drifts, the note drifts.

TrySlide Rate from 7 down to 2, then switch on Drift.

NoticeAs the wave slows, the note starts to move. With Drift, the middle leaves the band.

Go deeper
  • Which middle? One study found the note heard close to the geometric mean of the wave's top and bottom frequencies: for Merc's wave, 220 Hz, A3 exactly. The plain average is 0.7 cents higher, almost the same.

4Step 4 of 4

Does a singer need one?

No. Choir experts' views range from a free vibrato, like a soloist's, to a minimal one or none, and singers can change theirs. This lesson asks for none and scores none: a straight note is a finished answer.

Voice Mirror's hold reports a wave of 3.5 to 8.5 swings a second as, say, “Vibrato 5.6 Hz, ±38plus or minus 38 cents. That's a feature, not scored against you.” The app's wavy ribbon for a vibrato note is a mark: its swings follow how wide the note is drawn, not a singer's rate.

TryPlay the sung wave, then stretch the note.

NoticeThe ribbon gains swings as it widens; the sung line keeps its 11 swings.

Coda: the closing bars

Three lines to keep

  • A vibrato is a regular wave in a held note's pitch, measured by its rate, in swings a second, and its extent, in cents.
  • Quick enough, and we hear one note, at the middle of the swing. Slow it right down, and the ear follows it.
  • A straight note and a vibrato are both used in singing, and both are finished notes.
