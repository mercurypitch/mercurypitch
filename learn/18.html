1Step 1 of 4

Do I have to name every note?

At first, yes: in Lesson 16 you counted up from the house to each note, and the page waited for you. A beat doesn't wait. Each name takes a beat, so do comes one beat late, re two, mi three, and the two bars end before do, mi and so. Read a run of steps, then do-mi-so, each as one thing, and the tune keeps up with the beat.

Common mix-up

“Sight-singing means working out each note's name, then singing it.”

Why it seems right: that's how Lesson 16 began, and it works while the page waits for you.

The evidence: in time, the beat runs on without you. Some teachers train patterns: in one study, 67 beginning high-school singers worked on 15 pitch skills from scales, cadences and chords for 12 weeks, and their sight-singing improved. Every group was taught the skills, so it shows a gain, not a winner.

The better idea: learn the patterns, read each one whole, and keep the beat going.

2Step 2 of 4

What should my eye look for?

Three patterns to start. A run of steps: notes on neighbouring lines and spaces, as in Lesson 16. A repeated note: the same line or space again. And do-mi-so: three lines in a row, or three spaces, going up or down. As the cursor reaches each pattern, its bracket lights.

TryPlay both tunes, then tap a bracket to hear its pattern on its own.

NoticeYou can see each pattern's shape before you hear it.

3Step 3 of 4

How do I keep the beat going?

Count in first. Then keep going: the beat doesn't stop, so if a note slips, let it go and come in on the next one, on its beat. Each half note here holds two beats: count “1 (2) 3 (4)”, the bracketed beats in your head, as in Lesson 17.

TryPlay the tune with its count-in, and tap the beat on the pad as it goes.

NoticeThe cursor never waits, and each half note lasts two of your taps.

4Step 4 of 4

What do the other marks ask for?

Other marks say how to sing. p means quiet and f loud. A hairpin that opens means getting louder; one that closes, quieter. A staccato dot asks for a note separated, with space after it. A slur, a curve over different notes, asks for them smooth and joined; a tie, Lesson 17's curve, joins two of the same note. A breath mark, like a comma above the staff, shows where to breathe, at a phrase's end; here each bar is a short phrase. The breath takes its time from the note before, so the beat doesn't stop. Louder never means higher.

TryTap each mark to hear what it asks for.

NoticeEach note keeps its line or space.

Your turn: a tune you've never heard, in time. First, read it through and hear it inside. Then Merc sings do, in G, or Tavi an octave higher, and the click counts in one bar at 60 BPM. Sing the two bars in time, on the syllables or on “ooh”. This time the cursor won't wait for you: it moves on the beat, and nothing plays while you sing. Breathe at the breath mark. If a note slips, come in on the next one. Sing at about speaking volume. For the lower voice, mi and so sit above the bass staff. If so feels high, leave it out and come in again on the next note.

Stop immediately if anything feels uncomfortable.

What this does: it measures how far each of your notes sits from the written note, in cents, in any octave, listening for each note in its own beats, counted from your first note, and shows your pitch line after each tune, not while you sing. It doesn't score your timing. It doesn't diagnose anything, and it isn't a test of whether you can sing. Your voice is analysed on this device: nothing is recorded, kept or sent.

After each tune, your pitch line appears under the staff, with a line for each note, such as:

  • “So · In the band: 20 cents above the written note.”
  • “Re · About a semitone below, nearer a different note.”
  • “Mi · We didn't hear a steady note there.”
  • Added when it applies: “You sang it an octave lower: same note.”
