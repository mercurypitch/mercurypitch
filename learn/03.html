A semitone is the step from one piano key to the very next.

Predict before you see the result

In one test, 39 students who said they couldn't copy a note sang short tunes back. How many sang a semitone or more off?

Choose an answer to see the result.

Saying so didn't predict how they sang. Only four of them sang a semitone or more off. Of 40 others, not picked for saying so, six did.

For most, the ears were fine. Most of those who sang off heard small changes in pitch as well as the accurate singers did. The hard part was turning a heard note into a sung one: the link between ear and voice.

Real tone deafness is rare. Congenital amusia, the hearing condition the term “tone deaf” properly names, affects about 1.5 % of people.

Most people who say they can't sing still land within a semitone, and when a note does come out off, it's usually the link, not the ears. (The 59 % answered a questionnaire. In the tests, 10 of 79 and 7 of 45 sang off. If you chose “most of them”, you went with what “I can't sing” suggests.)

    1Step 1 of 5

    Where does my voice's note come from?

    Lesson 1 said every note starts with something shaking. In your voice, it's your vocal folds. When you breathe, they're apart. When you sing, they come together and air from your lungs sets them vibrating, opening and closing: 220 times a second for the A at 220 Hz. To go higher, muscles in your voice box stretch them longer and tighter, and tighter folds vibrate faster, like a guitar string tuned up.

    TryTurn on slow motion and hold Sing. Then move Stretch to tight and hold Sing again.

    NoticeSlowed 220 times, the folds at A3 open and close once a second; stretched, they speed up. When you sing, don't think about your folds: aim for the note you hear.

    2Step 2 of 5

    Do I need to sing loudly?

    No. Loud and high are different things (Lesson 1): a quiet note is just as much the right note as a loud one, and MercuryPitch's pitch line draws both at the same height. When you sing along here:

    • Sing at about the volume you talk at: no shouting, no whispering.
    • Keep water nearby, and sip it.
    • Rest when your voice feels tired.
    • Stop if it feels scratchy or hoarse.

    3Step 3 of 5

    If my ears are fine, why is my note off?

    Because hearing a note and making it are different jobs. Matching is a loop: hear Merc's note, try it, compare (higher, lower or the same?), adjust, and compare again. In one study, musicians and non-musicians alike matched notes more accurately with a slider than with their voices.

    TryPlay Merc's note, drag the slider until your note sounds the same, then press Compare.

    NoticeCompare shows which way to go and how far, in cents. When you sing, your pitch line on screen shows you that.

    Common mix-up

    “Some people are just tone deaf.”

    Why it seems right: the word “deaf” puts the blame on the ears.

    The evidence: most people who sing off hear pitch changes as well as accurate singers do.

    The better idea: singing in tune is a skill, the link between hearing a note and making it, and it can get better with practice.

    4Step 4 of 5

    Does it count if I'm an octave away?

    From one person to the next, smaller folds vibrate faster. So children's voices sit highest, then most women's, then most men's: two people singing one tune are often an octave apart. The researchers counted octave matches too.

    TryPlay both, then tap the A that feels easier to hum along with. You'll sing with that Merc at the end.

    NoticeEither choice works: the same notes, an octave apart.

    5Step 5 of 5

    How close is close enough?

    Even the accurate singers in the study's second test sat about 30 cents from the target, on average. So “in tune” can't mean one exact line: it's a band around the note. On this page, as in Voice Mirror, the band reaches 35 cents either side, about a third of a semitone. The study's cut-off, a semitone off, is 100 cents: nearly three times as far as the band's edge.

    TryPress Play to watch a line chase the band, then drag the line yourself.

    NoticeInside the band, the readout says “in the band”. Past 50 cents the nearest note changes (Lesson 2).

    Cadenza: your solo, three notes, slowly

    Sing it: three notes

    Your turn: three notes, slowly, with the Merc you chose. Merc sings each one, then you sing it back. Slow helps: in one study, most occasional singers sang a well-known song as accurately as professionals once they slowed down.

    Before you sing: how is your voice today?

    Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along.

    Choose listening if anything hurts or singing has felt uncomfortable, if your voice feels unusually hoarse or tired, if notes you usually reach have suddenly gone, if you're ill, or if talking or singing feels like hard work today.

    Three lines to keep

    • Your voice makes a note with two vocal folds: tighter, they vibrate faster and the note goes up. Sing at about speaking volume; stop if it feels scratchy or hoarse.
    • Matching is a loop: hear, try, compare, adjust. For most people the ears are fine; the link to the voice is the skill.
    • An octave up or down counts, and in tune is a band: here and in Voice Mirror, 35 cents either side.
