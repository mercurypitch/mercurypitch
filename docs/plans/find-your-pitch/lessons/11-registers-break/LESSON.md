## Four words you'll meet

- **Register**: one of your voice's gears, a way your vocal folds vibrate. [Play: one note in each
  gear]
- **Chest voice**: the lower gear, the one most speech uses. [Play: the lower gear]
- **Head voice, or falsetto**: names often given to the upper gear; how they differ depends on who
  you ask. [Play: the upper gear]
- **Flip**: a sudden switch between gears, often with a jump in pitch. Also called a crack or a
  break. [Play: a siren that flips]

## Step 1 of 5: What changes when a voice flips?

Your voice has gears, called registers: different ways your vocal folds vibrate. In chest voice,
the lower gear, the folds are thick and vibrate along their whole length. Most speech uses it. In
head voice or falsetto, the upper gear, the folds are stretched thinner, and less of them vibrates.
A flip is a sudden switch from one gear to the other. Around the middle of a voice, both gears can
sing the same notes, and they usually sound different.

**Try:** play one note in each gear.
**Notice:** the same height on the chart, two sounds: the upper gear is closer to a plain tone.
Merc's gears are a model of what voices tend to do.

_Toy: the range chart, one note in each gear, with Lower gear and Upper gear._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Two more gears.** Below chest voice, many researchers count a lowest gear, vocal fry, which
>   usually makes a voice's lowest notes. Above head voice, some singers reach a whistle register,
>   the gear researchers understand least. Singing mostly uses the two middle gears.

## Step 2 of 5: Why does a slide flip?

For a higher note, your vocal folds stretch longer and tighter (Lesson 3). On a slow slide up, the
stretch grows smoothly. But at some point the layers of each fold stop moving together, and
suddenly less of each fold vibrates. That's the switch, and the pitch jumps with it. Researchers
have seen this even in a larynx studied in a lab, with no throat or mouth above it: a small,
smooth change in stretch was enough to set off the jump.

**Try:** play the slide slowly, and watch the stretch gauge.
**Notice:** the stretch rises smoothly the whole way, but the line jumps at the seam.

> **Common mix-up:** "A crack means you're doing it wrong."
> **Why it seems right:** a flip is sudden, it can happen without your choosing it, and classical
> singers train to hide it.
> **The evidence:** a small, smooth change in stretch can set off the switch, even in a larynx in
> a lab. Every voice has the gears.
> **The better idea:** a flip is your voice changing gear. If it comes, let it, gently.

_Toy: the range chart, Merc's slide slowed, with the stretch gauge, Play, Slow and scrub._

## Step 3 of 5: Why only sometimes?

Around the middle of a voice, either gear can sing the same notes, and you may or may not get to
choose which one sings. So a slide doesn't have to switch at one fixed note, and a short slide may
not switch at all. In one study, 67 choir singers each slid up to their highest note. Listening, and
looking at each slide drawn as a chart, researchers found a break in fewer than a third of the
slides. The app can't tell which gear you're in.

**Try:** play Merc's three model slides.
**Notice:** the first two switch at different notes. The third stays in one gear the whole way,
with no seam.

_Toy: the range chart, three model slides up, with Slide 1, Slide 2 and Slide 3._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Up and down differ.** In one lab study, the jump on the way up came at a higher note than the
>   jump on the way down. The gear a voice is in can depend on the gear it was just in.

## Step 4 of 5: Can a flip get smoother?

Yes: singers can learn to smooth the switch until listeners hear no flip at all. The gears are still
there. A lab instrument that tracks how the folds touch can still pick up the switch, and
registers can't be removed. One guide for new singing teachers suggests slow two-note moves,
shifted up and down through the switch, and sirens.

**Try:** play Merc's five slow two-note slides.
**Notice:** the jump shrinks with each slide, and the seam narrows to a hairline that stays. It's a
model of what practice can do, not a promise.

_Toy: the range chart, five two-note slides across the seam, the signature, with Play the five
slides and a slide picker._

## Step 5 of 5: What do other singers do with a flip?

It depends on the style. Classical singing works to hide the switch. Yodelling, from the Alps to
parts of Africa, does the opposite: it uses the flip as an ornament, or as a whole way of singing.
An international committee of voice experts was set up to agree on what registers are. It agreed:
register effects can be fundamental to some kinds of singing. Whether to hide a flip or show it off
is up to the style.

**Try:** play the figure both ways: flipping on purpose, then hiding the switch.
**Notice:** the same notes each time. Flipped, each leap across the seam changes gear, and you hear
it. Hidden, the sound stays even.

_Toy: the range chart, a short figure written for this page, with Flip on purpose and Hide the
switch._

[ Hard work ] [ Uncomfortable ] in place of [ Easy ] [ Workable ] [ Effortful ] [ Uncomfortable ].
Here the answer decides whether Again is offered, so a word a learner guesses at can misreport how
the singing felt. The labels stay: REQ-GVC-022 names Easy, Workable, Effortful and Uncomfortable,
so they change only if the spec does, for the app's Guided Voice Check and every lesson at once.
The review's answers are a proposal for the spec (FACTS 65).
