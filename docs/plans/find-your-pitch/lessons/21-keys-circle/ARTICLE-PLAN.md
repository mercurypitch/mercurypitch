## Four words you'll meet

- **Key**: the scale a piece uses, named after its do: G major's do is G. [Play: G major]
- **Key signature**: the sharps or flats written once at the start of each staff, instead of before every
  note. [Play: G major, with the F♯ lit]
- **Circle of fifths**: the twelve keys in a ring, each a fifth from its neighbours. [Play: C, G, D, A]
- **Equal temperament**: tuning every semitone the same size, 100 cents. [Play: C4 to C5, the twelve
  semitones]

Facts: R21-5, R21-6, Lesson 2 FACTS 22. Words: about 80.

## Step 1 of 5: Why does G major need F♯?

- Point: build Lesson 4's pattern on G with white keys only: G A B C D E F G. The last two steps come out
  semitone then tone, the wrong way round. Raise F to F♯ and ti sits a semitone below do again. The sharp
  is G major's ti.
- Examples: G major on white keys (wrong ending), then with F♯; the staircase under each.
- Try: tap F, then F♯, as the scale's seventh note. Notice: only one note had to move.
- Toy: keyboard and staircase, with the black key's toggle.
- Facts: R21-1, R21-3. Words: about 100.

## Step 2 of 5: Why does each key a fifth up add one sharp?

- Point: the key a fifth up starts on the last key's so. Its first six notes are notes the last key
  already had; its seventh, ti, is the old key's fa, a tone below the new do, and has to rise. So the
  sharps pile up in order: F♯ (G major), C♯ (D), G♯ (A). The order of sharps is F C G D A E B, each a
  fifth above the last. In a signature, the last sharp is ti: do is a semitone above it.
- Signature: Lesson 2's ring re-threads by fifths, C G D A E B F♯ ...; the house walks round it and each
  step lights one more sharp in the signature beside it (card).
- Examples: C, G, D and A major in turn, each 0.3 s a note, with the signature growing to three sharps on
  the staff (both clefs by the voice choice).
- Try: step the house round the ring, C to A. Notice: each key keeps the last one's sharps.
- Toy: the circle toy (kit, SEASON-2 § 6.2 item 4) beside a staff with the signature.
- Facts: R21-4, R21-5, R21-8. Words: about 130.

## Step 3 of 5: And the flats?

- Tavi, sincere guess (card's misconception): "A♯ and B♭ are the same key. Can't F major use A♯ instead?"
- Point (half right): on a keyboard, and in equal temperament, A♯ and B♭ are one key and one sound. But a
  scale uses each letter once: F major is F G A B♭ C D E. With A♯ it would have two As and no B. Going the
  other way round the circle, a fifth down each time, each key adds a flat on its fa: B♭ (F major), E♭
  (B♭), A♭ (E♭). The order of flats is the sharps' backwards: B E A D G C F. In a signature of two or more
  flats, the second-to-last is do; one flat is F major.
- Common mix-up card: Belief "A♯ and B♭ are the same key, so a key can use either." Why it seems right:
  they are one key on the piano. The evidence: F major with A♯ would spell F G A A♯ C D E: two As, no B.
  The better idea: one letter per step; the key decides the name.
- Examples: F major on white keys (B sounds high), then with B♭; then F, B♭ and E♭ major in turn, the
  signature growing to three flats.
- Try: switch the fourth note between A♯ and B♭ on the staff. Notice: the sound is the same; only B♭
  keeps every letter.
- Toy: circle toy, flat side; staff with Spell as A♯ · B♭.
- Facts: R21-2, R21-3, R21-5, Lesson 2 FACTS 18. Words: about 140 with the card.

## Step 4 of 5: How do I find do from a key signature?

- Point: sharps: the last sharp is ti, so do is the next note up, a semitone above. Flats: the
  second-to-last flat is do; one flat means F. No sharps or flats: C. The house lands on do, and the
  syllables follow.
- Examples, both clefs: signatures of 1, 2 and 3 sharps (G, D, A) and 1, 2 and 3 flats (F, B♭, E♭); for
  each, the page plays do, then do-mi-so.
- Try: Find do: a signature appears; tap where the house goes, then hear it. Six rounds, each signature
  once (a short guided task, playbook rule 17).
- Toy: staff with a key signature and a draggable house.
- Facts: R21-5, R21-9 (this season reads up to three, as ABRSM's Grade 2 does for majors). Words: about
  110.

## Step 5 of 5: Why does the circle close after twelve? (Go deeper if long)

- Point: walk twelve fifths from C and you pass every note once and land on C again, seven octaves up.
  But a pure fifth, the 3:2 of Lesson 13's harmonics, is a hair wider than the keyboard's: twelve of them
  overshoot seven octaves by about a quarter of a semitone, 23.46 cents, the Pythagorean comma. Equal
  temperament narrows each fifth by about 2 cents, 701.96 to 700, so the ring closes. That's why twelve
  notes, equally spaced.
- Signature: the ring's thread pulled taut; twelve pure fifths overshoot, the gap shown; each fifth
  narrowed by 2 cents and the ring closes (card).
- Examples: twelve pure fifths stacked and folded into one octave, then played: the last note lands at
  265.2 Hz against the starting C4's 261.6 Hz, audibly sharp; then the same walk in equal temperament,
  landing on C4.
- Try: play Pure · Equal. Notice: the gap is about a quarter of a semitone after twelve steps, under 2
  cents a step.
- Toy: circle toy with Pure · Equal and the gap drawn.
- Facts: R21-7, R21-10, R21-11, R21-12. Words: about 140.

## Wrap

- A major scale keeps Lesson 4's pattern on any note, with one letter per step.
- Each key a fifth up keeps its sharps and adds one on its ti (F C G D A E B); each key a fifth down adds
  a flat on its fa (B E A D G C F).
- The last sharp is ti; the second-to-last flat is do. And twelve fifths close the circle only because
  equal temperament narrows each by about 2 cents.
