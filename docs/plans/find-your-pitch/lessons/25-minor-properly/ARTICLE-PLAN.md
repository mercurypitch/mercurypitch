## Step 1 of 4: Why raise te?

- Point: in natural minor, te sits a whole tone under do; raised a semitone, it becomes ti, the
  leading note, a semitone under do, and the ending gains the closure of major's ti to do (R3). It
  also changes the chord on so: so-te-re (E, G, B) is minor; so-ti-re (E, G♯, B) is major, and a
  major V pulls to i (R4). That raised te is harmonic minor.
- Examples: E minor chord to A minor; E major chord to A minor; A harmonic minor, up.
- Toy (signature, first state): the staircase; te slides up a semitone to ti; the chord on so
  changes its middle bead.
- Try: play so-te-do, then so-ti-do. Notice: the last step shrinks from a tone to a semitone.
- Facts: R3, R4, R8. Words: 140.

## Step 2 of 4: What does the raised note do to the scale?

- Predict (written out): "With te raised to ti, how far is it now from le up to ti?" [ Three
  semitones ] [ Two semitones ]. Reveal: "Three. F up to G♯ is wider than any other step in the
  scale: an augmented second, a major second made a semitone wider (Lesson 23). (Every other step
  in the scale is one or two semitones, which made 'two' a fair guess.)"
- Point: harmonic minor's gap is awkward to sing (R5). The toy draws it as a wider stair.
- Examples: A harmonic minor up and down; le to ti alone (F4 to G♯4).
- Toy: the staircase with the wide stair outlined.
- Facts: R1, R5, R8. Words: 130.

## Step 3 of 4: Is there more than one minor scale?

- Tavi: "So there's natural minor, and harmonic minor. Which one is a minor key?"
- Common mix-up card (half right):
  - Belief: "There is one minor scale."
  - Why it seems right: a minor key has one key signature, built on natural minor (R2).
  - The evidence: inside that key, the sixth and seventh change by what the music needs: raised te
    for the pull home (harmonic), raised le and te on the way up to smooth the gap (melodic), and
    natural going down (R1, R5). Open Music Theory calls the three forms "flavors of ice cream":
    a piece is simply "in minor" (R2).
  - The better idea: one minor key, with two notes that can move.
- Point: melodic minor raises le too, going up only, and comes down natural (R1, R5). Going up it
  sounds nearly major: A melodic minor differs from A major by one note, C (R RESEARCH finding 3).
- Examples: A melodic minor up (A B C D E F♯ G♯ A), then down (A G F E D C B A).
- Toy: le slides up too, going up only, and slides back on the way down.
- Facts: R1, R2, R5. Words: 200.

## Step 4 of 4: Why do C major and A minor share a key signature?

- Point: A minor's notes, A to A on the white keys, are C major's notes from la. Same notes, same
  signature: no sharps, no flats. Keys that share a signature are relative; A minor's do sits three
  semitones below C major's (R6). In do-based singing the syllables follow home: in C major, A is
  la; in A minor, A is do. The house moves; the signature stays (R7). Keys that share do are
  parallel: Lesson 10's C major and C minor (R6, R8).
- Examples: C major scale, then A natural minor, on one staff with the same (empty) signature; the
  house slides from C to A; then C minor, three flats, the house back on C.
- Toy: the staff with a key signature; the house on do; "C major" and "A minor" share one
  signature line.
- Tavi's re-explanation: "Same notes, different home. So the signature tells you the notes, and the
  house tells you where home is."
- Facts: R6, R7, R11. Words: 190.

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **For instrumentalists.** Open Music Theory says the three named forms are "useful categories
>   primarily for instrumental performers" (R2). Singers meet them as raised notes in a tune.
> - **Do or la?** As Lesson 10 said, some teachers sing minor from la. In la-based minor, A minor
>   keeps C major's syllables and starts on la; this series keeps do as home (R7).
