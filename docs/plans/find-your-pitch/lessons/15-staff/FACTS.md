## Lines, spaces and letters

11. A staff is five evenly spaced lines; a note sits on a line or in a space between two lines; five lines leave four spaces between them, so a staff has nine places from its bottom line to its top line | R15-1, Strong (OMT 2e 1.2: "A staff consists of five horizontal lines, evenly spaced"; "Noteheads in a space should just touch the lines above and below"); arithmetic | carried; the reveal, the four words' Staff, Step 1, the Wrap
12. The note letters run A to G and repeat; each line or space up the staff is the next letter, so lines and spaces take turns and no letter is skipped; the nine places of the treble staff read E, F, G, A, B, C, D, E, F | R15-2, Strong (OMT 2e 1.4: letters "repeating in a loop"; OMT 2e 1.3's treble lines and spaces, which interleave as E F G A B C D E F) | carried; the reveal, Step 1 and its Notice, the Wrap
13. The treble clef curls round the second line from the bottom, which it names G: the G above middle C, G4, 392.00 Hz. ## Unsupported, not used

- "Guido invented the staff": the popular line overstates it. Basel's teaching page says Guido of
  Arezzo "claimed, that he had invented this system", and Haines (2008), which argues against the
  story, was not read (its link led to a login page). The page says nothing about who made the
  staff.
- The first staves had four lines, two of them coloured (Basel § 2.10: "a red one to indicate the F
  and a yellow one to indicate the C"): a Moderate source with no author named, and Haines (2008)
  not read, so the Go deeper the plan offered stays out, as the lead asked.
- That clefs began as letters: the encyclopedia says so ("Before the advent of clefs, the reference
  line of a staff was simply labeled with the name of the note"; "Over time the shapes of these
  letters became stylised"), and no textbook read here gives the history, so the page leaves it
  out: it says only that the treble clef curls round G and the bass clef's dots sit either side of
  F.
- "Vocal tenor clef", OnMusic's name for the octave treble clef: read only in a summary, so the page
  does not use the name (26).
- That the octave treble clef is the commonest way to write tenor parts, or that guitarists and
  bassists read it: a search summary of Open Music Theory's Pressbooks chapter said so; the chapter
  answered 403 and was not read (R15-14). The page says "often", from the encyclopedia.
- 8va, the octave sign: ABRSM lists it among Grade 2's signs (ABRSM 2023 p. 18), but no source read
  here says how it is written or read, so the page does not teach it, and Step 5 says only "with
  ledger lines past their edges" (25).
- The 97-key piano's "richer sound": Yamaha's claim about its own instruments; the page says only
  that some makers build them to special order (28).
- That a beginner's exam sets both clefs (32): true of ABRSM's Grade 1, but the page names no exam
  board.
- Which key the season's sight-singing will use: Lesson 16's to decide; this page's G is for its own
  sing-along only (55).
