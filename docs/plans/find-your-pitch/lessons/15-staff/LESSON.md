## Four words you'll meet

- **Staff**: the five lines music is written on. [Play: E4 up to
  F5]
- **Clef**: the sign at the start of a staff that names one of its lines, so you can count the rest.
  [Play: G4]
- **Ledger line**: a short line that carries the staff on past its top or bottom. [Play: C4]
- **Grand staff**: a treble staff and a bass staff joined, the way piano music is written. [Play: C3,
  C4, C5]

## Step 1 of 5: Which notes does a staff hold?

Listen first. [Play G4] That's the G above middle C, G4, and the treble clef names its line: the clef
curls round the second line from the bottom. Count letters from that line. One step up is the space
above it, A, and one more the middle line, B. One step down is F, in a space, then E, the bottom
line. Lines and spaces take turns, and each step up is the next letter. So the treble staff's lines
read E, G, B, D and F, and its spaces F, A, C and E.

**Try:** tap any line or space to hear its note and see its letter.
**Notice:** line, space, line: no letter is ever skipped.

_Toy: the staff toy, treble clef, its nine notes, with Step up, Step down and See it on the pitch
line._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Two sentences to remember them.** Open Music Theory, a free textbook, gives the treble lines as
>   "Every Good Bird Does Fly" and notes that the spaces spell "face". This lesson counts from the
>   clef's line instead, which works in either clef.

## Step 2 of 5: Is every step up the staff the same size?

Tavi: "Each step up the staff looks the same. Isn't it the same step in sound?"

In letters, yes: each step is one letter. In sound, no. [Play E, F, G] E to F is a semitone and F to
G a tone, and B to C is a semitone too: Lesson 4's two short steps. The staff counts letters, not
semitones. [Play F, then F♯] [Play B, then B♭] To move a note by a semitone, write a sharp or a flat
to its left: F♯ stays in F's space, B♭ on B's line.

> **Common mix-up:** "Every step up the staff is the same step in sound."
> **Why it seems right:** the lines and spaces are evenly spaced.
> **The evidence:** E to F and B to C have no black key between them. On the pitch line they're a
> semitone apart, and every other pair of neighbouring letters a tone.
> **The better idea:** the staff counts letters. A sharp or a flat adjusts the sound.

**Try:** turn on See it on the pitch line, then play E to F and F to G.
**Notice:** one lane, then two.

_Toy: the staff toy beside the pitch line, with Play E, F, G · Play B, C, D · Play F, then F♯ · Play B,
then B♭._

## Step 3 of 5: What happens past the top or bottom line?

Keep climbing. [Play G, A, C above] G sits in the space just above the top line. A needs a short line
of its own, a ledger line, and C, two letters higher, needs two. [Play D, C, A below] Below the
staff it works the same way: D hangs under the bottom line, and middle C sits on one ledger line. A
ledger line is drawn only as far as its note needs. This season reads up to two each way.

**Try:** move the note above the staff, then below it.
**Notice:** ledger lines appear only as far as the note goes.

_Toy: the staff toy with one note to move, by Step up and Step down, a tap on a place, or a mouse or
pen drag, ledger lines drawn up to two each way, with Play G, A, C above and Play D, C, A below._

## Step 4 of 5: Where do the low notes go?

Merc's voice sits low. [Play Merc's F] On the treble staff, that F would need three ledger lines, so
low voices read another clef. The bass clef names the second line from the top: F3, the F below
middle C that Merc sang. Its two dots sit either side of that line. Count from it as before.
[Play G2 up to D4] The bass staff's lines read G, B, D, F and A, and its spaces A, C, E and G. Above
it: B in the space, C on middle C's ledger line, D just over it.

**Try:** switch Treble · Bass and tap the same line in each.
**Notice:** the same line names a different note. The clef decides.

_Toy: the staff toy with the clef switch Treble · Bass, with Play G2 up to D4._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **The octave treble clef.** Tenor parts are often written in the treble clef, though they sound
>   an octave lower. A small 8 under the clef, where it's drawn, says so.
> - **A third clef.** The alto clef sits on the middle line and names it middle C. Viola players read
>   it.

## Step 5 of 5: Where do the two staves meet?

Put the treble staff above the bass staff, joined by a brace: that's the grand staff.
[Play middle C on each staff] Middle C sits on the one ledger line between them. Below the treble or
above the bass, it's one key and one sound. Under the staves, every note has one key. So a piano
writes its 88 keys, A0 to C8, on two staves of five lines, with ledger lines past their edges. Which
clef is yours? This season, lower voices read the bass clef and higher voices the treble.

**Try:** tap any key to see its note light on the grand staff.
**Notice:** a black key takes a letter's line or space, with a sharp.

_Toy: the grand staff over Lesson 1's keyboard, with Play the white keys from G2 to F5._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **From 54 keys to 88.** Bartolomeo Cristofori's first piano had 54 keys, Yamaha says. Makers
>   added more as composers asked, and by the 1890s the piano had settled at 88. Some makers now
>   build 97-key pianos to special order.
