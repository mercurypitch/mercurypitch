## Four words you'll meet

- **Vocal folds**: two folds of tissue in your voice box that vibrate to make your voice. [Play:
  Merc's "ooh"]
- **Matching**: singing back a note you've just heard. [Play: Merc's A, then a reply on the same A]
- **Cent**: a hundredth of an equal semitone, the unit MercuryPitch measures in (Lesson 2). [Play:
  a slow glide of 100 cents]
- **Target band**: the strip around a note that counts as landing on it. [Show: the band lights]

## Step 1 of 5: Where does my voice's note come from?

Lesson 1 said every note starts with something shaking. In your voice, it's your vocal folds, two
folds of tissue in your voice box. When you breathe, they're apart. When you sing, they come
together, and air from your lungs sets them vibrating, opening and closing over and over. Sing the
A at 220 Hz and they open and close 220 times a second. To go higher, muscles in your voice box
stretch them longer and tighter, and tighter folds vibrate faster, a little like a guitar string
when you tune it up.

**Try:** turn on slow motion, then drag Stretch from slack to tight.
**Notice:** slowed 220 times, the folds at A3 open and close once a second. Stretch them and they
speed up, and the bead climbs. You don't need to think about your folds when you sing, though: aim
at the sound instead.

_Toy: the vocal folds, seen from above, with Stretch and slow motion._

## Step 2 of 5: Do I need to sing loudly?

No. Loud and high are different things (Lesson 1), in your voice too: a quiet note is just as much
the right note as a loud one, and on MercuryPitch's pitch line it sits at the same height. So sing
at about the volume you talk at. Looking after your voice is that simple:

- Sing at about speaking volume: no shouting, no whispering.
- Keep water nearby, and sip it.
- Rest when your voice feels tired.
- Stop if it feels scratchy or hoarse.

**Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along.** Every step works without singing.

**Try:** move Louder, then Higher.
**Notice:** only Higher moves the bead. Louder makes the wave taller and leaves the note where it
is.

_Toy: Lesson 1's loud-or-high figure in Merc's voice, its sliders renamed Louder and Higher, beside
the four care lines._

## Step 3 of 5: If my ears are fine, why does my note come out off?

Because hearing a note and making it are two different jobs. Matching is a loop: **hear** Merc's
note, **try** it, **compare** the two (higher, lower or the same?), **adjust**, and compare again.
Your ears do the comparing, and for most people they're up to it. In one study, musicians and
non-musicians alike matched notes more accurately with a slider than with their voices. So start
with a slider.

**Try:** play Merc's note, drag the slider until your tone sounds the same, then press Compare. Do
three rounds.
**Notice:** each Compare tells you which way to go and how far, in cents, so the gap shrinks as you
adjust. Your voice is a slider too, just one you can't see. The pitch line is how you see it.

> **Common mix-up:** "Some people are just tone deaf."
> **Why it seems right:** when a note comes out off, it feels as if the ear must be at fault, and
> "tone deaf" is the everyday word for that.
> **The evidence:** the condition the word really names, congenital amusia, affects about 1.5 % of
> people. Most people who sing off hear pitch changes as well as anyone, and people match a note
> better with a slider than with their voice.
> **The better idea:** singing in tune is a skill: the link between hearing a note and making it.
> In a large online study of people aged 6 to 99, singers with musical training were more
> accurate, and accuracy showed no strong decline with age. It can get better with practice.

_Toy: Match it by ear, a slider tone against Merc's note, with Compare._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Beats.** Play your tone and Merc's together when they're close and you'll hear a slow
>   wobble, a beat. The number of beats a second is the difference between the two frequencies:
>   220 Hz against 222 Hz beats twice a second. As the tones meet, the beats slow down and stop.
>   Tuners listen for exactly this.
> - **Drifting home.** In the second singing test, the people who sang off tended to drift towards
>   their own comfortable note: a little flat on notes above it, a little sharp on notes below it.
>   If your line keeps landing low on a high note, it may be the same pull.

## Step 4 of 5: Does it count if I'm an octave away?

Voices come in sizes. Smaller folds vibrate faster, so children's voices sit highest, then most
women's, then most men's, and two people singing the same tune are often an octave apart. So when
you match Merc, the exact note isn't the only target: the same letter an octave up or down counts
too. It's the same spot on the spiral, one turn away (Lesson 1). Even the researchers counted it:
when someone sang in a different octave, they moved the target into that octave before measuring.

**Predict:** blue Merc sings an A3. Orange Merc answers with an A4, an octave higher. Does it count?
[ Yes, it counts ] [ No, it's a different note ]

(After a choice: Play blue's A3 · Play orange's A4 · Play both.)

Reveal: It counts: the same letter, twice as fast, one turn up the spiral. On the chart, orange's
line lands in the A band one octave up, and MercuryPitch's Voice Mirror counts it too. (If you said
no: it does sound higher, and that's what makes it tricky.)

**Try:** play both, then tap the A that's nearer your voice. You'll sing with that Merc at the end.
**Notice:** either choice is fine. The notes are the same; only the octave changes.

_Toy: two bands an octave apart, A3 and A4, with the predict and the voice choice._

## Step 5 of 5: How close is close enough?

Lesson 2 measured pitch in cents, a hundred to a semitone. Look at Merc's own note: it wavers 14
cents either side, on purpose, a small wobble called vibrato. So "in tune" can't be one exact line.
It's a band around the note. On this page, as in Voice Mirror, it reaches 35 cents either side,
about a third of a semitone. Even the accurate singers in the study's second test sat about 30
cents from the target, on average. A semitone off, the study's line, is 100 cents: nearly three
times as far as the band's edge.

**Try:** press Play to watch a line chase the band, then drag the line yourself.
**Notice:** inside the band, the readout says "in the band" and the band's edge lights. Past 50
cents the nearest note changes (Lesson 2): you're nearer a different note than Merc's.

_Toy: the band chart, Merc's amber band and a violet line, with Play and drag._

## Sing it: three notes

Your turn. Merc sings a note, then you sing it back: three notes, one at a time, slowly, in the
voice you chose in Step 4. Slow helps: in one study, when occasional singers sang a well-known song
slowly, most sang it as accurately as professional singers.

### Before you sing

> **Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along.**
> Choose listening if anything hurts, if your voice feels unusually hoarse or tired, if notes you
> usually reach have suddenly gone, if you're ill, or if talking or singing feels like hard work
> today.
>
> [ I'm comfortable: let's sing ] [ Just listen today ]

Just listen today: "Good call. Merc sings all three notes for you, and nothing listens. Rest your
voice. If hoarseness or discomfort lasts more than a couple of weeks, see a doctor or a voice
specialist."

### Warm-up (optional, about a minute)

1. **Three sighs.** Breathe in easily, then let a quiet "hoo" slide down from a comfortable high
   note to a comfortable low one, like a relieved sigh. [Play Merc's sigh]
2. **Three lip trills, if you like.** Close your lips loosely and blow so they flutter, "brrr",
   sliding up a little and back down. If your lips won't flutter, hum on "oo" instead. [Play the
   slide]

Nothing listens during the warm-up, and nothing is scored. Stop immediately if anything feels
uncomfortable.
[ Skip the warm-up ]

### Three notes

> **What this does:** it measures how far your note sits from Merc's, in cents, in any octave. It
> doesn't diagnose anything about your voice, and it isn't a test of whether you can sing. Your
> voice is analysed on this device: nothing is recorded, kept or sent.

[ Start ] (the browser asks for the microphone now)

For each note: **Listen**, while Merc sings it for two seconds; then **Your turn**: start when
you're ready, at about speaking volume, and hold it for about three seconds. Your violet line draws
against Merc's amber band, with the gap in cents beside it. The notes are A, then G a tone below,
then B a tone above: around A3 with blue Merc, or around A4 with orange Merc.

After each note, one line about the sound, then [ Again ] or [ Next note ]. For example:

- "In the band: 12 cents above Merc's note."
- "Right in the middle: 4 cents below."
- "Close: about 45 cents below. Listen again, then aim a touch higher."
- "About two semitones below, nearer a different note. Listen again, then slide up until your line
  meets the band."
- Added when it applies: "You sang it an octave lower: same note."
- "We didn't hear a steady note that time. Try again at about speaking volume."

After the third note: **How did that feel?** [ Easy ] [ Workable ] [ Effortful ] [ Uncomfortable ]

- Easy or Workable: "This time, 2 of your 3 notes landed in the band." Then, when fewer than three
  landed: "If that felt tricky, good: that's learning."
- Effortful: the same count, then "Keep it gentle: rest before you sing again."
- Uncomfortable: no count and no Again. "Thanks for saying so. That's enough singing for today:
  rest your voice. If hoarseness or discomfort lasts more than a couple of weeks, see a doctor or a
  voice specialist."

No microphone, or it's blocked: "No microphone? Sing along anyway: Merc plays each note and the
band waits for you. Nothing listens."

     Speaking volume is the gentle middle."

## Wrap

- Your voice makes a note with two vocal folds: stretched tighter, they vibrate faster and the note
  goes up. Sing at about speaking volume, and stop if it feels scratchy or hoarse.
- Matching is a loop: hear, try, compare, adjust. For most people the ears are fine; the link to
  the voice is the skill. An octave up or down counts.
- In tune is a band, not a line: on this page and in Voice Mirror, within 35 cents of the note.

**One-sentence challenge:** explain to a friend, in one sentence, why "I can't sing" usually isn't
an ear problem.

**Practise it:** in MercuryPitch, Voice Mirror asks you to glide up and down and to hold one note,
then plays five notes from the middle of your range for you to sing back. It counts a match in any
octave, and tells you how many landed within a third of a semitone: the band from Step 5. Keep the
glides comfortable at both ends. Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along, and come back
another day. Free, no account: **mercurypitch.com/mirror**

Ten minutes, gently, most days. Missed a day? Just come back.
