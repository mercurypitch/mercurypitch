## The study behind the hook

Pfordresher, P. Q. and Brown, S. (2007), "Poor-pitch singing in the absence of 'tone deafness'",
_Music Perception_ 25(2), 95–115, doi:10.1525/mp.2007.25.2.95 ("P&B 2007" below). The full text
was read from the first author's site (acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf).

1. Asked: in a prescreening questionnaire of 1,105 university students on an introductory psychology course, 59 % "indicated that they could not imitate melodies by singing" | Pfordresher and Brown (2007), "Poor-pitch singing in the absence of 'tone deafness'", _Music Perception_ 25(2), p. 95 | read; the hook's "In a survey, most of 1,105 students said they couldn't copy a tune", the cold open's "ASKED 59 %", and blue's "Lots of people say that. So two researchers tested it." (the paper's two authors), as Lesson 2 FACTS 36
2. Tested, Experiment 1: 79 students with "no formal music training" heard four-note sequences and sang them back; 10 of the 79 (13 %) were poor-pitch singers, whose note errors were "greater than +100 cents or less than –100 cents on average, i.e., more than one semitone off pitch" | P&B 2007, pp. 98, 100 | read; the cold open's "In the tests, 10 of 79 ... sang off", and its predict's "sang short tunes back"
3. Tested, Experiment 2: 45 students, "sampled randomly, regardless of training" (26 of them, 58 %, had more than a year of instrument or singing experience); 7 (16 %) sang every pitch except their comfort pitch outside ±1 semitone of the target | P&B 2007, pp. 108, 109, 113 | read; the cold open's "and 7 of 45 sang off"
4. Across the samples the rates "essentially converged": 15 %, 10 % and 16 %; "Transposers comprised 10-16% of each sample"; the abstract says "10-15% of the participants imitated pitch at least one semitone off"; and "The prevalence of poor production skills may be far lower than self-assessments indicate" | P&B 2007, abstract p. 95, pp. 113, 114 | read; on the page "TESTED 10–16 %" and, in the title, "about one in ten to one in six", Lesson 2 FACTS 36's figures
5. The 1,105 answered a questionnaire; the singing tests measured smaller groups of 79 and 45 students, so "Asked" and "Tested" describe different groups, and the page never says the tests measured the 1,105 | P&B 2007, pp. 95, 98, 108 | read; hence the hook's "In a survey" and "In tests", the cold open's figures, "ASKED" and "TESTED", and its "(The 59 % answered a questionnaire. In the tests, 10 of 79 and 7 of 45 sang off.)"
6. In Experiment 1, participants 41 to 79 (39 students) were picked by a prescreening "designed to increase the proportion of poor-pitch singers", and "all claimed that they were unable to imitate pitch while singing"; "Surprisingly, this prescreening procedure did not increase the proportion of poor-pitch singers": 4 of them (10 %) sang off, against 6 (15 %) of participants 1 to 40, who were not prescreened; so 35 of the 39 sang within a semitone on average | P&B 2007, pp. 98, 100, 113; 39 − 4 = 35 | read and arithmetic; the cold open's predict ("39 students who said they couldn't copy a note"), reveal card 1 ("Saying so didn't predict how they sang. Only four of them sang a semitone or more off. Of 40 others, not picked for saying so, six did.") and the answer's "most people who say they can't sing still land within a semitone" (with 1 and 4). One participant's signal was too weak and was removed (p. 98), group not stated, so the page gives the paper's own counts and no ratio
7. Most poor-pitch singers told pitches apart as well as accurate singers: Experiment 1 (participants 1 to 40) found "no significant difference in pitch-discrimination skill between accurate singers and singers who transpose while singing" (p. 105); "most of the poor-pitch singers actually performed well on the perceptual discrimination task", though "one poor-pitch singer did demonstrate a deficit in both" (p. 106); they "were able to discriminate pitch changes of greater than 50 cents" (p. 107); poor-pitch singing "is apparently a disorder of production but not perception, at least for most participants" (p. 112); in Experiment 2 the two groups "did not differ significantly" (p. 110); "pitch-discrimination skills did not predict accuracy in production" (p. 114) | P&B 2007 | read; said as reveal card 2's "For most, the ears were fine. Most of those who sang off heard small changes in pitch as well as the accurate singers did", the answer's "it's usually the link, not the ears", and "as well as accurate singers do" in the mix-up card and quiz question 2: "most", never "all", and "as well as accurate singers", never "as well as anyone"
8. The authors' account: poor-pitch singing "results from mismapping of pitch onto action, rather than problems specific to perceptual, motor, or memory systems" (abstract, p. 95), "a deficit in converting heard notes to phonation targets" (p. 114) | P&B 2007 | read; said as "The hard part was turning a heard note into a sung one: the link between ear and voice", Step 3's "Because hearing a note and making it are different jobs", and quiz question 2's "the link to the voice needs practice"
9. The word suggests a hearing problem: "the term 'tone deafness' implies that poor-pitch singing has a perceptual basis", and "such an implication may not be valid" | P&B 2007, p. 95 | read; the mix-up card's "Why it seems right: the word 'deaf' puts the blame on the ears."
10. The researchers counted an octave match: in Experiment 1, when a participant's mean absolute note error exceeded 600 cents, "it was assumed that the participant chose a different octave" and "the target pitches were rescaled to a different octave" (p. 100); Experiment 2's ±1 semitone boundaries were drawn "also including octave equivalents" (p. 109) | P&B 2007 | read; Step 4's "The researchers counted octave matches too."
11. In Experiment 2, the seven singers classed as good (all but one without music training) had "mean absolute error scores of 30 cents" | P&B 2007, p. 109 | read; Step 5's "Even the accurate singers in the study's second test sat about 30 cents from the target, on average", which motivates the band; a small group, so the page names the test and never says "accurate singers are 30 cents off"
12. In Experiment 2, poor-pitch singers "tended to sing as flat those notes that were higher than their comfort pitch, and to sing as sharp those notes that were lower than their comfort pitch", and missed every note but their comfort pitch by more than a semitone, with mean absolute errors of "229 cents on average (range = 114-307)" | P&B 2007, p. 109 | read; cut from the page's Go deeper in the review pass, for length; if it returns, it says "flat" and "sharp", never "a little", and never applies the finding to the viewer
13. The paper measures pitch in cents "such that 100 cents = 1 semitone" | P&B 2007, p. 100; Lesson 2 FACTS 37 | read; "a semitone off" is 100 cents (Step 5)

## The voice

20. The voice's sound comes from the vocal folds vibrating; the folds are two bands of tissue that sit opposite each other in the larynx (the voice box); they are apart for breathing; to make sound they come together and air from the lungs sets them vibrating, between open and closed | NIDCD (US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders), "Taking Care of Your Voice", nidcd.nih.gov/health/taking-care-your-voice, updated 11 June 2025: "The sound of your voice is produced by vibration of the vocal folds"; "two bands of smooth muscle tissue that are positioned opposite each other in the larynx" (the page says only "two folds of tissue"); "When you are not speaking, the vocal folds are open so that you can breathe"; "The vocal folds snap together while air from the lungs blows past, making them vibrate"; figure, "vibrate between open and closed positions" | fetched; Step 1 and "Vocal folds" in the three words; Lesson 1 FACTS 6
21. Each cycle of vocal fold vibration opens and closes the glottis (an open phase and a closed phase), and the voice's fundamental frequency is the frequency of that vibration | Zhang, Z. (2016), "Mechanics of human voice production and control", _Journal of the Acoustical Society of America_ 140(4), 2614–2635, doi:10.1121/1.4964509: § III.A, p. 2618 (the phonation cycle); § III.B, p. 2620 ("the fundamental frequency of vocal fold vibration") | read (UCLA-hosted PDF, surgery.medsch.ucla.edu/spl/papers/2016JASA10.pdf)
22. So a sung A3 at 220 Hz means the folds open and close 220 times a second, and slowed 220 times they do it once a second (at 440 Hz, twice) | 21; hertz as cycles per second (Lesson 1 FACTS 2); 220 / 220 = 1, 440 / 220 = 2 | arithmetic; Step 1's "220 times a second for the A at 220 Hz" and its Notice, and the folds toy's readout
23. Pitch rises when the folds are stretched longer and tighter: "Vocal fold tension is regulated by elongating or shortening the vocal folds", and cricothyroid contraction "elongates the vocal folds and increases the stiffness and tension" (p. 2617); "stiffening or tensioning the vocal folds would increase the F0 of the voice" (p. 2626); with elongation, "the effect of the increase in stiffness and tension on F0 appears to dominate that of increasing length" (p. 2627) | Zhang (2016), § II.B "Vocal fold posturing" and § IV.A "Fundamental frequency" | read; said as "To go higher, muscles in your voice box stretch them longer and tighter, and tighter folds vibrate faster" (Step 1, quiz question 1, the Wrap)
24. The guitar-string comparison is a simplification the field itself uses: "The vibration frequency of a string is determined by its length, tension, and mass", and by analogy F0 "increases with increasing tension"; the analogy is "conceptually simple and heuristically useful" but misses features of the folds | Zhang (2016), § IV.A, p. 2626 | read; said only as "like a guitar string tuned up"
    24a. The throat, mouth and nose shape the sound the folds make: the vocal tract "acts as a filter and shapes the sound source spectrum into different sounds before they are radiated from the mouth" | Zhang (2016), § III.A, p. 2618; NIDCD (as 20): "The vibrations produce sound waves that travel through the throat, nose, and mouth" | read and fetched; quiz question 1's feedback, "The mouth shapes the sound; the note starts with the folds" (Lesson 13 teaches the rest)
25. Smaller folds vibrate faster: "because of the small vocal fold size, children tend to have the highest F0, followed by female and then male" | Zhang (2016), § IV.A, p. 2626 | read; Step 4's "From one person to the next, smaller folds vibrate faster. So children's voices sit highest, then most women's, then most men's" ("most", never "all")
26. Two people singing the same tune are often an octave apart | Lesson 1 FACTS 18 (Titze 1994: average speaking pitch roughly an octave apart for adult men and women) | carried; "often", never "always"
27. The same letter an octave up or down is the same note name, an octave is a 2:1 ratio (440 Hz is twice 220 Hz), and on the pitch spiral the two sit on the same spot, one turn apart | Lesson 1 FACTS 7, 9, 12, 12a | carried; Step 4, its predict and quiz question 3.     28a. Loud and high are different things, and MercuryPitch draws a voice as a line whose height is its pitch, so a quiet note and a loud one at the same pitch sit at the same height | Lesson 1 FACTS 3 and 21 | carried; Step 2's text, quiz question 4, and Step 3's "When you sing, your pitch line on screen shows you that"

## Unverified, not used

- "Most listeners stop hearing a note as out of tune under about 20 cents": the app's FAQ says so
  (`src/seo/entry-pages.ts:506`) with no source (Lesson 2 FACTS, Unsupported). The page's band is
  the app's own 35-cent definition (41), offered as a definition, not as a limit of hearing.
- "Seeing your pitch line makes you sing more in tune on the spot": Hutchins and Peretz (2012) found
  that a visual representation of pitch did not improve matching (16), and Berglin et al. found
  visual-feedback training helped mostly with melodies (19). The evidence is mixed, so the page says
  only that the band shows where to aim and how far off a note sits.
- "Berglin et al.'s participants were poor-pitch singers": the title says "poor-pitch remediation",
  but the abstract read does not describe the participants, so the page never says who they were.
- "Singing louder pushes the pitch up, by 2 to 6 Hz per cm H2O of pressure (Titze 1989)": cited in
  research/pedagogy-research.md § 3.9, but the paper was not read for this lesson. Zhang (2016,
  p. 2627) says raised subglottal pressure often raises F0, which is why Step 2 no longer says loud
  and high are different "in your voice too"; it says only that a quiet note is just as much the
  right note.
- "A warm-up improves accuracy or protects the voice": the reviews (Ribeiro et al. 2016) were not
  read for this lesson, so the warm-up is offered without a claimed benefit.
- "Everyone imitates a recording of their own voice best (Pfordresher and Mantell 2014)": not read;
  not used.
- "Amusia was once put at 4 % (Kalmus and Fry 1980)": the older estimate was not read for this
  lesson; P&B 2007 (p. 113) puts perception-based estimates at "no more than 5%". Neither is used.
- "Short sessions let the voice recover (Hunter and Titze 2009)": not read; the page's "Ten minutes,
  gently, most days" is the playbook's house line (38), not a research claim.
- "Anyone can learn to sing in tune": not supported. Two of Dalla Bella's singers stayed off even
  slowly (17), and amusia exists (14). The page says "most" and "it can get better with practice".
