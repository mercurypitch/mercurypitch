## Where a source contradicts the card or the season plan

1. **Henry (2004) and Mishra (2014) could not be read past their abstracts.** Both are paywalled
   (SAGE); no open copy was found. The card asks for them "past their abstracts, before a script quotes
   a number". The numbers the plan uses (67 singers, 15 skills, 12 weeks, two groups; 92 studies, 124
   analyses, a small overall effect) are all in the abstracts, read in full here. Anything else stays
   unclaimed (R18-1, R18-2).
2. **Henry (2004) does not compare pattern teaching with note-naming.** Both groups were taught the
   15 targeted skills, one with new melodies and one with familiar ones, and both improved equally; there
   was no group without the skills. So it supports "pattern-based teaching was followed by gains", not
   "patterns beat names". The card's wording, "both groups improved", is right; the plan keeps to it.
3. **Mishra's abstract reports the effects with a minus sign** (d = -0.18; within-group d = -0.48) and
   does not explain the sign convention; the abstract's words ("small", "positively affecting",
   "generally improved") set the direction. The plan quotes the words, not the signed numbers.
4. **A breath mark is not one of ABRSM's Grade 1 signs** (its list is crescendo, diminuendo, slur, tie,
   staccato, accent, pause and others); it rests on Lesson 14's sources (R18-9). The card's "ABRSM Grade
   1 terms" covers p, f, the hairpins, staccato and the slur.

## Claims

**R18-1. In one 12-week study, 67 novice high-school singers taught 15 targeted pitch skills (scalar,
cadential and chordal) improved at sight-singing, whether taught with new melodies or familiar ones.**
Henry 2004, abstract: "Fifteen pitch skills encompassing scalar, cadential, and chordal tasks were
included in the study. Over a 12-week period, two randomly assigned groups of novice high school
singers received instruction. Group A (n = 41) received instruction with new melodies written
specifically to incorporate the targeted skills. Group B (n = 26) received instruction using familiar
melodies that contained the same skills. All participants (n = 67) achieved a significantly higher mean
score on the posttest than on the pretest (t = 4.38, 66 df, p < .00004). Each treatment group also
obtained significantly higher mean scores on the posttest than on the pretest; however, there was no
significant difference between the two treatments." Abstract only. Weak for "pattern teaching works"
(no untreated control); Moderate for "both groups improved".

**R18-2. A meta-analysis of 92 sight-reading intervention studies found a small overall effect; of the
moderators, only the kind of training mattered, and aural training and singing or solfège were among
the kinds that helped; readers in both trained and untrained groups generally improved.**
Mishra 2014, abstract: "A meta-analysis was conducted with 92 quasi-experimental research studies on
sightreading (124 individual analyses)"; "The analysis revealed a small overall effect size for
treatment"; "Of the moderator variables, only treatment-type was significant, with treatments
categorized as "Aural Training," "Controlled Reading," "Creative Activities," and "Singing/Solfege"
significantly and positively affecting sightreading"; "There was a moderately strong within-group
effect size ... indicating that sightreading generally improved pre- to posttest for both control and
treatment groups." The moderators tested included "sightreading mode (sightreading/sightsinging)" and
"age and experience of sight reader". Abstract only. Moderate (a meta-analysis of quasi-experiments,
with a small effect).

**R18-3. Exams start sight-singing with a short beat, then two bars, then four.**
RCM 2019 p. 11: "Students may tap one measure of the beat before performing the sight-singing excerpt
to establish a sense of pulse and to set the tempo for their performance"; p. 14: Preparatory's test is
"a two-measure, unaccompanied melody that moves by step"; p. 22: Level 2's is "a four-measure,
unaccompanied melody that moves by step or skip". Full text. Strong for the exam's design. Supports the
page's one bar of count-in and two bars to read.

**R18-4. How far ahead fluent readers look is not claimed.** The card marks it not verified; no source
was read for it here. **Not verified**, and not used.

**R18-5. Dynamics: p (piano) is quiet, f (forte) loud; mp and mf moderately; a crescendo hairpin opens
to get louder, a decrescendo or diminuendo hairpin closes to get quieter.**
OMT 2e 1.7: "The dynamic marking forte means loud, while piano means quiet"; "mezzo forte to mean
moderately loud and mezzo piano to mean moderately quiet"; "Crescendo (cresc.) means to get louder,
while decrescendo (decresc.) and diminuendo (dim.) both mean to get quieter"; "hairpin" names the
symbols, crescendo on the left of its figure, decrescendo on the right. ABRSM 2023 p. 18, Grade 1
terms include "f (forte)", "p (piano)", "mf", "mp", "pp", "ff", "crescendo (or cresc.)", "diminuendo (or
dim.)", and Grade 1 signs "crescendo" and "diminuendo". Full text. Strong.

**R18-6. A staccato dot asks for notes more separated; a slur asks for them smooth and connected.**
OMT 2e 1.7: "Staccato means to play or sing notes more separated, leaving space between notes. This
articulation is usually indicated by a dot placed above or below notes"; "Legato means to play or sing
smoothly or connected. This articulation is indicated by a curved slur marking." ABRSM 2023 p. 18, Grade
1 terms "legato", "staccato (or stacc.)"; signs "slur", "staccato". Full text. Strong.

**R18-7. A slur joins different notes; a tie joins two of the same pitch into one sound.**
OMT 2e 1.8: "A tie is a curved line that connects two or more notes with the same pitch"; OMT 2e 1.7's
slur marks legato across notes (R18-6); Lesson 17 R17-4. Full text. Strong.

## Interesting findings

1. **Readers improved whether or not they were trained.** In Mishra's meta-analysis, sight-reading
   "generally improved pre- to posttest for both control and treatment groups" (R18-2). Moderate.
   **Possible wrap line**: reading grows with reading.
2. **Singers and players improved alike.** Mishra tested sight-reading against sight-singing as a
   moderator, and only the kind of training mattered (R18-2). Moderate. **Possible Go deeper.**
3. **Familiar tunes taught reading as well as new ones** in Henry's study (R18-1). Weak to Moderate.
   **Possible Go deeper**: practise reading with tunes you know.
4. **Exam singers tap one bar of the beat before they sing** (R18-3). Strong. **Possible Go deeper or
   Step 3's line.**
5. **The breath mark is a comma above the staff, and it borrows its time from the note before it, so
   the beat never stops** (R18-9, from Lesson 14's sources). Moderate. **Possible Step line.**
