## Four words you'll meet

- **Harmony part**: a second tune sung with the first, fitting it. [Play: tune, then tune with
  harmony]
- **Diatonic third**: a third made only from the key's notes: sometimes 4 semitones, sometimes 3.
  [Play: C–E, then D–F] (R1)
- **Chord note**: a note of the chord sounding under the tune. [Play: C major with E]
- **Passing note**: a note that steps between two chord notes, in one direction (American books say
  passing tone). [Play: E, F, G over C major] (R5)
- Words: 80.

## Step 1 of 4: What's a third above each note?

- Point: count two letters up, staying in the key: C to E, D to F, E to G. Counted in semitones,
  the thirds differ: major (4) on do, fa and so; minor (3) on re, mi, la and ti (R1). Lesson 12's
  chords already showed it: the chord on re is minor because D up to F is 3.
- Examples: each third up the C major scale, played as a pair, with its semitone count.
- Toy: the ring with a chord-arm two letters wide that walks round the key; each third shows 3 or 4.
- Try: walk the arm from do to ti. Notice: 4, 3, 3, 4, 4, 3, 3.
- Facts: R1, R7. Words: 130.

## Step 2 of 4: Why not move every note the same distance?

- Common mix-up card (half right):
  - Belief: "A harmony is the tune moved up by the same distance everywhere."
  - Why it seems right: a harmony in thirds does move with the tune, up when it goes up.
  - The evidence: a fixed four semitones leaves the key: over re it gives F♯, where the key's third
    is F. MercuryPitch's own harmony code says so: a fixed shift "would harmonise the second degree
    as D-F#-A and leave the key", so it walks the scale instead (R8).
  - The better idea: the key decides each third's size; the harmony follows the tune's shape.
- Examples: "Twinkle" with its in-key third above (R3): five major thirds and three minor.
- Toy (signature): the contour copied a third higher, the copy bending to the key's staircase, some
  rungs 4 and some 3.
- Thirds are consonances, imperfect consonances in Lesson 23's terms, which is why the pair blends
  (R2).
- Facts: R2, R3, R8. Words: 190.

## Step 3 of 4: Above or below?

- Point: a harmony can sit a third below too: under do re mi re do, la ti do ti la (R4). Lower
  voices take the part below, higher voices the part above. When Tavi sings above Merc, an octave
  higher as always, her third becomes a tenth, an octave and a third: the same letters, so the same
  harmony (SEASON-2 § 6.3 item 3; Lesson 1's octave).
- Examples: C4 D4 E4 D4 C4 with E4 F4 G4 F4 E4 above; with A3 B3 C4 B3 A3 below.
- Toy: the pitch canvas, the tune chrome, the learner's band amber above or below.
- Tavi's re-explanation: "So whichever side I'm on, I count two letters and stay in the key."
- Facts: R4. Words: 140.

## Step 4 of 4: What's the note in the middle?

- Predict (written out): "Merc holds a C major chord, C, E and G. Over it, the harmony sings mi,
  fa, so: E, F, G. Which of the three isn't in the chord?" [ Fa ] [ Mi ]. Reveal: "Fa. E and G are
  chord notes; F passes between them by step, in one direction: a passing note (R5). (Mi is the
  harmony's first note, which made it a fair guess.)"
- Point: passing notes let a part move by step between chord notes. The tune does it too: in do re
  mi, re passes.
- Examples: C major chord under E F G; under C D E.
- Toy: chord notes glow; the passing note is drawn hollow.
- Facts: R5. Words: 130.

## Wrap

  doesn't give a harmony."
