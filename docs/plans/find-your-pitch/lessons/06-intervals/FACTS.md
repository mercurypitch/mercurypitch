## Unsupported, not used

- "Somewhere over the rainbow" for the octave, the anchor most often cited: published in 1939
  (Wikipedia, Rainbow, infobox: "1939 by Leo Feist, Inc."), after the US public-domain cut-off of
  works published before 1931 (Cornell chart), and its writers' deaths and renewals were not
  checked, so its UK and US terms were not worked out. Not public domain by any rule read here; left
  out. No public-domain octave anchor known in both countries was found, so the octave is Lesson
  1's two As.
- "Happy Birthday to You" for the major second: Wikipedia says it is in the public domain in the US
  and the EU ("in 2015, the copyright claim was declared invalid"; "Patty Hill died in 1946 as the
  last surviving author, so the copyright expired in these countries on January 1, 2017"), but that
  rests on a court case and on disputed authorship rather than a plain expiry, and neither the
  ruling nor the settlement was read. "Twinkle" already gives the tone, so it is left out to keep
  the anchors few and solid.
- "Here comes the bride" for the fourth: its English words were not traced to a source or a date,
  so it was not considered further.
- "Mozart wrote 'Twinkle, Twinkle'": the melody was published in 1761 and Mozart later wrote
  variations on it (Wikipedia, Twinkle, lead: "later arranged by several composers, including
  Mozart"). The page names no composer.
- How much less often the contour-only tunes were recognised: the abstract gives the order and the
  chi-square, not the rates (34). The page gives no numbers.
- "Change one note and it's a different tune": not claimed. One changed note changes two intervals
  (`verify.py`: C C G G B A G), and the page demonstrates two changed intervals only with Step 4's
  swap (37), whose kind D&F 1971 tested (34).
- How common absolute pitch is: no source read gives a figure (T&H 1993's abstract defines it and
  gives none). The Go deeper defines it only.
- "Most people think a tune is a list of notes": no data. The misconception is voiced only as
  orange's sincere guess, and the cold-open reveal calls "some of the notes" a fair guess only
  because the tune still sounds like "Twinkle".
- "Six-month-olds remember tunes by their intervals too" (P&T 2005): read and supported (32), cut
  from the Go deeper for length, so not on the page.
- "Tunes move by step more than by leap" (OMT 2e 2.01, summing up Huron 2006): read and supported
  (23), cut from the Go deeper for length. Huron's book was not read.
- "Leaps are harder to sing than steps": Lesson 9's hook, not claimed here.
- "Perfect intervals sound the most stable": not used; consonance is not this lesson's topic.
