## Four words you'll meet

- **Interval**: the distance between two notes, which this lesson counts in semitones. [Play: C
  up to G]
- **Step**: a move to the next letter up or down: in a major scale, a semitone or a tone. [Play: A
  down to G]
- **Leap**: a move that skips at least one letter, like D up to G. [Play: D up to G]
- **Transpose**: to move a whole tune up or down, every note by the same interval. [Play: from
  C, then from F♯]

## Step 1 of 5: What shape is a tune?

Here's the tune's first line on a chart like MercuryPitch's: each note at its height, one row per
semitone. Left to right: two notes the same, a big jump up, two the same, a small step up, two the
same, a step down. The tune's contour is which way it goes from each note to the next: up, down or
the same.

**Try:** play the line, then tap any note to hear it.
**Notice:** the ups, downs and repeats make a shape you could draw with a finger.

_Toy: the tune line, on C._

## Step 2 of 5: How far apart are the notes?

Contour says which way. An interval says how far, in semitones. From the first "twin-kle" to the
second, the tune leaps up 7 semitones, C to G. On "lit-tle star" it steps down 2, a tone. In the
second line, "I won-der" steps down just 1, a semitone. A step goes to the next letter, so in this
tune it's always a semitone or a tone. Anything bigger is a leap. The first two lines hold one leap
and six steps.

**Try:** tap a gap between two notes to hear it. Then play ten rounds of Step or leap?
**Notice:** a leap skips notes the tune could have stepped through: C up to G passes D, E and F.

_Toys: the tune line with both lines and its gaps numbered; the Step or leap? ear game._

## Step 3 of 5: What happens if I slide the whole tune?

Orange Merc: "If I slide the line up, won't the gaps stretch?"

Lift the line off the rows and slide it to a new start. Every note moves by the same number of
semitones, so every interval stays the same. That's transposing, and it's why "Twinkle" was still
"Twinkle" from F♯. The do-re-mi names stay too: from any start, the first line is do do so so la
la so. Only the letters change, as do moved with home in Lesson 4.

**Try:** slide the line to three starts and play each one, then switch the names to do-re-mi.
**Notice:** the gap numbers and the do-re-mi never change. Only the letters under the notes do.

_Toy: the tune line with Slide and the names switch._

## Step 4 of 5: So is it the ups and downs I remember?

**Predict:** this version keeps five of the first line's seven notes, and every up and down, but
swaps the sizes of its two rises, the leap and the step up. Is it still "Twinkle"?
[ Yes: most notes are the same ] [ No: it changes ]

(After a choice: Play the first line · Play the swapped version. With sound off, the hint reads:
"Sound is off, so nothing played. Turn sound on to hear them, then press Play the swapped
version.")

Reveal: It changes. Five notes match, and every up and down, but now the first rise is 2 semitones
and the second is 7. Two intervals changed, and so did the tune. (Most notes did match, so "yes"
was a fair guess.)

> **Common mix-up:** "A tune is a list of notes, so changing the notes changes the tune."
> **Why it seems right:** on a piano, a tune is a row of particular keys to press.
> **The evidence:** change every note but keep the intervals, and it's still "Twinkle". Keep five
> notes but swap two intervals, and it isn't. In one study, people heard familiar folk tunes that
> kept their rhythm and their ups and downs. Versions with new sizes were recognised less often than
> ones that kept the sizes too.
> **The better idea:** a tune is a pattern of intervals, so it can start on any note.

**Try:** press Swap sizes, play the line, then swap back.
**Notice:** the contour, the up-and-down shape, survives the swap. The tune doesn't.

_Toy: the tune line with Swap sizes._

## Step 5 of 5: Do the distances have names?

Musicians name an interval by counting letters, both ends included: C up to G runs C, D, E, F, G,
five letters, so it's a fifth. The number of semitones gives the rest of the name: a fifth of seven
semitones is a perfect fifth. To name an interval, think of a tune you know: hear a leap, and ask
which tune starts that way. Five to keep, with Leap's labels:

| Interval                      | Semitones | Hear it in                                              |
| ----------------------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Minor second (m2), a semitone | 1         | "Twinkle": "I won-", stepping down                      |
| Major second (M2), a tone     | 2         | "Twinkle": "lit-tle star", stepping down to "star"      |
| Perfect fourth (P4)           | 5         | "Auld Lang Syne": "Should auld", leaping up             |
| Perfect fifth (P5)            | 7         | "Twinkle": "twin-kle, twin-kle", leaping up             |
| Octave (P8)                   | 12        | Lesson 1's two As: the same letter, twice the frequency |

**Try:** play each tune, then its interval on its own. Then play eight rounds of Name it.
**Notice:** "Should auld" and "twin-kle, twin-kle" both leap up, and the fifth is a tone wider.

_Toys: the tune line with its intervals named; the anchor board; the Name it ear game._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Relative pitch.** Hearing notes by their distances from each other is called relative pitch.
>   Most adults remember a tune mainly by those distances, not by its exact notes, and know it sung
>   high or low (Plantinga and Trainor, 2005). Absolute pitch is something else: naming or singing
>   a note with no other note to go by (Takeuchi and Hulse, 1993).

## Wrap

- A tune is a pattern of intervals, the distances from note to note. Move every note by the same
  interval and it's the same tune: that's transposing.
- A step goes to the next letter, a semitone or a tone; anything bigger is a leap. The first two
  lines of "Twinkle" hold six steps and one leap.
- You can name an interval by a tune you know: a perfect fifth opens "Twinkle, Twinkle", and a
  perfect fourth opens "Auld Lang Syne".
