## Where a source contradicts the card or the season plan

1. **The card's Takeuchi and Hulse line says "absolute pitch is rare"; the abstract does not.** The
   abstract (read in full) supports "learned early, up to about age 6" and the shift to "perceiving
   relations among features", and that possessors' pitch memory runs on learned labels. It gives no
   prevalence. The "1 in 10,000" figure often quoted with it was found only in a secondary citation and
   is **not verified**; the plan says no number and does not say "rare" on this source (R16-1).
2. **The card's "whether choirs' practice of taking a note is worth a source" is answered better by an
   exam board.** The Royal Conservatory's voice syllabus has the examiner play "the broken tonic triad
   and the beginning note" before every sight-singing test, twice, from its first level (R16-2). That is
   exactly the page's "hear do, then do-mi-so-mi-do", so the plan cites RCM, not choir practice.
3. **The season plan's list of Sight-Singing's limits misses two that matter most for this lesson.**
   During a run the drill draws the live pitch tracker above the staff, with the target band and the
   next notes as rungs, the next two named (R16-15); and the hold bar names the target note, "Hold C4 to
   continue" (R16-14). So a learner can sing by following the pitch line, not by reading, and a
   "names-off" option (§ 5 item 10e) has to hide three things, not one.
4. **Henry (2004) is not a test of pattern teaching against note-naming.** Both of its groups were
   taught the targeted skills; the comparison was new melodies against familiar ones (R16 carried to
   Lesson 18; noted here because the card's family of claims leans on it).

## Claims

**R16-1. Absolute pitch, often called perfect pitch, is naming or producing a note with no reference
note; the leading account is that it is learned early, up to about age 6, after which people come to
hear relations between notes.**
T&H 1993, abstract: "Absolute pitch (AP) is the ability to identify a tone's pitch or to produce a tone
at a particular pitch without the use of an external reference pitch"; "evidence points toward the
early-learning theory. This theory states that AP can be learned by anyone during a limited period
early in development, up to about age 6, after which a general developmental shift from perceiving
individual features to perceiving relations among features makes AP difficult or impossible to
acquire." Abstract only. Moderate (a review's own summary; "the theory states" is the paper's hedge,
so the plan says "researchers think"). No prevalence figure: **not verified**, not used.

**R16-2. Sight-singing exams give the singer the key and the first note before they read.**
RCM 2019 p. 14 (Preparatory), Singing: "Students will be asked to sing a two-measure, unaccompanied
melody that moves by step. The examiner will play the broken tonic triad and the beginning note once.
The student will be given a short time to silently review the music. The examiner will then repeat
the broken tonic triad and the beginning note before the student sings." The table: beginning note
"tonic", key "C major", range "five notes (tonic to dominant)". Level 1 (p. 17) the same; Level 2 (p.
22) "a four-measure, unaccompanied melody that moves by step or skip"; Level 3 (p. 27) one that "may
include seconds, thirds, or fifths"; Level 4 (p. 32) "seconds, thirds, fourths, or fifths". Full text.
Strong for what the exam does (Practitioner as pedagogy). The page's checkpoint copies Preparatory's
shape: C major, do to so, by step, after do-mi-so and do.

**R16-3. Exams accept any naming system for sight-singing.**
RCM 2019 p. 11: "students may use any system that enables them to produce the correct pitches; for
example, solfège (fixed do), tonic sol-fa (moveable do), or numbers. Students may also use a vowel or
syllable (such as [a] or [la])." Full text. Strong for the exam's rule. Supports the plan's "sing on
the syllables or on 'ooh'".

**R16-4. Whether movable or fixed do teaches sight-singing better is not settled; the strongest
predictor in the largest study was choir experience.**
D&M 1995, abstract: 414 singers in four Texas high schools; "the number of years of school choir
experience was the strongest predictor of individual success"; "students in the movable-do groups
scored significantly higher than did those in the fixed-do groups. However, this finding was tempered
by the existence of other differences between the groups regarding private lessons, the consistency
of method in the students' early solfege training, and the sight-singing assessment procedures".
Abstract only. Weak on the method question (confounded; pedagogy-research.md § 3.7 grades the whole
question Weak). The plan claims nothing about methods, as the card says; it may use the choir-experience
line in Go deeper, as "in one large study".

**R16-5. Hearing a note in your head before you sing it ("audiation") is a practitioner idea with a
plausible link to singing in tune.**
Gordon's audiation, "hearing music that is not physically present" (pedagogy-research.md § 3.5,
secondary, Practitioner). P&H 2013, abstract: "The percentage of trials participants sung in tune
correlated significantly with self-reports of vividness for auditory imagery, although not with the
ability to control auditory imagery. Pitch discrimination was not predicted by auditory imagery
scores." Abstract only. Moderate for the correlation; Weak as a reason to practise it (no intervention
was tested). The plan says "singers often", "may help", and "in one study, people who said they heard
tunes vividly in their heads sang more of them in tune", never that it trains accuracy.

**R16-6. Fading the syllables after the first reading follows concreteness fading.**
Fyfe 2014, as pedagogy-research.md:491-493 reports it: "begin with concrete, manipulable
representations and fade to abstract ones". Not re-read. Moderate (a systematic review in maths and
science). Supports names shown on the first phrase and hidden on the second.

**R16-8. On the staff, the next line up from a line is two letters higher: do, mi and so in C sit on

(Lesson 9 quick check 2; Lesson 10 FACTS 12). Arithmetic and carried. Strong.

## Interesting findings

1. **The first sight-singing test in the Royal Conservatory's voice exams is five notes, do to so, by
   step, in C, and the examiner plays do-mi-so and do twice before it** (R16-2, p. 14). Strong. **Possible
   cold-open reveal line or Go deeper**: the page's checkpoint is that test's shape.
2. **People with absolute pitch name notes with learned labels; their memory for pitch is not better
   as such.** T&H 1993 abstract: "AP possessors' memory for pitches is mediated by verbal pitch names;
   they do not have superior memory for pitches per se." Abstract only; Moderate. **Possible Go
   deeper**, a myth worth correcting: perfect pitch is not a super memory for sound.
3. **In 414 high-school singers, years in a school choir predicted sight-singing best, ahead of piano
   and voice lessons** (R16-4). Abstract only; Moderate for the ranking within that sample. **Possible
   wrap line**: reading is a skill that grows with singing.
4. **Hearing it inside and singing in tune go together, but telling pitches apart did not.** P&H 2013
   (R16-5): accuracy correlated with how vividly people heard tunes in their heads; pitch discrimination
   did not correlate with it. Abstract only; Moderate. **Possible Go deeper** for "hear it inside".
5. **Exams do not mind how you name the notes**: fixed do, movable do, numbers or "la" all count
   (R16-3). Strong. **Possible Go deeper**, which also lets the page sing on "ooh" without apology.
