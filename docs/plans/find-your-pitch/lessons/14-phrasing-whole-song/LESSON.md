## Four words you'll meet

- **Phrase**: a musical thought that ends at a point of rest. [Play: the first phrase]
- **Legato**: singing smoothly, with no break in the sound, as the notes and words change. [Play:
  legato, then with breaks]
- **Swell**: one note growing louder, then softer. Classical singers call it messa di voce, Italian
  for "placing the voice". [Play: a swell]
- **Cadence**: the end of a phrase, where the tune or its chords come to rest, fully or partly.
  [Play: "what you are", with its chords]

## Step 1 of 5: What's a phrase?

A phrase is a musical thought that ends at a point of rest. Phrases vary in length, and musicians
don't always agree where one ends. In this lesson, each line of the song's words is one: eight
beats, ending on a long note where the words pause. You've met its parts already: its beat (Lesson
8), its notes from the major scale (Lesson 4) and its leap from do up to so (Lessons 6 and 9). This
lesson puts them together in one song, then shapes it.

**Try:** play the whole song, then tap a phrase to hear it alone.
**Notice:** six phrases, and each one ends on its only long note.

_Toy: the song line, the whole song in six phrases, with a button for each phrase and the playing
phrase's words as a caption._

## Step 2 of 5: Where do I breathe?

At the end of a phrase, where the words pause. A song's score can mark the place with a breath mark,
which looks like a comma and usually sits at a phrase's end. The breath takes its time from the note
before it, so the beat doesn't change. A breath is a pause in the sound, so a breath inside a phrase
breaks its legato. Merc's take 1 snatched one there, between "little" and "star".

**Try:** play take 1, then take 2, and watch the breath marks.
**Notice:** take 1's breath takes its time from "little", inside the phrase. Take 2's takes it from
"star", at the phrase's end.

> **Common mix-up:** "If every note is in tune, the song is finished."
> **Why it seems right:** a pitch line shows only the notes, and every note can sit in its band.
> **The evidence:** Merc's two takes put every note in its band and still differ: where the breath
> falls, and how "star" grows and fades. One guide for new singing teachers lists intonation as one
> area of technique among many, beside phrasing and legato.
> **The better idea:** sing the notes, then shape the phrase: plan its breaths and its swell.

_Toy: the song line, the first phrase, with take 1 and take 2 and their breath marks._

## Step 3 of 5: How do I make a note grow and fade?

With a swell: start the note soft, let it grow, then let it soften again, all on one breath.
Classical singers have prized it for centuries. In Lesson 5, a harder push made a note louder and
higher. A swell asks for louder without higher. In one study, nine adults with healthy voices and no
professional singing training swelled one note on "ee". Most kept its pitch almost steady. Here,
grow only to about speaking volume.

**Try:** play the swell, then Lesson 5's push.
**Notice:** both grow louder, and the strip under each grows with them. Then the push's line climbs
out of the band, and the swell's stays in it. Both are drawings, not recordings.

_Toy: the song line on one note, a swell beside Lesson 5's push, each with its level strip._

## Step 4 of 5: How does a phrase end?

With a cadence. Chords are numbered by the scale note they're built on, as in Lesson 12: I on do, IV
on fa and V on so. "What you are" lands on do as the chords go from V to I: a perfect cadence. To
most listeners, ending on do sounds finished, as in Lesson 4. "World so high" stops on re, over V:
an imperfect cadence. It sounds unfinished and calls for more, so the song goes on.

**Try:** play "How I wonder what you are", then "Up above the world so high", each with its chords.
**Notice:** after "are", the song could stop. After "high", it waits for more.

_Toy: the song line, its second phrase, then its third, each with its chords under it as numerals._

## Step 5 of 5: Why listen back?

You hear yourself differently from everyone else. As you speak, your voice reaches your ears through
your skull as well as through the air, so you hear more bass in it than others do. A recording
carries only the air's part, which is one reason your recorded voice can sound strange. Listen back
for your plan, not the sound of your voice: where did each take breathe, and did "star" grow and
fade? Sing it records your takes on this device, and nothing scores them.

**Try:** play Merc's two takes back to back, then scrub through take 2.
**Notice:** the breath mark lights where each take breathes, and the light on "star" grows and
fades.

_Toy: the song line, the first phrase, with both takes, Play and scrub._

### The phrase, the swell and the range

- The phrase: "Twinkle, twinkle, little star", seven notes, do, do, so, so, la, la, so, at 80 beats
  a minute: 6.0 s, from D3 up to B3 for the lower voice (or D4 to B4), the same notes as the
  CURRICULUM's checkpoint asks for, sung twice (FACTS 18, 20, 72).
- The phrase convention: in this lesson each line of the words is a phrase, two bars long, where
  common practice often calls four bars a phrase, and two bars a phrase member (FACTS 24). Keep the
  convention?
- The swell, on "star", the line's last and only long note, 1.2 s of Merc's take 2: from half his
  usual level up to it and back, 6 dB, so it peaks at the level of the line's other notes and never
  above it. The singer's cue: "On "star", start soft, never a whisper, grow to about speaking
  volume, then soften again." This is the first Sing it cue in the series to ask for a soft start in
  volume; the warm-up's "a soft "oo"" is the only earlier sung "soft" given as a cue, and Lesson 5's
  "a soft "h"" is an onset its Step 4 teaches. The alternative is to leave the swell out of the
  singer's take and keep it only in Merc's (FACTS 38, 83).
- The breath mark: "usually sits at a phrase's end" and "takes its time from the note before it"
  (Step 2, quiz question 2, the Wrap's first line) rest on one source, an encyclopedia article; a
  music dictionary and a notation handbook bear out its place above the staff and the breath, and
  say neither (FACTS 26). Do you agree with both, as a singer's rule of thumb?
- The breath: take 2's cue, "breathe before you start, sing through to "star", and breathe after
  it", a breath before and after 6 s of singing, and nothing asks for a longer breath or a longer
  note (FACTS 72).
- Move do moves do by up to four semitones each way. From the default, D3 or D4, the phrase's do to
  la sits inside every preset of its voice with at least two semitones to spare at each end. Within
  the four, it can sit in the middle half of the bass, baritone, alto and mezzo-soprano presets; for
  the tenor and soprano presets only the top position, +4, reaches it (FACTS 69, 70).
- Is D3 (or D4) the right default do? The phrase from D3 runs to B3, and Lesson 7's lit middle on
  its A2 to A4 column runs from D♯3 to D♯4: the bottom note sits a semitone under it (FACTS 71).

### The checkpoint: dose, takes and recording

- The dose: Merc's phrase, then the singer's take 1 and take 2, each 6.0 s after a count-in on do;
  one more take 2 ("Again") only after "Easy" or "Workable", once a visit; never a take after
  "Effortful" or "Uncomfortable"; at most three takes a visit (FACTS 72).
- The recording: each take is recorded on the device, in memory only, and gone on Delete, on a new
  take in its place (Again, "a new take 2"), or when the page is left; nothing is stored or sent. It
  is recorded with the browser's echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control
  off, and played back through one gain for the whole take, its peak at the same level for every
  take, never through a compressor or an automatic gain, so a swell keeps its shape and "Did "star"
  grow and fade?" can be heard in it (FACTS 51, 52).
- The lines: one a take, the range the line ran over, in note names, with Lesson 3's octave line
  where it applies, or "We didn't hear the phrase that time."; no count and no score (FACTS 53).
- The playback prompt, "Play them back: where did each breathe? Did "star" grow and fade?", a
  question for the singer, never scored (FACTS 48).

### The feelings and the Wrap

- "How did that feel?" after each take with something sung. After "Effortful" there is no further
  take and the Wrap shows the rest card, as Lessons 6, 7 and 9 specify; after "Uncomfortable" the
  singing stops, the lines go, the doctor lines show and the Wrap shows the rest card. Either way
  the takes already made stay, to play back or delete, since listening asks nothing of the voice
  (FACTS 73, 74).
- The practice card: Karaoke Night, a whole song with the singer's own take played back, which
  scores; its key tools, so a song can sit in the comfortable middle; and the card's care lines
  (FACTS 54).
- The season's closing card: after "Effortful", "Uncomfortable" or "Just listen today" it leaves out
  "Now sing a whole song." and its Karaoke Night button, and keeps "You've seen every note you
  sing." and the season map, a kit change the page waits for. The rest card names Karaoke Night for
  when the voice feels comfortable, as Lesson 5's names Voice Mirror (FACTS 77, 78).

### Proposed, not applied: the four answers to "How did that feel?"

As in the lists of Lessons 3, 5, 7 and 9, the plain-language review proposes [ Easy ] [ OK ] [ Hard
work ] [ Uncomfortable ] in place of [ Easy ] [ Workable ] [ Effortful ] [ Uncomfortable ]. Here the
answer decides whether take 2 and Again are offered, so a word a learner guesses at can misreport
how the singing felt. The labels stay: REQ-GVC-022 names Easy, Workable, Effortful and
Uncomfortable, so they change only if the spec does, for the app's Guided Voice Check and every
lesson at once (FACTS 73).
