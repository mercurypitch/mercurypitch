## Four words you'll meet

- **Harmonic**: one of the frequencies inside a sung note, at 1, 2, 3 or more times the note's own.
  [Play: the 1st harmonic of A3, then the 2nd]
- **Spectrum**: a note's recipe: how loud each of its harmonics is. Here, a row of bars. [Show:
  the strip lights]
- **Resonance**: a frequency that your throat and mouth pass on most strongly. Their shape sets
  where it falls. [Show: a curve over the strip, peaking at one resonance]
- **Formant**: a peak in the spectrum, where a resonance boosts the harmonics near it. [Show:
  the two peaks of ‘ee’ marked]

## Step 1 of 5: What's inside one note?

A sung note isn't one frequency. Your vocal folds vibrate at the note's frequency (Lesson 3). The
sound they make holds a whole stack of harmonics, at 1, 2, 3 and more times that frequency. Merc's
A3 is 220 Hz, so its harmonics are 220, 440 and 660 Hz, and on up. The pitch you hear comes mostly
from their spacing. How loud each one is shapes the timbre: whatever makes two sounds differ when
their pitch and loudness are the same.

**Try:** press Add a harmonic a few times, then All.
**Notice:** each new harmonic stands out as it joins, but the spacing stays 220 Hz, so the note
stays A3, and so does the line.

_Toy: the strip and the line, with Add a harmonic and All._

## Step 2 of 5: Then what makes ‘ee’ different from ‘ah’?

Your throat and mouth, the vocal tract, sit between your vocal folds and the air. They act as a
filter: some frequencies pass through boosted, others come out weaker. Each shape of the tract has
its own resonances, the frequencies it passes on most strongly. The harmonics nearest them come out
boosted, as peaks called formants. ‘Ee’ and ‘ah’ are two shapes, so their peaks fall on different
bars. In English, listeners tell vowels apart mainly by the first two.

**Try:** turn on Resonances, then play ‘ee’ and ‘ah’.
**Notice:** each peak sits under a resonance's curve. The curves move from vowel to vowel. The bars
don't.

_Toy: the strip with Resonances, ‘ee’ and ‘ah’._

> **Go deeper: high notes and words.** On a high enough note, the note itself can rise past the
> first resonance. In one study, eight classically trained sopranos, near the top of their range,
> raised that resonance to match the note they sang. The researchers say this makes the voice
> louder and more even, at the cost of the words: the vowels crowd together. It helps explain why
> words sung high by sopranos are hard to make out.

## Step 3 of 5: What moves a peak?

Two moves do most of it. Opening your mouth wider raises the first resonance, so the first peak can
move to a higher harmonic. Where your tongue narrows the mouth, near the front for ‘ee’ and further
back for ‘oo’, moves the second resonance most. Rounding or spreading your lips changes them too.
Your jaw, tongue and lips make the vowel. The note stays with your vocal folds.

**Try:** shape ‘ee’, then ‘ah’, without making a sound, and feel your mouth open. Then play ‘ee’
and ‘oo’.
**Notice:** from ‘ee’ to ‘oo’, the first peak stays on the 1st bar, and the second drops from the
10th to the 5th.

_Toy: the strip with ‘ee’, ‘ah’ and ‘oo’._

## Step 4 of 5: Are five vowels five different notes?

No. Sung on one note, ‘ee’, ‘eh’, ‘ah’, ‘oh’ and ‘oo’ all have the note's harmonics, at the same
frequencies. So on Merc's strip the bars stay put, and Merc's line stays level. Only the bars'
heights change. Your vocal folds set the note and your vocal tract shapes the vowel, and you can
often move each one almost without the other. In speech, too, you can usually swap one word in a
sentence and keep its tune.

**Try:** play the five vowels on one note.
**Notice:** the line stays level, while the tallest bars change.

> **Common mix-up:** "Different vowels are different notes."
> **Why it seems right:** vowels really do sound different. And in speech, ‘ee’ and ‘oo’ tend to
> come out higher in pitch than ‘ah’, in many languages.
> **The evidence:** a singer can hold one note at one loudness while the mouth opens and the tongue
> moves. That makes most of English's vowels. On one note, every vowel keeps the same harmonics;
> only their loudness changes.
> **The better idea:** a vowel is a shape, not a note. Keep the note level, and let only the vowel
> change.

_Toy: the strip and the line, the five vowels in turn._

## Step 5 of 5: Does everyone's ‘ah’ have the same peaks?

No, and that's one reason nothing here checks your vowel. A vocal tract is typically 15 to 20
centimetres long, a bit shorter in women and children. In a simple tube, a shorter length resonates
higher. One researcher measured the vowels of ten British broadcasters. The five women's average
‘ah’ had its first two peaks at about 910 and 1,320 Hz. The five men's had them at about 650 and
1,160 Hz. Merc's lower voice is modelled on those men, and the higher voice on those women.

**Try:** switch between Lower and Higher on ‘ah’.
**Notice:** the higher voice's bars are twice as far apart, and its tallest bar is the 2nd, not
the 3rd.

_Toy: the strip, ‘ah’, with Lower and Higher._

## Wrap

- A sung note is a stack of harmonics, and its pitch comes mostly from their spacing.
- Your throat and mouth boost the harmonics near their resonances. Those peaks, the formants, are
  mainly how a listener tells one vowel from another.
- A new vowel moves the peaks, not the note: keep the note level and let your mouth do the rest.
