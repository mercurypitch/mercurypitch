12. "Ng" is the sound of "ng" in "sing": the back of the tongue at the soft palate, the back of the roof of the mouth, closes the mouth's way out, and the sound goes through the nose | Wikipedia, Voiced velar nasal, lead ("It is the sound of ng in English sing"); § Features ("Its manner of articulation is occlusive, which means it is produced by obstructing airflow in the vocal tract. ## Unsupported, not used

- "A warm-up protects the voice": the app's warm-up help says so (76), a scoping review's
  conclusion says that "Evidence-based vocal exercises enhance vocal health and performance in
  trained singers" (Riquelme-Muñoz 2025, abstract; no protocol registration), and Wikipedia's
  Voice type article ties misclassification to damage (§ Classifying singers, citing books not
  read). Health claims are out of bounds for a lesson (TEACHING-PLAYBOOK house rules), and the
  other reviews read (16 to 18) do not support them. Not used.
- "Singing at the extremes, or above your best tessitura, causes vocal disorders": Wikipedia,
  Voice type, § Classifying singers, citing Cooper and Appelman, neither read. A health claim; not
  used.
- "A singer's ideal tessitura is centered on the single pitch in which their dynamic range is
  greatest": Wikipedia, Tessitura, citing Titze (2008), _Journal of Singing_ 65, 59–61, not read.
  Not used; the page's reason for the middle rests on Solomon 2007 (30).
- "Most people have medium voices": Wikipedia, Voice type (citing McKinney 1994, not read). Not
  used.
- William Vennard on not classifying a beginner too soon ("It is best to begin in the middle part
  of the voice and work upward and downward until the voice classifies itself."): quoted in
  Wikipedia, Voice type, from his 1967 book, not read. Not used.
- How many notes a whole voice can reach, from its lowest sound to its highest: Hollien, Dew and
  Philips (1971) and Colton and Hollien (1972) have no abstracts on PubMed and were not read. The
  page gives no figure for a whole voice, only the books' typical spans (23).
- "Training extends your range", or "lip trills widen your range": not supported by anything read,
  and the Ascent's week 3 says so (79); out of bounds for this lesson. A study of the voice range
  profile straight after lip trills (Brockmann-Bauser and colleagues, 2020, PMID 31196690) was
  seen only by title. Not used.
- "Your range depends on the time of day": van Mersbergen, Verdolini and Titze (1999) found no
  clear time-of-day effect in 20 untrained women, while Gelfer 1989 found variation within a day
  (26). The page describes Gelfer's design, three recordings a day on two days a month or two
  apart, and says the measures varied across recordings, never that the hour causes it.
- "The measured range depends on how it is asked for": Reich and colleagues (1990) found glides
  and stepped tasks gave larger ranges than discrete steps in 40 adults, and Zraick and colleagues
  (2000) found no task effect in 30 women. Mixed; not used.
- "Coaching and repeated tries raise the measured maximum range": Ma and Li (2017), in 30 girls
  aged 6 to 11. Read; not used, because the lesson never asks for a maximum.
- "Warming up raises phonation threshold pressure at high pitch" (Motel 2003, ten sopranos): read
  for its definition only (30); its finding is not used.
- The exercise-science view of warm-ups (Hoch and Sandage 2018): read; not used.
- "A good singer has the widest range" as a measured belief: no data. It is voiced only as Tavi's
  question, and the reveal calls "most of it" a fair guess for a reason on the page (28).
- The code comment that a beginner "strains at the top long before the bottom" (80): a product
  call, not a measurement. Not used.
- How often a gentle siren crosses a register change, or where: not read. The page's register line
  is conditional ("If your voice flips on the way up").
- How fast real lips buzz in a lip trill, and how a nose filters "ng": not read. The synthesised
  sounds' 22 Hz wobble and 900 Hz lowpass are the page's choices (82).
- Lycke 2013 (45): read and supported, cut for length.
