## Four words you'll meet

- **Range**: the span from your lowest note to your highest. [Show: the column's ends light]
- **Siren**: one smooth glide up from a low note and back down. [Play: Merc's siren]
- **Lip trill**: singing through loose lips that buzz, a "brrr". [Play: a siren on a lip trill]
- **Voice type**: a name for a kind of voice, such as soprano, alto, tenor or bass. [Show: the
  six names]

## Step 1 of 5: What does a siren do?

For a higher note, your vocal folds stretch longer and tighter (Lesson 3). A siren does that
smoothly: one glide up from a low note and back down, sliding, never stepping. On the pitch line it
draws a hill. When you sing one, keep it gentle, at about speaking volume, and go only as high as
is easy.

Lesson 3's care lines still apply: stop if your voice feels scratchy or hoarse.

**Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along.** Every step works without singing.

**Try:** play Merc's siren, then play it slowly.
**Notice:** the line has no steps, and turns round inside the lit middle, well short of the
column's ends.

_Toy: the range chart, Merc's siren sweeping across it._

## Step 2 of 5: Why lip trills and "ng"?

A lip trill sends the siren through loose, buzzing lips. "Ng" is the sound at the end of "sing":
the back of your tongue touches the soft palate, at the back of the roof of your mouth, so the sound
leaves through your nose. Both partly close the sound's way out. Voice teachers and therapists have
long used them, and in a computer model, partly closed lips raised the air pressure above the vocal
folds. Do they help singers? Studies that test singers are still few.

**Try:** play Merc's siren on "ng", on a lip trill and on "oo". Then, if you like, siren along
once, quietly, on the easiest. Stop immediately if anything feels uncomfortable.
**Notice:** one line for all three: the siren is the shape; the sound is your choice.

_Toy: the range chart, one siren in three sounds._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **The evidence.** The model is Ingo Titze's (2006). A review of voice training for singers
>   found eight studies, four with exercises like these: most had no comparison group, and most
>   results were the singers' own ratings, mostly positive (Carvalho and colleagues, 2026). In
>   healthy voices, many studies look mainly at what changes straight away (Oliveira and
>   colleagues, 2024).

## Step 3 of 5: How many notes can my voice reach?

Your range runs from your lowest note to your highest. Books often give a voice type two octaves,
24 semitones, and some voices reach further: these spans are a guide, not a rule. And a range can
move. In one study, adults were recorded three times a day, on two days a month or two apart. For
the average adult, the lowest note, highest note and range each varied by several semitones. A song
needs only part of it.

**Try:** slide "Twinkle" up and down the column, playing it as you go.
**Notice:** it fits in many places, but in the lit middle it has room to spare at both ends.

_Toy: the range chart, a voice's column with "Twinkle" and Slide._

## Step 4 of 5: So why is the middle worth more?

Because a note there may need the least push. In a small study, eight adults with untrained voices
sang their quietest note at ten pitches across their range. The air pressure each note needed seemed
least near a comfortable pitch, and higher towards the top. More push also makes a note louder and a
little higher (Lesson 5). Singers call that comfortable middle, where singing feels easiest, your
tessitura.

**Try:** press and hold Higher: the note climbs the column, bottom to top.
**Notice:** the push is lowest a third of the way up, and rises most towards the top.

> **Common mix-up:** "A good singer has the widest range."
> **Why it seems right:** a range is one number, easy to count and compare.
> **The evidence:** "Twinkle" spans 9 of a two-octave voice's 24 semitones. In one small study, a
> note needed the least push near a comfortable pitch. And teachers place solo singers in a voice
> type more by where their voices sit comfortably than by how far they reach.
> **The better idea:** a song asks for a slice of your voice, sung with ease. Put the slice in
> your comfortable middle.

_Toy: the range chart with the push gauge._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Pulled towards the middle.** In one study, singers who sang off tended to sing notes above
>   their comfortable note flat, too low, and notes below it sharp, too high (Pfordresher and
>   Brown, 2007).

## Step 5 of 5: So am I a tenor, or an alto?

**Predict:** two singers have the same range. Same voice type?
[ Yes, the same type ] [ Not always ]

Reveal: Not always. Teachers also listen for where a voice is most comfortable, its colour (how it
sounds) and where it changes gear (Lesson 11). Two singers who both reach from A3 to C6 could be a
soprano, comfortable high, and a mezzo-soprano, comfortable lower. (A range is easy to compare, so
"yes" was a fair guess.)

A voice type is a label, not a box: the spans overlap, and books draw them differently. In
MercuryPitch, the voice type in Settings just picks the notes drills use; change it freely.

**Try:** switch between the two singers, then show all six types.
**Notice:** one range, two comfortable middles, two labels.

_Toy: the range chart, two singers and the six voice-type bands._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Partly built in.** In X-rays of 132 singers, sopranos' vocal folds came out the shortest on
>   average and basses' the longest (Roers and colleagues, 2009). Yet even scans of the throat
>   picked out sopranos and altos correctly only about seven times in ten (Rast and
>   colleagues, 2023).

## Wrap

- A siren is one smooth glide up and back down. Warm up on "ng" or a lip trill, gently, at about
  speaking volume; studies of whether they help singers are still few.
- A range can move, and a song uses only a slice of it: put the slice in your comfortable middle,
  where a note may need the least push.
- A voice type is a label, not a box: teachers listen for where a voice sits comfortably, not only
  how far it reaches.

### Proposed, not applied: the four answers to "How did that feel?"

As in Lesson 3's and Lesson 5's lists, the review proposes [ Easy ] [ OK ] [ Hard work ]
[ Uncomfortable ] in place of the current [ Easy ] [ Workable ] [ Effortful ] [ Uncomfortable ].
"Workable" and "Effortful" are rare words for a teen, and here the answer decides whether Again is
offered ("Effortful" ends the sirens for the visit), so a learner who guesses at a word can
misreport how the singing felt. "Hard work" is the comfort check's own phrase. The four answers,
their order and what each one does would stay the same. The page keeps the current labels until the
coach decides, and the decision covers Lessons 3, 5 and 6 and the app's Guided Voice Check too,
which use the same four.
