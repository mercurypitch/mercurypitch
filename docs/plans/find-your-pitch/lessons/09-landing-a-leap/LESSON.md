## Four words you'll meet

- **Leap**: a move that skips at least one letter, like do up to so (Lesson 6). [Play: do up to
  so]
- **Third**: three letters, both ends counted. A major third is 4 semitones, a minor third 3. [Play:
  do up to mi, then mi up to so]
- **Arpeggio**: a chord's notes sung one at a time, such as do, mi, so and the top do. [Play:
  do-mi-so-do]
- **Landing zone**: the band around the note a leap aims for, 35 cents either side (Lesson 3).
  [Show: so's band lights]

## Step 1 of 5: Why is a leap harder than a step?

A step goes to the next letter, right beside the note you're on. A leap skips the notes between.
For a higher note, your vocal folds stretch longer and tighter (Lesson 3). A step asks for a little
of that, and a leap for a lot, all in one move. And in one study, hundreds of people in the United
States and India passed short tunes along by singing them. Over many passes, the tunes drifted
towards small intervals.

**Try:** play the step, then the leap. Under the chart, watch the folds, slowed 220 times.
**Notice:** the step makes the folds about an eighth faster. The leap to so makes them one and a
half times as fast.

_Toy: the ladder chart with Step and Leap, and the slowed folds under it._

## Step 2 of 5: Can I aim a leap, then?

Yes: leaps tend to miss the same way. In one study, students with no music training copied leaps
of a major third, a fourth and a fifth. Even the accurate singers landed them short, by 50 to 100
cents on average: half a semitone to a semitone. Those who sang off landed further short. Singing a
note back means planning it from the note you expect to hear, so hear the landing note first. Then
leap, land, listen and adjust: Lesson 3's loop, with a jump in it.

**Try:** press Hear it first, then Leap.
**Notice:** the violet line, an illustration of a typical copy, lands under so's band. While you
hear so first, its band lights: aim there.

> **Common mix-up:** "You jump to the top note and hope."
> **Why it seems right:** a leap is quick, and it skips every note on the way.
> **The evidence:** in one study, copied leaps tended to come out short, not just anywhere. In
> another, students who said they imagine sounds vividly tended to sing more of their notes in
> tune.
> **The better idea:** hear the landing note first, aim at its band, then check where you landed
> and adjust.

_Toy: the ladder chart with an illustrated copy, Hear it first and Leap._

## Step 3 of 5: What's the ladder for?

Do, mi and so make a major chord: three notes that can sound at once. Sung one at a time, up to the
top do, they make an arpeggio: a ladder. Do to mi is a major third, mi to so a minor third:
together, the perfect fifth from do to so. So to the top do is a perfect fourth, and do to do the
octave. Exams use it too. Before a singer sings a new tune from the page, the Royal Conservatory of
Music's examiners play do, mi and so, then the tune's first note.

**Try:** press Climb, then Leap to so and Leap to the top do.
**Notice:** each leap lands on a rung the climb touched.

_Toy: the ladder chart, every rung, with Climb and two leaps._

## Step 4 of 5: Which leap first?

Small ones, and slowly. The Royal Conservatory's voice exams ask for minor and major thirds first.
They add the fifth, then the fourth, then the octave, one level at a time. Teachers of the Kodály
method, a Hungarian way of teaching music, found that children struggled to sing semitones in tune
and to manage a wide range. The method's revised exercises start with so and mi alone, a minor
third. Slow helps too: in one study of "Happy Birthday", occasional singers' intervals came out
more accurate the slower they sang.

**Try:** play the five leaps in that order, with Slow on, then off.
**Notice:** slowed, each arc takes twice as long, so you can follow it into its band.

_Toy: the ladder chart, the five leaps in order, with Slow._

## Step 5 of 5: How do I know I landed?

Your landing zone is Lesson 3's band, 35 cents either side of the note. Land, listen and compare:
under the band? Slide up into it. An octave up or down still counts, as in Lesson 3. The octave is
the ladder's biggest leap, and it can wait. If the top do feels high, sing the smaller ladder,
do-mi-so-mi-do. It's the first chord the Royal Conservatory's voice exams ask singers to sing.

**Try:** press Play to watch a leap land 80 cents short, then adjust.
**Notice:** 80 cents short, the nearest note is the one just below so. Inside the band, the readout
says "in the band".

_Toy: the ladder chart, a landing and an adjustment, with Play, Slow and scrub._

## Wrap

  its band, then adjust.
- An arpeggio is a ladder. Do-mi-so-do climbs a major third and a minor third to so, then a
  perfect fourth to the top do. It holds the fifth and the octave too.

### The ladders and the range

- The full ladder, do-mi-so-do, from D3 to D4 (lower voice) or D4 to D5 (higher): the CURRICULUM's
  checkpoint and Arpeggio Jumper's major arpeggio. The smaller, do-mi-so-mi-do, from D3 up to A3 and
  back (or an octave up): the Royal Conservatory's first triad, sung on one slur. Each note 1.5 s,
  on one breath: 6 s for the full ladder and 7.5 s for the smaller (FACTS 38, 39, 73, 76).
- Move do moves do by up to four semitones each way, which can set the full ladder exactly in the
  middle half of any of the app's six voice presets (FACTS 75).
- Is D3 (or D4) the right default do? The full ladder from D3 sits inside every lower preset with
  at least two semitones to spare at each end, but its bottom is a semitone under the lit middle of
  Lesson 7's column; from E♭3 it would fill that middle exactly but come within a semitone of the
  bass preset's top (FACTS 74).
- The smaller ladder is offered to anyone the top do would make work hard, and the page never moves
  a singer from the smaller ladder to the full one within a visit; Again after the full ladder may
  take the smaller one instead (FACTS 77).

- "How did that feel?" and its endings. After "Effortful" there is no further ladder and the Wrap
