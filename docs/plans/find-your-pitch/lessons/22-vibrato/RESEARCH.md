## Where the sources and the card disagree (read first)

1. **Extent: use Sundberg's definition, not Timmers & Desain's range.** The card plans "extent about
   0.6 to 2 semitones" from Timmers & Desain (2000). That paper does not say whether its range is
   peak to peak or either side of the centre (R2). Sundberg's review, read in full, defines extent
   as how far the pitch departs up and down from its average, written with ±, and gives "generally
   narrower than ±1 semitone" (R1, R3). Read that way, Timmers & Desain's 2 semitones is likely the
   whole swing, but that is an inference. The plan quotes Sundberg: rate about 5 to 8 waves a
   second, extent usually under a semitone either side of the centre.
2. **Shonle & Horan (1980) is now read to the abstract on the publisher's page, plus a quoted
   conclusion in a paper read in full** (R5). The card's "geometric mean of its two extremes" holds.
   Their widths were totals ("modulation widths up to a total of 200 cents"), so their largest
   vibrato was ±100 cents.
3. **Bretos & Sundberg was read in full in its report version** (TMH-QPSR 2002), not the Journal of
   Voice version the card cites (R8). The finding the card names (rate rises towards the end of long
   tones) is there, with numbers.
4. **Mitchell & Kenny (2010) and Nix (2014) could not be read past an abstract or a summary** (R9,
   R10). Nothing from either is planned. The card's "singers move between vibrato and a straight
   tone by style" now rests on a systematic review read in full (Acosta Martínez & Daffern 2025,
   R11), which also says the empirical evidence on choirs is too mixed for general conclusions.
5. **The card's sample report, "about 50 cents either side of its centre", is wider than the band.**
   Lesson 5's band reaches 35 cents either side of the note (Lesson 5 FACTS 43), and Lesson 5 told
   the learner "a small vibrato ... can sit inside the band" (lessons/05-breath-steady-note/
   LESSON.md:183-184). A ±50-cent wave crosses both edges while its centre stays in. The plan keeps
   the example on purpose and makes the crossing the point of Step 3, but it changes the checkpoint's
   engineering (R17): the kit's hold reader counts every frame inside ±35 cents, so it would score
   that wave as partly out of the band today.
6. **The card's Long Note line is Long Note Lantern's.** "A vibrato counts as a wobble, so a
   straight tone scores highest" is in `exercise-help.ts:43`, inside the `EXERCISE_LONG_NOTE_LANTERN`
   entry (:37-45). Long Note's own help (:28-35) says "less wobble is better" and does not name
   vibrato (R15).

## Claims

### What a vibrato is, and its two measures

**R1. A vibrato is a regular, roughly sinusoidal wave in a held note's pitch, described by its rate
(waves a second) and its extent (how far the pitch moves up and down from its average).**
- Source: Sundberg, J. (1994). Acoustic and psychoacoustic aspects of vocal vibrato. STL-QPSR
  35(2-3), 45-68, KTH Stockholm. https://www.speech.kth.se/qpsr/1994/1994_35_2-3_045-068.pdf.
  Reprinted in Dejonckere, Hirano & Sundberg (eds.), Vibrato (1995), pp. 35-62 (the book not read).
- Says: p. 48, "the vibrato physically corresponds to a periodic, rather sinusoidal, modulation of
  the phonation frequency"; it "can be described in terms of four parameters ... the rate, the
  extent, the regularity, and the waveform"; "The vibrato rate specifies the number of undulations
  per second while the extent describes how far phonation frequency departs up and down from its
  average during a vibrato cycle. Often the extent is preceded by plus and minus signs".
- Read: in full. Strength: Moderate (an expert review of many measurement studies).

**R2. Timmers & Desain give singers' rate as 5.5 to 8 Hz and extent as 0.6 to 2 semitones, without
saying how extent is measured.**
- Source: Timmers, R. & Desain, P. (2000). Vibrato: questions and answers from musicians and
  science. Proceedings of the Sixth ICMPC, Keele University (CD-ROM).
  https://www.mcg.uva.nl/mmm-2003/papers/mmm-35/mmm-35.html.
- Says: Introduction, "rate between 5.5-8 Hz, extent between 0.6-2 semitones for singers and between
  0.2-0.35 semitones for string players, see Seashore, 1938; Sundberg, 1987; Meyer, 1992". No
  definition of extent for these figures; its own analysis measures half-cycles "between subsequent
  local maxima and minima".
- Read: in full. Strength: Moderate (secondary summary of three sources). Not used for a number;
  superseded by R3 for the definition.

**R3. Typical singers' vibratos run at about 5 to 8 waves a second, with an extent of less than a
semitone either side of the centre.**
- Source: Sundberg 1994 (R1).
- Says: p. 57, "the main acoustic characteristic of the vibrato is an undulation of the fundamental
  frequency ranging from 5 to 8 Hz in rate and less than ±1 semitone in extent" (the scan prints
  "+1"); p. 51, "the extent is generally narrower than ±1 semitones which corresponds to a frequency
  swing of ±6%, approximately".
- Read: in full. Strength: Moderate. The review focuses on Western operatic singing (p. 45); the page
  says "measured in trained singers", not "everyone".

**R4. Measured mean rates sit near 5.5 to 7 Hz: 5.4 Hz (men) and 5.9 Hz (women) for sustained
vowels in the lab, 6.6 Hz across 29 recorded artists, 6.1 Hz across ten singers on record.**
- Sources: Sundberg 1994, p. 50 (Shipp, Leanderson & Sundberg 1980: "The average vibrato rate found
  was 5.4 Hz for the male singers and 5.9 Hz for the female singers"; "Seashore (1938), analysing
  recordings of 29 artists, arrived at an overall average of 6.6 Hz"). Prame, E. (1992).
  Measurements of the vibrato rate of ten singers. STL-QPSR 33(4), 73-86,
  https://www.speech.kth.se/qpsr/1992/1992_33_4_073-086.pdf, p. 73 abstract: "Disregarding the
  increase of vibrato rate toward tone endings, the mean rate across singers was 6.1 Hz."
- Read: Sundberg in full; Prame to the abstract and introduction. Strength: Moderate.
- Use: one line in Step 2, "measured singers average about six waves a second". Go deeper may name
  the 29 recorded artists.

### What a listener hears

**R5. A long vibrato tone is heard as one pitch near the middle of its swing: close to the
geometric mean of its two extreme frequencies.**
- Source: Shonle, J. I. & Horan, K. E. (1980). The pitch of vibrato tones. JASA 67(1), 246-252.
  https://doi.org/10.1121/1.383733.
- Says: abstract (publisher's page, pubs.aip.org): test tones at 220, 440, 880 and 1500 Hz, vibrato
  widths of 50, 100 and 200 cents, rates of 4, 6 and 8 Hz; "The pitch is close to the geometric mean
  of the two extreme frequencies"; a second experiment with an asymmetrical waveform pointed to an
  "averaging of all frequencies present". Their conclusion, as quoted by d'Alessandro & Castellengo
  (1994, JASA 95(3), 1617-1630, p. 1617, read in full from the HAL copy, hal-02008510): "the pitch
  of vibrato tones for the frequency range 220 to 1500 Hz, with modulation widths up to a total of
  200 Cents, is the mean pitch, with the geometric mean being a somewhat better fit than the
  arithmetic."
- Corroborated: Sundberg 1994, p. 58: listeners tuned a straight tone "within a few cents, with the
  linear average of the vibrato tone's undulating fundamental frequency" (Sundberg 1977, synthetic
  sung tones); "Essentially the same result was found by Shonle & Horan (1980) and Iwamiya et al.
  (1983)"; "For typical vibrato extents the difference between these averages is small."
- Read: Shonle & Horan to the abstract plus two secondary accounts read in full. Strength: Moderate
  (three experiments agree). "Geometric mean" is said once, in Go deeper; the step says "the middle
  of the swing".

**R6. The vibrato does not make the pitch harder to judge: listeners matched vibrato tones as
accurately as straight ones.**
- Source: Sundberg 1994, p. 58-59: "The subjects' matchings of the pitch of the same stimulus tones
  were almost entirely equally accurate, regardless of the presence of the vibrato."
- Read: in full (a review reporting the author's 1977 experiment). Strength: Weak to Moderate (one
  experiment, synthetic tones, musically trained listeners). Used in the misconception card's
  evidence.

**R7. Slow the wave down and it stops being one note: near 4 waves a second and below, the pitch
is heard to move.**
- Sources: Sundberg 1994, p. 59: "the pitch of a vibrato tone remains constant only if the vibrato
  rate is not too slow; for rates near 4 Hz and lower, the pitch is clearly undulating."
  d'Alessandro & Castellengo 1994, p. 1626 (citing Miller & Heise 1950 and Shonle & Horan 1976):
  "fusion tends to fail when the vibrato rate comes below 5 Hz, and when the vibrato extent comes
  above 200 cents, in the 500-Hz frequency region."
- Read: both in full. Strength: Moderate. Use: Step 3's second predict (2 waves a second, heard as
  a moving note), a clear case well below either threshold. The page names no exact threshold.

### Rate and extent change within a note

**R8. In recordings, the rate speeds up towards the end of a held note, and the extent can grow
over it.**
- Sources: Prame 1992, p. 73 (R4): "the vibrato rate typically increased at the end of each tone".
  Bretos, J. & Sundberg, J. (2002). Measurements of vibrato parameters in long sustained crescendo
  notes as sung by ten sopranos. TMH-QPSR 43(1), 37-44,
  https://www.speech.kth.se/qpsr/2002/2002_43_1_037-044.pdf (published as J Voice 17(3), 343-352,
  2003, not read). F5 and A5 from "O patria mia" in ten commercial recordings of Aida, tones 3.5 to
  7.5 s (p. 38). p. 40: mean rate "6.2 Hz (SD 0.45) and 6.3 Hz (SD 0.70)" for F5 and A5. p. 42: "on
  average, VR was increased by about 20% at the end of these tones. Thus, if the mean VR at a
  distance of 10 vibrato cycles from the end of the tone was 6.0 Hz, it was 7.2 Hz for the final
  vibrato cycle"; extent increased over time, "between 17 and 80 cents".
- Read: Bretos & Sundberg's report in full; Prame to the abstract. Strength: Weak to Moderate (two
  small studies of famous recordings, one aria and one song). Use: Go deeper only.

**R9. Mitchell & Kenny (2010) followed 15 conservatorium singers over two years; extent rose and rate
variability fell.**
- Source: Mitchell, H. F. & Kenny, D. T. (2010). Change in vibrato rate and extent during tertiary
  training in classical singing students. J Voice 24(4), 427-434,
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2008.12.003.
- Says (abstract, Macquarie University's record): "VR was within expected limits for classical
  singers in all semesters"; "VE showed significant increases between the start of year 1 and year
  2"; "Periodicity of singers' VR (SD) improved over training".
- Read: abstract only; no open full text found. Strength: Weak. **Not used.** A training claim would
  also suggest that a straight note is unfinished, against the card's objective.

### Straight tone and vibrato

**R10. Nix (2014) offers studio and choir exercises for moving between vibrato and straight
singing.**
- Source: Nix, J. (2014). Shaken, not stirred: practical ideas for addressing vibrato and nonvibrato
  singing in the studio and the choral rehearsal. Journal of Singing 70(4), 411-418.
- Says: the NATS summary page only (members' text not reached): airflow "about 10% greater" with
  vibrato; a survey of 350 singers on who taught vibrato and non-vibrato.
- Read: summary only. **Not verified; not used.** Its exercises are a method, which the lesson does
  not give.

**R11. Singing uses both: choral and ensemble styles often ask for less vibrato or a straight tone,
and singers can change their vibrato to fit the group.**
- Source: Acosta Martínez, G. & Daffern, H. (2025). The role of vibrato in choral singing: a
  systematic review. J Voice 39(3), 847.e21-847.e39. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2022.11.004.
  Open access (CC BY-NC-ND) at https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/228375/.
- Says: p. 847.e21, quoting a choral article, "Experts offer a wide range of view points regarding
  vibrato in choral singing, from those who believe in a free, soloistic vibrato to those who
  advocate a minimal vibrato or even straight tone"; p. 847.e30, "Studies in the Adjustment category
  demonstrate that singers have the capability to change their vibrato behaviour. The degree to
  which this is a conscious or unconscious behaviour is still not clear"; quoting one study, "choral
  style favours minimal vibrato, and barbershop style generally forbids vibrato"; abstract
  conclusion, "it is not possible to draw general conclusions around vibrato behavior during group
  singing."
- Read: in full. Strength: Moderate for "both are used and singers adjust"; the review itself says
  the evidence is thin. The page says "styles differ", not which is better.

**R12. Choir singers' vibratos have been measured as small, about 0.1 semitone.**
- Source: Sundberg 1994, p. 51: "Choir singers show rather irregular vibratos with very small
  extents averaging to no more than 0.1 semitone."
- Read: in full. Strength: Weak (no study named on the page). Go deeper at most; not planned.

### What the plan deliberately does not claim

**R13. How a vibrato is produced, whether it can be trained, and anything about health.** Sundberg
1994, p. 45, links the operatic vibrato to "pulsating contractions of the cricothyroid muscle" and
says it "develops quasi automatically as voice training successfully proceeds"; the Vibrato
exercise's help calls one "healthy" (R15). None of this goes on the page: it is anatomy (playbook
rule 24), a training promise (SEASON-2 § 1.1, "never promises ... a vibrato on demand") or a
health word (house rules). Listed for the coach.

**R14. Whether a vibrato suits a style is the coach's line, not a fact.** Sundberg's limits for an
"acceptable vibrato" (p. 57: slower than 5 "tends to sound unacceptably slow", faster than 8 "tend
to sound nervous") are aesthetic judgements of operatic singing. Not used.

### Arithmetic

**R20. A ±50-cent wave on A3 swings between 213.74 and 226.45 Hz; the geometric mean of the two is
220 Hz exactly, and their plain average is 220.09 Hz, 0.72 cents higher.** 220 × 2^(±50/1200);
geometric mean √(213.74 × 226.45) = 220.00; arithmetic mean 220.09 Hz, 1200 × log2(1.000417) =
0.72 cents. On A4 every figure doubles. Run with `python3 -I`. This is why the page can say "the
middle of the swing" without choosing between the two averages (R5).

## Interesting findings

1. **Slow it down and the one note comes apart.** Below about 4 to 5 waves a second the ear hears
   the pitch go up and down; above it, one note (Sundberg 1994, p. 59; d'Alessandro & Castellengo
   1994, p. 1626). A rate slider makes it audible. Possible: Step 3's second predict.
2. **A wave glues sounds together.** In a sound example by John Chowning, a cluster of steady sine
   tones turns into "three voices singing a chord" the moment each group of tones starts to wave in
   its own synchrony (Sundberg 1994, pp. 60-61). Possible: Go deeper.
3. **A vibrato hides the beats.** Two notes a little out of tune beat; sung with vibrato, Sundberg
   writes, "no beats will occur", so the vibrato "gives the singer access to fine tuning as a means
   of musical expression" (1994, p. 64; the scan prints "tino tuning"). Barbershop, which "generally
   forbids vibrato", tunes its chords to remove beats (Acosta Martínez & Daffern 2025, p. 847.e30).
   Possible: wrap line, and Lesson 24's reason to hold the drone notes straight.
4. **The wave speeds up as a note ends.** In ten famous recordings of one Aida aria, the rate rose
   about 20 % over the last ten waves, from about 6.0 to 7.2 a second (Bretos & Sundberg 2002, p.
   42; Prame 1992 found the same in Schubert's Ave Maria). Possible: Go deeper.
5. **Merc has had a vibrato all along.** The kit's Merc "ooh", which the lesson pages play, carries
   a 5.5 Hz, ±14-cent wave, small enough to sit inside Lesson 5's band (R18). Which pages use it,
   and whether the films' Merc does, was not checked page by page. Possible: Step 2's first example.
