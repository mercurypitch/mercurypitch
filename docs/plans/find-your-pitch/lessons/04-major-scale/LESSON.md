## Four words you'll meet

- **Scale**: notes in order, climbing step by step from one note to the same letter an octave
  higher. [Play: C up to C]
- **Tonic**: a scale's first note, which is also its last: its home. [Play: C]
- **Scale degree**: a note's place in the scale, counted up from the tonic: 1, 2, 3 and on to 7.
  [Play: 1, 2, 3]
- **Solfège**: a sung name for each degree: do, re, mi, fa, so, la, ti. [Play: do, re, mi]

## Step 1 of 5: Count the steps

Play the white keys from C up to the next C and you hear a major scale: the do-re-mi you probably
know. Now measure the steps between neighbouring notes, as Lesson 2 did. Where a black key sits
between two white keys, the step is a tone. E to F and B to C have no black key between them, so
those two steps are semitones.

**Try:** play the scale, then tap each stair from the bottom.
**Notice:** the staircase is uneven: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. The two
short steps are E to F and B to C.

_Toy: the staircase, on C._

## Step 2 of 5: Two groups and a joint

Read the pattern in pieces. The first four notes climb tone, tone, semitone. The last four climb
tone, tone, semitone too. One tone joins the two groups. Count it in semitones: 2, 2, 1, then 2,
then 2, 2, 1. That's twelve, exactly one octave, which is why the scale ends on the note it began
with, one octave up. In the tuning MercuryPitch uses, that's 1,200 cents (Lesson 2). Books often
write the pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H, for whole and half steps.

**Try:** press Group, then tap each bracket to play its group.
**Notice:** the two groups have the same shape: C D E F, then G A B C.

_Toy: the staircase with Group._

## Step 3 of 5: Start anywhere

Tavi: "Same white keys, just starting on D. Surely that's the same scale?"

**Predict:** start on D and play only white keys, up to the next D. Is it still do-re-mi?
[ Yes: same keys, same scale ] [ No, it changes ]

(After a choice: Play white keys from D · Play the pattern from D. With sound off, the hint reads:
"Sound is off, so nothing played. Turn sound on to hear them, then press Play the pattern from
D.")

Reveal: It changes. From D, the white keys climb tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone, tone:
the short steps land in the wrong places, and the third and seventh notes come out a semitone low.
Keep the major pattern instead and you need two black keys, F♯ and C♯. That's do-re-mi from D,
called D major: a major scale is named after its first note.

> **Common mix-up:** "A scale is a fixed set of notes, and it starts on C."
> **Why it seems right:** the major scale on C is the only one you can play on the white keys
> alone, so it's the one a keyboard shows most plainly. And in one naming system, called fixed do,
> the note do is always C.
> **The evidence:** play the white keys from D to D. The steps come out in a different order, and
> it isn't do-re-mi.
> **The better idea:** a major scale is a pattern of steps, not a set of keys. Start the pattern on
> any of the twelve notes and you get a major scale.

**Try:** slide the staircase to start on D, then on any note you like.
**Notice:** the staircase never changes shape, and it still sounds like do-re-mi, just higher or
lower. Only the keys under it change: every start except C needs at least one black key.

_Toy: the staircase with Slide and White keys only._

## Step 4 of 5: Name the stairs

The first note of a scale is its tonic, its home. Musicians number the notes up from it, 1 to 7,
and sing them as do, re, mi, fa, so, la, ti, with do again at the top. (Many books, and
MercuryPitch's Ear Lab, write so as sol.) The names belong to the places in the scale, not to the
keys. In C major, do is C. Slide to D and do is D, re is E, mi is F♯. That's movable do: home moves,
and the names move with it. There's also a fixed do, in which do is always C; this lesson uses
movable do.

**Try:** switch the names between letters, numbers and do-re-mi, then slide the staircase.
**Notice:** the letters change as you slide. The numbers and the do-re-mi stay on their stairs.

_Toy: the staircase with the names switch._

## Step 5 of 5: Ti leans home

Back to the opening question. Ti is the seventh note, and it sits just a semitone below do: the
pattern's last step is a short one. Climb to ti and stop, and most listeners hear the scale waiting
for one more note. Take that step, and it lands home. Why is do home? When listeners are asked how
well each note finishes a scale, they rate do highest. That sense of home seems to be learned just
by hearing music (Go deeper, below). And whatever the start, ti sits just under home. It's called
the leading note (American books, and MercuryPitch's Ear Lab, say leading tone), because it's
thought of as leading to the tonic. It leads wherever home is: from D, ti is C♯, and it leans up to
D.

**Try:** press Stop on ti, wait a moment, then press Land on do. Slide the staircase and try again.
**Notice:** the last step is always a semitone, so ti always sits right under home.

_Toy: the staircase with Stop on ti and Land on do._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **An experiment on endings.** Listeners heard a C major scale, then one more note, and rated
>   how well that note completed it. Do came out on top, then mi and so, then the scale's other
>   four notes, and last the five notes outside the scale. The order was clearest for listeners
>   with musical training; for the others, how close a note sat in pitch counted for more
>   (Krumhansl and Shepard, 1979).
> - **Learned by listening.** Later studies found the same kind of order in children and adults,
>   with and without training. It was only sharper in musicians and in adults, which suggests it is
>   picked up just by hearing music (Janata and colleagues, 2003, summing up those studies). A
>   review of studies on tension and release, expectation and more came to the same view: some of
>   these responses come from listening, not from lessons (Bigand and Poulin-Charronnat, 2006).
> - **A drill for the pull.** MercuryPitch's Ear Lab has one, The Pull: a few chords set home, two
>   notes sound, and you say which one leans harder.

> Sore, hoarse or unwell today? Just listen along.

**Your turn:** play Merc's scale, then sing it with the guide, on the names do, re, mi or on "ooh".

## Wrap

- A major scale is a pattern of steps: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. That's
  two tone-tone-semitone groups, joined by a tone.
- Start the pattern on any note and you get a major scale. C is just the one on the white keys
  alone.
- Do is home, the tonic. Ti sits a semitone below it, and most listeners hear ti lean up to do.
