## The major scale

1. A scale is notes in order, step by step; a major scale starts and ends on the same letter, an octave apart (the words strip: "notes in order, climbing step by step from one note to the same letter an octave higher") | OMT 2e 1.12, opening: "A scale is an ordered collection of half and whole steps"; § Major Scales: "A major scale always starts and ends on notes of the same letter name, one octave apart" | fetched; quoted
2. A major scale climbs tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone; books write it W-W-H-W-W-W-H, for whole and half steps (Lesson 2 FACTS 11 and 12: a tone is a whole step, a semitone a half step) | OMT 2e 1.12, § Major Scales: "A major scale is an ordered collection of half- (abbreviated H) and whole steps (abbreviated W) in the following ascending succession: W-W-H-W-W-W-H"; Mount § 6.3: "Every major scale features the exact same pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H): W-W-H-W-W-W-H"; Hutchinson § 2.2 (the same pattern) | fetched; quoted. Mount § 6.3 adds that a "half step" needs consecutive letter names (B to C, never F to F♯); every step of a major scale joins consecutive letters (13), so here half step and semitone name the same steps, as Lesson 2 uses them
3. The pattern is two tone-tone-semitone groups joined by a tone: C D E F, then G A B C | Hutchinson § 2.2: "It is helpful to think of the pattern as consisting of two tetrachords. (A tetrachord is a four-note scale segment.) The lower tetrachord consists of the pattern whole step, whole step, half step. A whole step joins the lower tetrachord to the upper tetrachord. The upper tetrachord duplicates the pattern in the lower one: whole step, whole step, half step."; Mount § 6.3: "the major scale can be divided into two tetrachords, each of which follows the W-W-H pattern. The two tetrachords are themselves separated by a whole step. In C major, the lower tetrachord contains C, D, E, and F, while the upper contains G, A, B, and C."; Mount § 6.6: "Every major scale is built of two tetrachords separated by a whole step" | fetched; quoted. The page says "groups" and never "tetrachord"
4. The steps add up to 12 semitones, one octave, so the scale ends on its first note an octave up: 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 12 | arithmetic on 2; Mount § 6.3: "An octave is equal in size to twelve semitones"; Lesson 2 FACTS 1 and 3 | arithmetic; script-checked (scratchpad `verify.py`, run with `python3 -I`)
5. In the tuning MercuryPitch uses, the steps measure 200, 200, 100, 200, 200, 200 and 100 cents, 1,200 in all | Lesson 2 FACTS 22, 23 and 29a (a tone is 200 cents; every semitone is 100 cents in the tuning MercuryPitch uses) | arithmetic; scoped as Lesson 2 scopes it, never "in all music"
6. ## Tonic, scale degrees and names

16. All of the other notes in the scale are organized around this note."; Mount § 6.6: "It begins and ends with the most important pitch, the keynote (or tonic)" | fetched; quoted
17. "Home" is the series' word for the tonic (the CURRICULUM card: "feel the tonic as home"); the page backs it only with what listeners hear (23 to 26) and claims nothing more for the word | CURRICULUM § 2.1, Lesson 4 | plan
18. Scale degrees number the notes up from the tonic, 1 to 7, and the top note is 1 again (some write 8) | OMT 2e 1.12, § Scale Degrees, Solfège, and Scale-Degree Names: "Scale degrees are solmization syllables notated by Arabic numerals with carets above them. The first note of a scale is 1̂ and the numbers ascend until the last note of a scale, which is also 1̂ (although some instructors prefer 8̂)." | fetched; quoted (the carets are images on the page, restored here). The numbers switch shows 1 to 7, then 1 at the top
19. Solfège names the degrees do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti, with do again at the top; in movable do, do is the tonic of whatever scale is sung, so do moves with home; in fixed do, do is always C | OMT 2e 1.12, same section: "The syllables do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, and ti can be applied to the first seven notes of any major scale; ... The last note is do (1̂) because it is a repetition of the first note. Because do (1̂) changes depending on what the first note of a major scale is, this method of solfège is called movable do. This is in contrast to a fixed do solmization system, in which do (1̂) is always the pitch class C." | fetched; quoted. The page uses movable do and names fixed do twice: step 4, and the mix-up card's "why it seems right"

## How listeners hear it

23. Most listeners hear do, the tonic, as the note that finishes a major scale: stop on ti and the scale sounds unfinished, waiting for do | K&S 1979 (abstract): "Listeners rated test tones falling in the octave range from middle to high C according to how well each completed a diatonic C major scale played in an adjacent octave just before the final test tone. Ratings were well explained in terms of three factors. The factors were distance in pitch height from the context tones, octave equivalence, and the following hierarchy of tonal functions: tonic tone, other tones of the major triad chord, other tones of a diatonic scale, and the nondiatonic tones."; Janata 2003, p. 3, summing up probe-tone studies with "ascending and descending scales (Krumhansl & Shepard, 1979)" and other contexts: "Tones that serve prominent functions in the key of the context, such as the tonic or dominant, are judged to fit well with the context whereas other diatonic tones and non-diatonic tones are judged to fit more poorly."; Janata 2003, p. 4: "Tonal hierarchies have been measured using the probe tone rating technique across participants varying in age and degree of musical experience ... While the exact shapes of the profiles vary across studies, the basic features mentioned above are generally preserved. The relative differences among the tones are generally more pronounced in musicians than non-musicians, and in adults than children." | abstract and preprint read. K&S 1979 found the order clearest in musical listeners ("pitch height was more prominent for less musical listeners", abstract); Janata p. 4 is why the page still says "most": the basic order holds across age and experience, only weaker in non-musicians and children. Said as "most listeners", never "everyone" or "always": step 5, quiz question 3 and the Wrap. The promise says only that the viewer will "hear ti lean towards home", which is what most listeners hear, and gives no reason. The cold-open reveal claims no listening result; it describes what was played (Land on do finishes the scale on D) and the textbook framing (26)
    23a. Step 5's answer to "Why is do home?": "When listeners are asked how well each note finishes a scale, they rate do highest. That sense of home seems to be learned just by hearing music" | K&S 1979 (abstract, as 23: the tonic heads the order of how well each test tone completed the scale); Janata 2003, p. 4: "The existence of tonal hierarchies in non-expert groups suggests that much of our knowledge of tonal hierarchies is acquired automatically through repeated exposure to musical regularities"; Bigand 2006 (25) | abstract and preprint read; said with "seems", as Janata's "suggests" allows. The page claims no reason beyond these two and ti's place a semitone under home (22); "the nearer the note, the stronger the pull" is not claimed (Larson, under Unverified)
24.
