## The beat and the tempo

8. A beat is a pulse that comes again and again at regular intervals, the thing a listener taps or claps along to | OMT 2e 1.9, Key Takeaways: "A beat is a pulse in music that regularly recurs."; § Terminology: "it is easy to tap or clap along to this recording. What you are tapping along to is called a beat—a pulse in music that regularly recurs."; Ableton, "Beat and tempo": "a regular, repeating pulse that underlies a musical pattern. People tap their foot along with "the beat" in this context." | fetched; quoted; the four words' "Beat", Step 1 ("Tap your foot to a song and you're tapping its beat: a pulse that comes again and again, evenly spaced"; "the clicks are evenly spaced. That evenness is what makes them a pulse") and the Wrap
9. "A beat" can also mean a drum pattern, as against "the beat", the pulse under it | Ableton, "Beat and tempo": "People commonly use the word "beat" to mean "a pattern (or rhythm) played by drums." The thing you're making when you create and play patterns in these lessons is "a beat." But, confusingly, there's another use of the word "beat," which means "a regular, repeating pulse that underlies a musical pattern."" | fetched; quoted; Step 1's "People also say "a beat" for a drum pattern, but "the beat" is the pulse under it"
10. Tempo is how fast the beats come, measured in beats per minute, BPM | Ableton, "Beat and tempo": "The speed at which your patterns play back is called the tempo. Tempo is measured in beats per minute or BPM. ## Unsupported, not used

- "A march claps on 1 and 3": no source read says so, and Wikipedia's marches of Germany and Austria
  put the snare on the off-beats (25). The page's march pattern is the French marches'
  BOOM-whack-whack-whack, named "this march pattern", and claps on 1 and 3 are called the strong
  beats, never a march.
- "Most pop songs have a backbeat", or any share of songs: not stated, as the CURRICULUM asks (26).
  Ableton's "many styles" and Wikipedia's "a defining characteristic of rock'n'roll and ska music"
  were read; the page uses only Ableton's "many styles", as "many styles with four-beat bars".
- How a backbeat feels, or why it feels so: the Encyclopedia of Percussion's "stompin' backbeats
  that make it so eminently danceable", as quoted on Wikipedia, was not read in the original, and
  no study of how listeners feel the backbeat was read. The page lets the viewer hear the two
  versions and describes only where the stresses fall; the plan's predict, "Will it feel the
  same?", is not asked (30b).
- That a clap stresses any beat it lands on: no source read says it, and as a general rule it is
  loose, since claps on every beat single out none, and the page's own march claps on 2, 3 and 4
  while beat 1 stays the strongest (25). The general rule is not claimed: the page says only that
  claps on 2 and 4 make those weak beats stand out, as the backbeat's sources say, and describes the
  one move the cold open plays (21a).
- Grove Music Online's entries behind Wikipedia's strong-beat and backbeat sentences: behind a
  subscription, not read (20, 21). The page keeps the article's "usually".
- How many people think rhythm is just speed: no data (29). The belief is orange's sincere guess.
- Typical output latency for any device, and for Bluetooth audio: no figure was read; MDN says only
  that it "varies depending on the platform and the available hardware" (39). The page names no
  device and no number.
- How coarse a browser's event timestamps can be: MDN says only that precision "may be reduced"
  (37). The judge's window is 120 ms; the page claims no finer timing than its lines state.
- Whether people tap ahead of a click, and by how much: not read. The simulation's 20 ms lead is
  illustrative (42), and the page says nothing about it; the lining-up cancels a steady lead up to
  the device's own delay, and a larger one stays in, for the 120 ms window to hold (40, 42).
- Italian tempo words (Allegro, Andante): the stats snippet's `tempoName` thresholds are the stats
  kit's own, with no source, and ABRSM 2023 lists the terms (p. 18) without speeds. Not used.
- Ableton's genre tempo table ("Hip-hop: 60-100 bpm", "House: 115-130 bpm" and so on): read, cut for
  length; the page's one tempo fact beyond the arithmetic is the march's (13).
- "Johann Maelzel patented a wind-up metronome in 1815" (Wikipedia, Metronome): read, cut for
  length.
- The march tempos of other countries (British 116, German 114): read, used only for scale (13).
- Clapping heard by the page: no onset detector exists in the kit, and a microphone open with echo
