## Four words you'll meet

- **Beat**: the steady pulse under the music, the one you tap your foot to. [Play: four clicks]
- **Tempo**: how fast the beats come, counted in beats per minute, or BPM. [Play: 60, then 120]
- **Bar**: a group of beats, counted from 1. Written music ends each bar with a bar line. [Play: two
  bars of four]
- **Backbeat**: a clap or a snare drum on beats 2 and 4 of a bar of four. [Play: a bar with claps on
  2 and 4]

## Step 1 of 5: Where is the beat?

Tap your foot to a song and you're tapping its beat: a pulse that comes again and again, evenly
spaced. People also say "a beat" for a drum pattern, but "the beat" is the pulse under it. A
metronome makes the pulse plain: it clicks on every beat. This one has an arm that swings to one
side, then the other, one swing a beat.

**Try:** press Start. Then tap the pad, or press Space, with each click.
**Notice:** the clicks are evenly spaced. That evenness is what makes them a pulse.

_Toy: the metronome._

## Step 2 of 5: How fast is the beat?

How fast the beats come is the tempo, counted in beats per minute, BPM. At 60 BPM a beat comes once
a second; at 120, twice a second. A modern march typically moves at around 120. Slide the tempo and
the clicks come closer together or further apart, but they stay evenly spaced: a faster beat is
still a beat.

**Try:** drag Tempo, then tap along. The readout works out your taps per minute.
**Notice:** tap faster and your number climbs. The metronome's number is the same kind of count.

_Toy: the metronome with Tempo and the tap readout._

## Step 3 of 5: Why do musicians count to four?

Lay the metronome's beats in a row, one pad each, and they fall into groups called bars, each
counted from 1. Musicians call beat 1 the downbeat; here its click is higher. Bars of 2, 3 or 4
count 1 2, 1 2 3 or 1 2 3 4. Written music shows the group with a time signature: in 3/4, the top
number means three beats in a bar. ABRSM, a British exam board, starts Grade 1 theory with 2/4, 3/4
and 4/4.

**Try:** switch the bars between 2, 3 and 4 and count aloud. Then play six rounds of Count the bar.
**Notice:** the count goes back to 1 on every high click.

_Toy: the beat row with Bars, and the Count the bar ear game._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Two or four?** Four beats split into two pairs, so a bar of four can feel like two bars of
>   two. Open Music Theory notes that, by listening alone, it isn't always clear which one a piece
>   is in.

## Step 4 of 5: What makes a backbeat?

Put sounds on the pads, a row per sound and a column per beat: the inside of a drum pattern. The
kick, a bass drum played by foot, sits on the strong beats, 1 and 3. The hi-hat, two small
cymbals, ticks twice a beat. Claps sit on the weak beats, 2 and 4: a backbeat. Many styles in four
have one, a basic rock beat among them. Move the claps and the stresses move: the rhythm changes,
at the same speed.

**Try:** move the claps to 1 and 3 and play. Then move them back to 2 and 4.
**Notice:** on 1 and 3 the claps land with the kick; on 2 and 4, between its hits.

_Toy: the beat grid with Kick, Clap and Hi-hat._

## Step 5 of 5: So is rhythm just speed?

**Predict:** this march pattern hits beat 1 hard, BOOM-whack-whack-whack, at 120 BPM, a typical
march tempo. Slowed to the rock beat's 90, is it the rock beat?
[ Yes: same speed now ] [ No: still the march's pattern ]

(After a choice: Play the march at 90 · Play the rock beat. With sound off, the hint reads: "Sound
is off, so nothing played. Turn sound on to hear them, then press Play the march at 90.")

Reveal: No: slower, but still BOOM on 1 and whack on 2, 3 and 4; the rock beat claps on 2 and 4
only. The tempo changed, not the rhythm. (They now share a speed, so "yes" was a fair guess.)

Orange Merc: "So the tempo is the speed, and the rhythm is what happens on the beats."

> **Common mix-up:** "Rhythm is just how fast the song goes."
> **Why it seems right:** a faster song does sound different, and a tempo is one easy number.
> **The evidence:** move the claps and the rhythm changes at the same speed; slow the march and it
> keeps its BOOM-whack-whack-whack. A song's style comes from its sounds and patterns as well as
> its tempo.
> **The better idea:** tempo is how fast the beats come. Rhythm is the pattern of sounds on them:
> which beats are stressed, and how they group into bars.

**Try:** swap the tempos: the march at 90, the rock beat at 120.
**Notice:** each keeps its stresses at either speed.

_Toy: the beat grid with March, Rock and Tempo._

## Sing it: tap it, then sing it

**First, the backbeat.** The groove has a kick on 1 and 3 and the hi-hat ticking twice a beat, but
no clap on 2 and 4: you add the backbeat. Press Play the groove and clap on 2 and 4 with it. Then
press Start and tap instead: every beat for two bars, then only 2 and 4. The page can't hear claps,
only taps.

[ Play the groove ] [ Start: tap the backbeat ]

(After Start, the metronome counts one bar in. The two bars of tapping every beat line your taps up
with the sound; then come four bars of tapping only 2 and 4. Each tap sounds a clap.)

After the four bars, one line about your taps, such as:

- "All 8 landed on 2 and 4: a backbeat."
- "On 2 and 4: 6 of 8. Two taps landed on 1 or 3."
- "We couldn't line your taps up with the beat that time. Tap every beat for the first two bars,
  then 2 and 4."

[ Again ]

## Wrap

- The beat is the steady pulse you tap along to; the tempo is how fast it comes, in beats per
  minute.
- Beats group into bars of 2, 3 or 4, counted from the downbeat, beat 1. A time signature such as
  4/4 says how many.
- In a bar of four, claps or a snare on the weak beats, 2 and 4, make a backbeat. Move them and the
  rhythm changes, even at the same speed.
