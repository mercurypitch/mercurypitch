## Four words you'll meet

- **Number**: an interval's size in letters, both ends counted: C up to E is a third. [Play: C up
  to E]
- **Quality**: the word before the number: major, minor, perfect, augmented or diminished. [Play: a

## Step 1 of 4: How do you name an interval?

An interval's name has two parts. The number counts letters, both ends included: C, D, E is three
letters, so C up to E is a third. [Play C up to E] The quality says which third: C up to E is 4
semitones, a major third. [Play C up to E♭] C up to E♭ is still three letters, but 3 semitones: a
minor third. The staff counts the letters, a line or a space each, and the pitch line the
semitones. Seconds, sixths and sevenths come in major and minor too.

**Try:** move the top note up to A, then press Flat.
**Notice:** Flat changes the semitones, never the letters.

_Toy: your staff and the pitch line, C at the foot, with the top note to move by Step up, Step down
or a tap on a place, Sharp and Flat, and the readout: letters, semitones, the interval's name. Until
the cold open's choice the line draws no black-key lane but E♭'s as it sounds, and Sharp and Flat
wait._

## Step 2 of 4: Is every interval major or minor?

Tavi: "So every interval comes major and minor, like the thirds?"

Not the unison, fourth, fifth and octave: those are perfect. [Play C up to F, then F♯] A semitone
wider, the perfect fourth C up to F becomes augmented: C up to F♯. [Play C up to G, then G♭] A
semitone narrower, the perfect fifth C up to G becomes diminished: C up to G♭. Both are six
semitones, one piano key with two names (Lesson 2): the letters make one a fourth, the other a
fifth. That's the tritone, "three tones": C to D to E to F♯.

> **Common mix-up:** "Every interval comes in a major and a minor."
> **Why it seems right:** seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths do.
> **The evidence:** unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves are perfect, never major or minor. A
> perfect or major interval made a semitone wider is augmented; a perfect or minor one made a
> semitone narrower is diminished.
> **The better idea:** two families: perfect, and major and minor.

**Try:** press Sharp on F, then Flat on G.
**Notice:** one piano key, two places on the staff.

_Toy: your staff and the pitch line, as in Step 1, with Play C up to F, then F♯ and Play C up to G,
then G♭, both waiting for the cold open's choice._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Halfway in hertz.** Halfway between C4, 261.63 Hz, and C5, 523.25 Hz, is 392.44 Hz: exactly
>   3:2 above C, a pure fifth, 2 cents above the piano's G. From there up to C5 is 4:3, a pure
>   fourth. But your ear hears steps by multiplying, not adding (Lesson 2), so to the ear the halfway
>   note is F♯.
> - **Perfect is a name, not a promise.** In counterpoint, the perfect fourth counts as a dissonance
>   when it sits against the lowest voice, and as a consonance between two upper voices.

## Step 3 of 4: What happens if you turn an interval upside down?

Move the bottom note up an octave. [Play C up to G, then G up to C] C up to G, a fifth, becomes G up
to C, a fourth. The numbers add up to 9, 5 and 4, and the semitones to 12, 7 and 5: the whole
octave. [Play C up to E, then E up to C] A major third, 4 semitones, turns into a minor sixth, 8.
Major turns minor and minor turns major, perfect stays perfect, and augmented and diminished swap.

**Try:** fold a fifth, then a third, then C up to F♯.
**Notice:** the numbers always make 9. The tritone folds onto six semitones again: it's its own
upside down.

_Toy: your staff and the pitch line with Fold: the bottom note lifts an octave, and the pitch line
shows the interval and its upside down filling the octave, with the sums, as in "5 + 4 = 9" and
"7 + 5 = 12". Fold and the step's two plays wait for the cold open's choice._

## Step 4 of 4: Why does the halfway interval want to move?

Tavi: "An exact half: shouldn't that be the calmest interval of all?"

Exact, yes, but not settled: the tritone counts as a dissonance, less stable, as if it needs to
resolve. Among the white keys, only F and B are a tritone apart: fa and ti in C major. Ti leans up
to do, as in Lesson 4. [Play B and F, then C and E] Move each a semitone, B up to C and F down to E,
and it closes into a major third: do and mi. Lesson 27 comes back to this pair.

**Try:** play B and F, then curl the pitch line into a ring.
**Notice:** on the ring, B and F sit straight across.

_Toy: your staff and the pitch line, with Play B and F, then C and E, and Curl into a ring, which
bends the line into Lesson 2's ring of twelve, its B lane joining the ring's B bead. The play and the
curl wait for the cold open's choice._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **The devil in music.** The tritone's old nickname is diabolus in musica, Latin for "the devil in
>   music". Johann Joseph Fux's counterpoint textbook of 1725 quotes the phrase in a proverb and
>   bars the tritone leap in counterpoint.

## Wrap

- An interval's name has two parts: the number counts letters, both ends included, and the quality
  comes from the semitones.
- Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves are perfect; seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths are major
  or minor. A perfect interval a semitone wider is augmented, a semitone narrower diminished.
- Turn an interval upside down and the numbers make 9, the semitones 12. The tritone, six
  semitones, stays a tritone, and leans.
