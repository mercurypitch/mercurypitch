## Four words you'll meet

- **Number**: an interval's size in letters, both ends counted: C up to E is a third. [Play C4–E4]
  (R1)
- **Quality**: the word before the number: perfect, major, minor, augmented or diminished. [Play
  major third, then minor third] (R2, R3)
- **Perfect**: the quality of the unison, fourth, fifth and octave. [Play C4–G4] (R2)
- **Tritone**: six semitones, half an octave. [Play C4–F♯4] (R11)
- Words: 80.

## Step 1 of 4: How do you name an interval?

- Point: two parts. The number counts letters, both ends included, so C up to E is a third and so
  is C up to E♭: accidentals do not change the number (R1). The quality says which third: 4
  semitones is major, 3 is minor (Lesson 10). Seconds, sixths and sevenths work the same way (R2).
- Examples, sound first, then staff (treble C4–E4, bass C3–E3): C–D (major second, 2), C–E♭ (minor
  third, 3), C–E (major third, 4), C–A (major sixth, 9), C–B♭ (minor seventh, 10), C–B (major
  seventh, 11).
- Toy: the ruler with two readouts, letters counted and semitones counted; tap a note to move the
  top.
- Try: make a third, then lower its top note. Notice: the letter count stays 3; the semitones drop
  to 3.
- Facts: R1, R2. Words: 140.

## Step 2 of 4: Is every interval major or minor?

- Tavi: "So every interval comes in a major and a minor, like the thirds?"
- Common mix-up card:
  - Belief: "Every interval comes in a major and a minor."
  - Why it seems right: seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths do.
  - The evidence: the unison, fourth, fifth and octave are called perfect, and are never major or
    minor (R2). Widen a perfect interval by a semitone and it is augmented; narrow it and it is
    diminished (R3).
  - The better idea: two families. Perfect, with augmented and diminished either side; and major
    and minor.
- Predict (written out): "Merc widens the perfect fourth, C up to F, by one semitone. Then he
  narrows the perfect fifth, C up to G, by one semitone. Do they land on the same key?" [ Yes, the
  same key ] [ No, a semitone apart ]. Reveal: "The same key. C up to F♯ is an augmented fourth,
  C up to G♭ a diminished fifth: two names for the same six semitones, the tritone (R3, R11). (The
  two started a semitone apart and each moved a semitone, which made 'a semitone apart' a fair
  guess.)"
- Examples: C4–F4 (5), C4–F♯4 (6), C4–G4 (7), C4–G♭4 (6). Staff in both clefs shows F♯ and G♭ on
  different lines, the same key on the keyboard (Lesson 21's "one key, two names").
- Toy: the ruler, with Widen and Narrow buttons on a perfect interval.
- Facts: R2, R3, R11. Words: 220.

## Step 3 of 4: What happens if you turn an interval upside down?

- Point: move the bottom note up an octave. C up to G, a fifth, becomes G up to C, a fourth. The
  numbers add to 9 (5 + 4) and the semitones to 12 (7 + 5) (R5, R6). Major turns minor, minor turns
  major, perfect stays perfect, augmented and diminished swap (R5).
- Examples: C4–G4 then G4–C5 (P5 to P4); C4–E4 then E4–C5 (major third 4 to minor sixth 8); D4–F4
  then F4–D5 (minor third 3 to major sixth 9).
- Toy (the signature): Lesson 6's ruler folds at the octave, so each interval lies against its
  inversion; the two spans always fill 12.
- Try: pick any interval, then Fold. Notice: the two numbers always make 9.
- This is the sing-along's interval: do up to so, then so up to do.
- Facts: R5, R6. Words: 150.

## Step 4 of 4: Why does the tritone want to move?

- Predict (written out): "Turn the tritone upside down: F up to B, then B up to F. How many
  semitones is the new interval?" [ Six again ] [ Five ]. Reveal: "Six again. An augmented fourth
  inverts to a diminished fifth, and both are six semitones: the tritone is its own upside down
  (R5, R11). (Most intervals change size when they turn over, which made 'five' a fair guess.)"
- Point: the tritone counts as a dissonance, an interval heard as needing to resolve (R8). In C
  major it is the only tritone between white keys, F and B: fa and ti (R4, R11). Ti leans up to do,
  Lesson 4's leading note (R9, R15). Play B3 and F4 together, then let each move a semitone, B up
  to C and F down to E: C4 and E4, a major third. Lesson 27 shows why fa moves down: on the V chord
  it is the added seventh (R10).
- Examples: F4+B4 (augmented fourth), B3+F4 (diminished fifth), then B3+F4 moving to C4+E4.
- Toy: the ring, its diameter lit between F and B, folding inwards to E and C.
- Tavi's re-explanation: "So it sits in the middle, and the notes either side of it pull it home."
- Words: 210. "Hardest" and "strongest" are not used (RESEARCH, contradiction 1).

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **The devil in music.** In a 1725 counterpoint textbook, Johann Joseph Fux has the teacher
>   remind his student of a well-worn saying, "mi against fa is the devil in music", and forbid the
>   tritone leap because it is hard to sing and sounds bad (R12). The saying was about
>   solmisation's mi and fa. Stories of a church ban are just stories: no record of one is known
>   (R13). (Wording waits for the translation check in Open questions.)
> - **Why "perfect"?** A pure fifth is the frequency ratio 3:2 and a pure fourth 4:3, as the octave
>   is 2:1 (R7). The piano's fifth is 700 cents, two cents under the pure 702: Lesson 21's nudge.
> - **The fourth's odd status.** In counterpoint the perfect fourth counts as a dissonance against
>   the lowest voice, and as a consonance between upper voices (R8).
> - **Another way to find a quality.** Learn the white keys: every fourth between white keys is
>   perfect except F up to B, the tritone (R4).

## Wrap

- Three lines: an interval has a number, letters counted both ends, and a quality; the unison,
  fourth, fifth and octave are perfect, the rest major or minor, and a semitone either way makes

  tritone stays six, leaning.
- One-sentence challenge: "Explain to a friend, in one sentence, why there is no such thing as a
  major fifth."
