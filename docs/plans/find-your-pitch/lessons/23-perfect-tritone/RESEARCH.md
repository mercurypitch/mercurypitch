# Research: Lesson 23, Perfect, major, minor and the tritone

Every "Facts to check" item on the Lesson 23 card (SEASON-2 § 3), resolved, and every other claim
the article plan makes. App claims were read at `b803c7d43`; web sources on 30 October 2026.
Open Music Theory (OMT) was read in its LibreTexts editions, whose music examples and tables are
images the text layer does not carry; no fact rests on an example's content unless the text names
it. Strength follows pedagogy-research.md; for standard theory definitions "Strong" means two or
more standard texts agree.

## Where the sources and the card disagree (read first)

1. **"Fa and ti, the two notes that lean hardest" is not supported.** OMT 1e names a different pair:
   "The most prominent tendency tones in Western tonal styles are ti (not te) and le (not la)" (R9).
   Fa's pull is contextual: as the seventh of a seventh chord it "has a strong tendency to resolve
   down by step" (R9, R10). The plan says: the tritone in a major key lies between fa and ti; ti
   leans up to do (Lesson 4); and fa leans down to mi when it is the seventh of the V chord, which
   is Lesson 27. It never says "hardest" or "strongest".
2. **Inversion adds to 9 in numbers and to 12 in semitones.** The card's "the two add up to an
   octave" is right for semitones (and the ruler adds to 12); OMT's rule is that "the size of
   inverted pairs always adds up to 9" (R5). The plan teaches both, numbers first.
3. **Perfect does not mean always consonant.** OMT's counterpoint chapter says the perfect fourth
   "is dissonant when it involves the lowest voice in the texture, but it is consonant when it
   occurs between upper voices" (R8). The plan keeps "perfect" as a name, not a promise, and puts
   the fourth's special status in Go deeper.
4. **OMT does not recommend counting semitones to find a quality** ("We do not recommend this
   method, because it is time consuming and often inaccurate", R4). The series counts semitones
   (Lesson 6). The plan keeps semitones, since Season 1 built on them, and adds OMT's white-key
   method as Go deeper; noted so a reviewer is not surprised.
5. **The tritone's "devil" story: the nickname is real, a church ban is not documented.** Fux's
   1725 textbook, read in its Latin scan, calls "mi contra fa est diabolus in Musica" a well-worn
   proverb and bars the tritone leap in two-part counterpoint as hard to sing and bad-sounding (R12).
   That is a teacher's rule, not a church's. No primary source for a ban was found; two secondary
   accounts say none is known (R13). The card's sources (Randel 2003, Werckmeister 1702, Smith 1979)
   were not reached.

## Claims

### Naming an interval

**R1. An interval has a number (its size) and a quality. The number counts letter names, both
ends included: C up to E is a third, whatever accidentals the notes carry.**
- Source: Gotham, M., Gullings, K., Hamm, C., Hughes, B., Jarvis, B., Lavengood, M. & Peterson, J.
  Open Music Theory, 2nd ed. (VIVA, CC BY-SA), § 1.15 "Intervals", LibreTexts edition,
  https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/01%3A_Fundamentals/1.15%3A_Intervals.
- Says: "Every interval has a size and a quality. A size is the distance between two notes on a
  staff—i.e., it is a measurement of the number of lines and spaces between two notes"; "Always
  begin with “one” when counting size"; "the interval between two notes on the same line or space
  is called a “unison,” not a “first,” and notes eight lines and spaces apart are said to be an
  “octave,” not an “eighth.”"; "it doesn't matter what accidentals you apply to the notes—the size
  is always the same" (§ Size).
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong (standard; Lesson 6 already counts letters this way).

**R2. Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves are perfect; seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths are
major or minor.**
- Source: OMT 2e § 1.15, § Perfect, Major, and Minor Qualities: "Unisons, fourths, fifths, and
  octaves form perfect intervals, while seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths form major and minor
  intervals." It adds that "Different theorists (in different locations and time periods) have
  applied these qualities to different sizes of intervals, depending on milieu", and that its table
  "shows how these qualities are applied today."
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong. The "today" caveat goes into Go deeper, not the step.

**R3. A perfect or major interval made a semitone wider is augmented; a perfect or minor interval
made a semitone narrower is diminished.**
- Source: OMT 2e § 1.15: "Augmented intervals are one half step larger than a perfect or major
  interval. Diminished intervals are one half step smaller than a perfect or minor interval." And:
  "the perfect fifth F–C is made a half step smaller by lowering the top note to C♭, forming a
  diminished fifth (also called a tritone, usually abbreviated as d5 or o5)" (§ Augmented and
  Diminished Qualities).
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R4. OMT prefers a scale method to counting semitones.** § 1.15, § The "Major Scale" Method: "One
method you may have heard of is counting half steps. We do not recommend this method, because it
is time consuming and often inaccurate." It also teaches a white-key method: "All of the fourths
are perfect except for one: F–B, which is an augmented fourth (a tritone)." Read: in full. Used in
Go deeper (the white-key fourths) and as contradiction 4.

### Inversion

**R5. Turn an interval upside down and the two numbers add to 9; major turns to minor and minor to
major, perfect stays perfect, augmented and diminished swap.**
- Source: OMT 2e § 1.15, § Intervallic Inversion: "the size of inverted pairs always adds up to 9";
  "Fourths invert to fifths (4 + 5 = 9)"; "Perfect intervals invert to perfect intervals. Major
  intervals invert to minor intervals (and minor intervals to major intervals). Augmented intervals
  invert to diminished intervals (and diminished intervals to augmented intervals)"; "a perfect
  fourth inverts to a perfect fifth"; "A diminished fifth inverts to an augmented fourth".
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R6. In semitones, an interval and its inversion add to 12, an octave.** Arithmetic: inversion
moves the lower note up an octave (OMT § 1.15: "inverted by moving the C up by an octave"), so the
two spans fill 12 semitones. Fifth 7 + fourth 5; major third 4 + minor sixth 8; tritone 6 + 6.
Strength: Strong (arithmetic on R5).

### The perfect intervals as ratios

**R7. Pure octave, fifth and fourth are the frequency ratios 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3; in equal temperament
the fifth is very slightly narrow.**
- Source: Wolfe, J. (UNSW Physclips). "Tartini tones, consonance, temperament, harmony and scales".
  https://animations.physics.unsw.edu.au/jw/Tartini-tones-temperament.html.
- Says: octave "has a frequency ratio of 2:1 exactly"; "3:2 = 1.500"; "4:3 = 1.333"; "every second
  harmonic of the higher note coincides with one of those of the note a fifth below"; in equal
  temperament "the fifth is very slightly narrow".
- Read: in full (a short page). Strength: Strong (physics). Arithmetic: a pure fifth is 1200 ×
  log2(3/2) = 701.96 cents and a pure fourth 498.04, against 700 and 500 (`python3 -I`). Lesson 1
  gave 2:1; Lesson 13's harmonics show 3:2 as harmonics 2 and 3. Used in Go deeper; Lesson 24 builds
  on it.

### Consonance and the tritone

**R8. In counterpoint, unisons, fifths and octaves are perfect consonances, thirds and sixths
imperfect consonances, and all seconds, sevenths, augmented and diminished intervals dissonances;
the fourth depends on where it sits.**
- Source: OMT 2e § 2.01 "Introduction to Species Counterpoint", § Consonance and dissonance:
  "Perfect consonances (perfect unisons, fifths, and octaves)"; "Imperfect consonances (major and
  minor thirds and sixths)"; "Dissonances (all seconds, sevenths, and diminished and augmented
  intervals)"; § The elusive perfect fourth: it "is dissonant when it involves the lowest voice in
  the texture, but it is consonant when it occurs between upper voices". § 1.15, § Consonance and
  Dissonance: dissonant intervals "are considered less stable, as if they do need to resolve. These
  categorizations have varied with milieu."
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong for the classification within this style; the "milieu" caveat
  is kept. So the tritone, as an augmented fourth or a diminished fifth, is a dissonance, "as if"
  it needs to resolve: the hook's "why does it want to move?".

**R9. Ti is the most prominent tendency tone, pulling to do; a chord's seventh tends to resolve down
by step.**
- Source: Wharton, R. & Shaffer, K. (eds.). Open Music Theory, 1st ed. (Hybrid Pedagogy, CC
  BY-SA), § 2.12 "Strict Four-Voice Composition - Tendency Tones and Functional Harmonic
  Dissonances", LibreTexts, last modified 16 July 2023.
- Says: "A tendency tone is a pitch (class)—usually represented as a scale degree—that tends to
  progress to some pitch classes more than others"; "The most prominent tendency tones in Western
  tonal styles are ti (not te) and le (not la)"; "when ti appears it tends to be followed by do in
  the same voice ... this tendency is strongest when ti occurs in a dominant-functioning chord";
  the dissonant degrees "include the seventh of every seventh chord", and such a degree "has a
  strong tendency to resolve down by step".
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong (standard; Lesson 4 already says ti "leans up").

**R10. Adding fa to the V chord makes a dominant seventh, whose dissonance strengthens its pull to
I.**
- Source: OMT 2e § 4.02 "Strengthening Endings with V7": "the V chord contains an extra note, fa,
  that transforms it from a major triad into a dominant seventh chord. Since the seventh adds
  dissonance (and therefore instability) to the chord, it strengthens the pull of the V chord
  toward I." (The scale-degree glyphs after "fa" are images.)
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong. Used only as a one-line preview of Lesson 27.

**R11. The tritone is six semitones, 600 cents, exactly half the octave; the augmented fourth and
the diminished fifth are the same six semitones and each other's inversion.** Arithmetic: 12 / 2
= 6; 6 × 100 = 600 cents. OMT 2e § 1.15 names both a tritone (R3, R4) and gives the d5–A4 inversion
(R5). On Lesson 2's ring of twelve, six steps from C is F♯, straight across. In C major the only
tritone between white keys is F–B (R4), fa and ti. Strength: Strong.

### The tritone's history

**R12. Fux (1725) calls "mi contra fa est diabolus in musica" a well-worn proverb, and forbids the
tritone leap in counterpoint because it is hard to sing and sounds bad.**
- Source: Fux, J. J. (1725). Gradus ad Parnassum, sive Manuductio ad compositionem musicae
  regularem. Vienna. p. 52 (Exercise 1, Lesson 2, note against note, the dialogue of Aloysius and
  Josephus). Scan with OCR at https://archive.org/details/gradusadparnassu00fuxj_0.
- Says (Latin, spelling normalised from the scan's long s): "Non dubito, quin saepius audiveris
  tritum illud sermone proverbium: mi contra fa, est diabolus in Musica, quod fecisti procedendo de
  sexta Nota fa ad septimam mi per saltum Quartae majoris, sive Tritoni, quem cantu difficilem,
  atque male sonantem in Contrapuncto adhibere nefas est." This plan's translation: "No doubt you
  have often heard that well-worn proverb, 'mi against fa is the devil in music', which is what you
  did by going from the sixth note, fa, to the seventh, mi, by a leap of a major fourth, or tritone,
  which, being difficult to sing and bad-sounding, may not be used in counterpoint."
- Read: the passage in the primary scan's OCR. The translation is this plan's and is for a
  Latinist or a published translation (Mann, The Study of Counterpoint, 1943/1965, not read) to
  check before a script quotes it. Strength: Strong that Fux wrote it; it shows a teaching rule
  about a melodic leap, not a church ruling.

**R13. Secondary accounts: the phrase is found from the early 18th century; no medieval use and no
church ban are documented.**
- Sources: Wikipedia, "Tritone", read 30 October 2026: the term "has been applied to the interval
  from at least the early 18th century"; "stories that singers were excommunicated or otherwise
  punished by the Church" are "likely fanciful"; it cites Werckmeister 1702 (Harmonologia musica, p.
  6), Mattheson 1739 and Telemann 1733 describing it as an old singers' name, and Smith 1979 (Journal
  of Musicological Research 3, 63-74) and Hammerstein 1974 for its absence in medieval sources. Sword,
  A. (31 March 2022), "Diabolus in musica: did the Catholic Church ban the tritone ...?", Skeptical
  Inquirer, quoting the Harvard musicologist Thomas Forrest Kelly: "I'm not aware of any medieval
  references to" diabolus in musica; "The earliest references I know of are from the eighteenth
  century".
- Read: both in full. Werckmeister, Mattheson, Telemann, Smith, Hammerstein and Randel (2003) **not
  read**. Strength: Weak (secondary and journalistic, though consistent with R12).
- Use: Go deeper only, worded to what R12 shows and R13 reports, with the ban named as a story with
  no known evidence.

## Interesting findings

1. **The "devil in music" was a textbook proverb.** Fux, in 1725, has his teacher tell the student
   he has surely heard "mi against fa is the devil in music", and bars the tritone leap in two-part
   writing because it is hard to sing and sounds bad (R12). No church ban is documented (R13).
   Possible: Go deeper.
2. **The tritone is its own upside down.** Every other interval turns into a different one when
   inverted; the tritone's six semitones invert to six (R5, R6, R11). Possible: Step 4's predict.
3. **Lesson 6 already used one.** Its cold open moved "Twinkle" up six semitones, a tritone, to
   prove a tune survives a move (R14). Possible: wrap line or a Step 4 aside.
4. **"Perfect" is a name, not a verdict.** In counterpoint the perfect fourth counts as a
   dissonance when it sits against the lowest voice (R8). Possible: Go deeper.
5. **On the ring of twelve, the tritone is the diameter.** C sits opposite F♯, and every note sits
   opposite the note a tritone away, so there are six such pairs (R11; arithmetic, 12 / 2). Possible:
   cold open's reveal.
