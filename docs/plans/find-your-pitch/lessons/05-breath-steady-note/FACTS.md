## The breath: lungs, diaphragm and holding back

14. The diaphragm cannot push air out: upright, it returns to its dome only when the belly-wall muscles press the belly's contents up, and those are muscles for breathing out; in speech it is mostly passive | Sundberg 1992, p. 50 ("With the body in an upright position, the diaphragm muscle can be restored to its upward-bulging shape only by means of the abdominal wall muscles"; "Therefore, they are muscles for exhalation."), p. 59 ("most researchers have found that the diaphragm, the other main inspiratory muscle, is passive in speech") | read; Step 2's "It can't push air out", quiz question 1's feedback, and, with 21 and 22, "mostly works on the breath in" (Step 2, quiz question 1, the Wrap). ## How a note starts

34. The start of a note is its onset, and onsets are commonly sorted into three kinds | DeJonckere 2025, § 1 ("Vocal onset can be defined as the process that occurs between the first detectable glottal (vibratory) movement and the steady-state vibration of the vocal folds."; "Three broad categories of vocal onset (or 'attack') are generally recognised: soft (or 'coordinated'), hard and breathy (or 'aspirated')"); Watson, Baken & Roark 2016, abstract ("Vocal onsets have commonly been qualitatively characterized into three types: hard, simultaneous, and breathy.") | read; the four words' "Onset" and Step 4's "A voice has three main ways to start a note, its onset": "main", because the three sit on a continuum (38; review 1, O7). The page names the kinds in plain words (air first, a catch, together), not by the three sets of labels in use
35. Air first: the sound is preceded by a flow of air, which can be audible, like an "h"; in Estill's terms, "aspirate where expiration precedes vocal fold closure" | DeJonckere 2025, § 3 ("A voice onset can also be 'breathy' when the sound production is preceded by a voluntary and conscious airflow increase, which can in some cases become audible"); Wikipedia, Estill | read; Step 4's "Air first: a puff of breath, like a soft "h", then the sound." and the three starts toy
36. A catch: the vocal folds close firmly before the sound, much like closing for a cough, and the sound starts as they open; in Estill's terms, "glottal where the vocal folds are closed before expiration" | DeJonckere 2025, § 3 ("a voice onset can be 'hard', which means that the VF oscillation is preceded not by just a VF-contact on the midline with a low adduction force, but by a stronger closure of the larynx, resembling to some extent an effort closure like in a cough"), § 4.2.1 ("Sound occurs with the first glottal opening"); Wikipedia, Estill | read; Step 4's "A catch: the vocal folds are pressed shut, and the note starts as they open, a little like a cough." and quiz question 3's "A catch starts with the folds pressed shut"
37. Together: the folds come close (a slit with inaudible airflow) or touch lightly, and vibration starts as the air flows; in Estill's terms, "smooth where vocal fold closure is synchronised with expiration" | DeJonckere 2025, § 3 ("a slightly spindle-shaped glottal slit is observed just before oscillation starts. ## Unverified, not used

- Hixon, T. J. (2006), _Respiratory Function in Singing: A Primer for Singers and Singing
  Teachers_: not read. The lesson's Hixon facts come from Watson & Hixon 1985's abstract (16), and
  the term "inspiratory checking" from Lowell 2025, which cites Hixon and Hoit (2005), also not read
  (23).
- "Checking action" in Draper, Ladefoged and Whitteridge (1959), _Respiratory muscles in speech_:
  the paper has no abstract on PubMed and was not read.
- "Breathing myths debunked", McGill University's Music Performance and Body seminar site
  (sites.music.mcgill.ca/mpbs/out-of-breath/breathing-myths-debunked): read, but it shows no author
  or date, so nothing is cited from it; what it says about the diaphragm agrees with 14 and 15.
- "The diaphragm only helps you breathe in": contradicted by the singers who keep it working
  through a phrase (21, 22). The page says "mostly".
- "Steady air is what holds a note still": not shown by any source read; air pressure moves pitch
  (8 to 10), and muscles in the voice box set it (Lesson 3 FACTS 23). The page says a steady note
  needs steady air.
- "All trained singers draw the belly in before singing": Salomoni 2016 found variation, and one
  singer breathed like the untrained group (31). The page says "most" of that study's singers.
- "Singing teachers usually aim for the easy start": one teacher's guide (39). The page says
  "many".
- "Many singers sing with a vibrato", or how wide a beginner's is: not read. The page defines the
  word and says a small one can sit inside the band (42).
- "See a voice specialist": no source read offers it as the referral; the page uses Lesson 3's
  doctor lines (51).
- "A noisy breath in means a tight throat": teachers' handouts found by a web search say so with no
  data. Not used; "quietly" is an instruction (32).
- "A low breath, or support, makes a note steadier": no study read shows it. Salomoni 2016 measured
  breathing, not the sound (31), and VF 2026 found "empirical data were lacking" (25). The page
  says only that a steady note needs steady air (7 to 10).
- "Pushing, or a hard start, harms the voice": DeJonckere 2025 says a forceful catch "may be
  considered phonotraumatic", and VF 2026's simulation ties air pressure to the folds' impact
  stress. Health and injury claims are out of bounds for a lesson (TEACHING-PLAYBOOK house rules),
  so neither is used.
- The extra pitch irregularity in a sung note's first five cycles, against the next ten
  (DeJonckere 2025, §§ 5.7 and 6: "significantly larger during the initial cycles of the target
  tone"): five cycles last a few hundredths of a second (23 ms at A3), far shorter than the scoop a
  pitch line shows, so it is not used to explain the scoop.
- "Five seconds is over before a steady tone settles", "fifteen is a long way to go without a rest"
  and "half of a five-second run is already gone": code comments recording the owner's product call
  (`src/features/exercises/timer-preference.ts:17-24`, `:39-44`; 63), with no measurement behind
  them. Not used on the page. On 19 October 2026 the owner decided that the lesson's first hold and
  the proposed slug open at ten seconds, with fifteen for the longer hold (63).
- The National Association of Teachers of Singing's view that abdominal breathing is one of the
  most effective directives (Spillane's Delphi study, cited by Salomoni 2016, p. 2): not read.
- How often sung notes scoop, and how long a beginner's note takes to settle: L-M & P 2018's
  abstract gives no rate, and Hutchins and Campbell (2009), "Estimating the time to reach a target
  frequency in singing", gives no numbers in its abstract. The page says "can" (41).
- Typical maximum phonation times: not read and not needed; the lesson never measures the longest
  hold.
- Whether the three onset names are agreed: Estill says glottal, smooth and aspirate (35 to 37),
  others say hard, simultaneous and breathy (34). The page uses plain words instead.
