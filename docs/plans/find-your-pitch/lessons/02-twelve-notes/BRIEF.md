# Brief

## Intent

- What the video is for: Lesson 2 of "Find Your Pitch", MercuryPitch's music theory and singing
  series, and its second theory lesson (topics T03, twelve notes, and the cents half of T04; T04's
  "in tune" moves to Lesson 3, DECISIONS 62). It answers the question Lesson 1's film ends on and
  embeds in the Lesson 2 web page.
- Who is watching: beginners who have seen Lesson 1 or know what an octave is, many of whom think
  the black keys are a mystery, or that they "can't sing". They want to understand, not memorise
  jargon. Most watch with the sound on (this is about hearing small steps), but the picture must
  still carry every idea with the sound off and captions on.
- What they should do at the end: answer the recap in their head, then listen for semitones and
  tones in MercuryPitch's Ear Lab, in its Leap drill. Spoken at the end of the recap: "In Ear Lab's
  Leap, listen for the semitone and the tone." On screen: Leap's twelve answers with m2 and M2 lit,
  then "Practise it free: mercurypitch.com/ear-lab", with "In Leap: m2 = semitone · M2 = tone".
  Next-episode tease: Lesson 3's hook, ending on orange's "So what's going on?"

## Teaching

### Objectives (CURRICULUM § 2.1, Lesson 2)

1. Find the twelve notes on a keyboard and on the spiral.
2. Tell a semitone from a whole tone by ear.
3. Know why one key has two names (C sharp, D flat).
4. Know that a semitone is 100 cents, the unit MercuryPitch measures in.

### Hook

The cold open re-asks the question Lesson 1's film closes on, after a recall question from Lesson 1
(playbook rule 18). Orange guesses "Is there a hidden note between every two letters?"; blue:
"Seven letters, but twelve notes." Orange: "Then where do the other five come from?" (done by
about 0:24; rule 1). The web page's title is the curriculum's wording: "Musicians only use seven
letters, so where do the other five notes come from?" YouTube title variants for Test & Compare
(MASTER-PLAN § 4.1): "Seven letters, but twelve notes: where do the other five come from?" (67
characters); "Why are there 12 notes but only 7 letters?" (42); search-style, in the audience's own
words, "Why is there no black key between B and C?" (42).

### Misconceptions (orange voices them sincerely; a demonstration breaks them)

1. "Black keys are sharps and white keys are naturals" (o6, "So black keys are the sharps and
   flats, and white keys are the plain ones?"). Broken by: a predict ("one semitone up from E: a
   black key or a white one?"), then the sound and the arrow landing on F, a white key, so E sharp
   is F; a sharp is a direction, not a colour; and one black key carries two names.
2. "B to C is the same step as C to D: on the keyboard they look just as far apart" (o5, "But B to
   C looks just as far as C to D. Isn't it the same step?"), the eye's guess. Broken by: a
   three-way predict (B to C, the same, C to D), then the sound (B to C is the same step as C to
   the black key above it, and half of C to D), then the picture (the keyboard drawn with every key
   the same width). Every neighbouring key, white or black, is one semitone.
3. The cold-open guess: there is a hidden note between every two letters (o1), one in each of the
   seven gaps. Blue's count ("Seven letters, but twelve notes") opens the doubt, and the film's
   central demonstration breaks it by sight and sound in Act 2: only the five letter gaps a tone
   wide hold a note; B to C and E to F hold none. Orange says the answer itself (o5a).

### Signature object and its transformation

Lesson 1's pitch spiral (three turns, A2 to A5, 30 degrees per semitone, the seven letters at their
true angles, five dim beads on every turn). The five beads light up one by one as twelve steps
climb A3 to A4; seen from straight above, the turn is a ring of twelve equal beads. The spiral then
unrolls into the keyboard, as in Lesson 1, but now with all twelve keys of every octave lit. The
keyboard straightens into a row of equal tiles, one semitone each; the row stands up into a ruler
of cents, a hundred to each semitone and twelve hundred to the octave; and the ruler's lanes
become MercuryPitch's pitch canvas, where Merc sings the semitone. Twelve equal steps make an
octave, on every object.

### Three signature moments

1. **The ring of twelve.** As the climb plays, the five dim beads on the lit turn light up one by
   one, the count reaches twelve just as A comes back one turn up, and then we look straight down
   the spiral: the turn is a ring of twelve beads, evenly spaced like the hours on a clock.
2. **The keyboard drawn fair.** Every key slides into one row at the same width, and B and C turn
   out to touch, as close as C and its black key. Then the five dark tiles lift out, leaving the
   seven letters with five holes a note wide and two touching pairs, B and C, E and F; the five
   drop back into their holes: that is where the other five come from.
3. **One key, two names.** An arrow climbs one semitone from C and writes C♯ on the black key;
   another steps one semitone down from D and writes D♭ on the very same key; both names stay, one
   above the other (the dark tile is too narrow for two side by side), under one gold ring.

### Checkpoint (sung)

Slide up one semitone from a held note, and back. Blue announces it as a demonstration ("Listen
first."), holds A3, slides to A#3 and comes back; orange sings it an octave higher and asks how to
know it was exactly one semitone; blue answers with the app's own reading (the monitor's name turns
A4 to A#4 with the marker in the middle, then back: FACTS 30a). Only then is the viewer invited
(rule 16: blue sings it, orange tries, the viewer is invited), with the safety said first: "Hum it
quietly, or sing it on ooh. Never push, and stop if anything feels uncomfortable." (the short form,
approved by the owner for both lessons, DECISIONS 74). They start from Merc's A or the A an octave
up, "whichever feels easy" (each of the app's six range presets contains one of them, FACTS 38), in
a 6.5 s guided pause. Nothing listens in the film: a plain ring timer, no microphone, the Mercs wait
rather than listen, and nothing of the app's drawing stays on screen while the viewer sings (the
film's own plain guide replaces it, DECISIONS 68). A quiet one-semitone hum slide is within a
comfortable hum, so the comfort-check line is not needed (DECISIONS 25).

## Must never appear

- Claims not in FACTS.md; "wrong", "failed", "bad" or "tone deaf" about anyone; emojis; any
  AI-assistant attribution.
- "The smallest step in music", or "the smallest step" with no scope (only "the smallest step a
  piano has", "on a piano", or "between them" for the twelve); "every semitone is a hundred cents"
  as a law of all music (only "in the tuning MercuryPitch uses"); "sharp" or "flat" meaning a
  little too high or low (the film keeps one meaning per word); any cents reading the app does not
  show (such as "+100 ¢" from a starting note on an app-labelled drawing); any claim or picture
  that the film or the app hears or checks the viewer's slide (no microphone, no listening ring, no
  Merc listening to the viewer, and nothing of the app's drawing, neither its title, marker, singer
  line nor target band, over the viewer's turn), that Leap scores singing, or that the pitch line "always" or
  "only" shows sharps.
- A link that does not exist yet (no `/exercises/...` slug on screen).
