## Four words you'll meet

- **Semitone**, or half step: the smallest step on a piano, from one key to the very next.
  [Play: A to A♯]
- **Tone**, or whole step: two semitones. [Play: A to B]
- **Sharp ♯ and flat ♭**: one semitone up, and one semitone down. [Play: C to C♯]
- **Cent**: a tiny unit for measuring the distance between notes. In the tuning MercuryPitch uses, a
  semitone is 100 cents. [Play: a slow glide of 100 cents]

## Step 1 of 6: What's the very next note after A?

Start on A and step up to the very next note. You land on the bead between A and B. That step is
a semitone, the smallest step a piano has, and the bead is a real note, one of the five with no
letter of its own.

**Try:** tap A3, then the bead after it, then B3.
**Notice:** to your ear, the bead sits halfway between A and B, so A to B is two of these small
steps.

_Toy: the spiral (Lesson 1's), its five dim beads now playable. Each copy of the spiral keeps its
own lit beads, so the hero's climb leaves this one's dim (DECISIONS 81)._

## Step 2 of 6: How many steps until we're back at A?

Keep taking semitone steps and count. From A3 you reach A4, one turn up the spiral, in twelve
steps: seven letters, and five notes between them. In hertz each step is a little bigger than the
last. To your ear, though, every step is the same size, so each one turns the spiral by the same
amount. And twelve of them double the frequency, from 220 Hz to 440 Hz, which is exactly what an
octave is.

**Try:** press Climb and watch the beads light. Then press Look down.
**Notice:** from above, one turn is a ring of twelve beads, evenly spaced like the hours on a
clock.

_Toy: the spiral, with Climb and Look down._

## Step 3 of 6: Isn't B to C the same step as C to D?

Unroll the spiral and every bead lands on a key: seven white keys and five black ones in every
octave. Now look at B and C: there's no black key between them.

**Predict:** which step is bigger, B to C or C to D, or are they the same?
[ B to C ] [ The same ] [ C to D ]

(After a choice: Play B to C · Play C to D.)

Reveal: C to D is the bigger step, two semitones; B to C is one. E to F is one too. [Play E to F]

> **Common mix-up:** "B to C looks just as far as C to D, so it must be the same step."
> **Why it seems right:** on the keyboard every white key is drawn the same width.
> **The evidence:** [Play C to C♯, then B to C] B to C is exactly as big as C to the black key
> above it: half of C to D.
> **The better idea:** B and C are already neighbours. Every neighbouring pair of keys, white or
> black, is one semitone apart.

**Try:** press Straighten to draw every key the same width.
**Notice:** B and C touch, just like C and the black key above it.

_Toy: the keyboard, with Unroll and Straighten._

## Step 4 of 6: So where do the other five come from?

Two semitones make a tone, or whole step, like C to D with the black key between them.
[Play C, C♯, D, then C to D] Now the opening question has its answer. Five pairs of neighbouring
letters (A–B, C–D, D–E, F–G, G–A) are a tone apart, and each of those gaps holds one note. B–C
and E–F are only a semitone apart, so there's no gap to fill. Seven letters plus five: twelve
notes.

**Try:** press Lift out the five. Then play ten rounds of _Semitone or tone?_ below.
**Notice:** a tone always skips over one key; a semitone never skips any.

_Toys: the tile row on the stage, with Lift out the five; and the Semitone or tone? ear game, a
card under the Notice in the step's own text, since it is played by ear and needs no stage
(DECISIONS 82; WEB-SPEC, Toys 4)._

## Step 5 of 6: Why does one key have two names?

The five need names, and they borrow them from their neighbours. A sharp (♯) means one semitone
up; a flat (♭) means one semitone down. So the key just above C is C♯, and the key just below D
is D♭: the same key, two names. Musicians call that enharmonic. Both are right: the music around
it decides which one is written. MercuryPitch's pitch line shows the sharp names, typed with a #,
like C#4.

> **Common mix-up:** "The black keys are the sharps and flats; the white keys are the plain notes."
> **Why it seems right:** every black key does carry a sharp name and a flat name.

**Predict:** one semitone up from E: a black key or a white key?
[ A black key ] [ A white key ]

(After a choice: Play E to F; on the stage, Sharp, Flat and Show the rare ones unlock.)

> **The evidence** (once E to F has played): one semitone up from E is F, a white key, so E♯ is F.
> **The better idea:** a sharp or a flat is a direction, not a colour: it can land on any key.

**Try:** switch the names to Both. Then tap F and press Flat.
**Notice:** one semitone down from F lands on E, a white key too: F♭ is another name for E.

_Toy: the tile row with the two-names toggle, opening on C♯ written above D♭ on one key under one
gold ring; on phones, a close-up of B to F (WEB-SPEC, Toys 1)._

## Step 6 of 6: How big is a semitone, exactly?

We measure it in cents. In the tuning MercuryPitch uses, every semitone is a hundred cents wide, so
the twelve semitones of an octave make 1,200 cents, and a tone is 200. MercuryPitch's Singing tab
measures in cents too. Its Input monitor names the nearest note, and a bar shows how far above or
below it you are, up to 50 cents either way. Fifty cents is halfway; past that, the next note is
nearer.

**Try:** drag the marker slowly from A3 up to A♯3.
**Notice:** at 50 cents the nearest note changes from A3 to A#3, just as the Input monitor does.

_Toy: the semitone ruler with cents._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Why twelve equal steps exactly double the frequency.** Each semitone multiplies the frequency
>   by the same number, the twelfth root of 2, about 1.0595. Twelve of those multiply to exactly 2:
>   an octave. So the gap in hertz grows as you go up (A3 to A♯3 is 13.08 Hz, A4 to A♯4 is
>   26.16 Hz), while every semitone is still 100 cents. Your ear hears multiplying, not adding
>   (Lesson 1).
> - **Where the cent comes from.** The cent comes from Alexander Ellis, who used it in 1885 to
>   compare the scales of different musical cultures. One cent multiplies the frequency by about
>   1.000578.
> - **A second meaning.** Singers also say a note is "sharp" when it's too high and "flat" when
>   it's too low: the same directions, for a miss of any size.
