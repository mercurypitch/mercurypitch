## Note values

11. Note values are fractions of each other: a whole note divides into two half notes, four quarter notes, eight eighth notes and sixteen sixteenth notes; the common values are the whole, half, quarter, eighth and sixteenth notes. So a quarter note is a quarter of a whole note, and four of them last as long as one whole note | R17-1 (Strong): OMT 2e 1.8, "a whole note divides into two half notes, four quarter notes, eight eighth notes, and so on"; values are "hierarchical", "their lengths are defined relative to one another" | carried; the four words' "Note value", Step 1, the reveal, quick-check question 1, the Wrap
12. How the shapes change as a value halves: the whole note is an open head with no stem; the half note adds a stem; the quarter note fills the head; the eighth note adds a flag, the sixteenth two. Filled heads always have stems, and flags go only on the stems of filled heads; a quarter note and an eighth note share a filled head and differ by the flag | R17-2 (Strong): OMT 2e 1.8, halving a value adds a stem, fills the notehead or adds a flag (RESEARCH's close paraphrase); read again on 1 November 2026, OMT 2e 1.8, "Note Values": "Unfilled noteheads may or may not have a stem, but filled noteheads always have stems." and "Flags are only added to the stems of filled noteheads." | carried and read; Step 1 ("an open head with no stem", "open heads with stems", "their heads filled in", "each with a flag", "with two flags") and its halvings, which write each shape as it sounds (66); 5b. ## The tempo turns values into seconds

16. A beat lasts 60 divided by the tempo in BPM, in seconds: at 60 BPM 1 s, at 120 BPM 0.5 s. With the quarter note on the beat, as in 4/4, a quarter note lasts 1 s at 60 BPM and 0.5 s at 120, a half note 2 s and 1 s, a whole note 4 s and 2 s | R17-7 (Strong): Lesson 8 FACTS 11 (carried: "a tempo of 60 beats per minute signifies one beat per second, while a tempo of 120 beats per minute is twice as rapid"); arithmetic (the simulation, its § 6: at 60 BPM 4, 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.25 s for 4 to a quarter of a beat; at 120 BPM 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25 and 0.125 s). ## Counting

20. In simple metre each beat gets a number, "1, 2, 3, 4"; the half-beat is counted "and"; the quarter-beats "e" and "a", so four sixteenths on a beat count "1 e and a", and "a" is said "uh". A bar of eighths counts "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" | R17-8 and R17-11 (Strong): OMT 2e 1.9, "In each measure, each quarter note gets a count, expressed with Arabic numerals" and "the divisions are counted aloud with the syllable "and," which is usually notated with the plus sign (+)"; read again on 1 November 2026: "Further subdivisions at the sixteenth-note level are counted as "e"" and ""a" (pronounced "uh")"; RCM 2019 p. 11 lets a singer "count the rhythm (1& 2&, etc.)" | carried and read; Step 3, the Wrap, the checkpoint's count. The page writes "and" as a word, where OMT writes "+"; Lesson 8 counted "1 2 3 4" (Lesson 8 FACTS 16)
21. A note longer than a beat holds its count over the beats it lasts, and beats that are not sounded, because of a rest, a tie or a dot, are not counted aloud; Open Music Theory writes those counts in parentheses, which the page calls brackets, the British word. So a half note on beat 3 counts "3 (4)", and quarter, quarter rest, quarter, quarter rest counts "1 (2) 3 (4)" | OMT 2e 1.9, "Counting in Simple Meter", read on 1 November 2026: "When notes last longer than one beat (such as a half or whole note in this example), the count is held over multiple beats."
