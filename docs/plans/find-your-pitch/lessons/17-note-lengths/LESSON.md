## Step 1 of 5: What is a quarter note a quarter of?

A whole note: an open head with no stem, which in 4/4 fills a bar of four beats. Halve it for two
half notes, open heads with stems. Halve again for four quarter notes, their heads filled in; then
eight eighth notes, each with a flag, and sixteen sixteenths, with two flags. Flags in one beat join
into a beam. In 4/4 the top 4 counts four beats a bar, and the bottom 4 says a quarter note gets the
beat. In Britain these are the semibreve, minim, crotchet, quaver and semiquaver.

**Try:** play the halvings, then choose a beat and press Halve or Join.
**Notice:** however you split it, the bar still lasts four beats.

_Toy: Lesson 8's beat pads, written on one line, with Play the halvings, Halve, Join and the
stopwatch strip._

## Step 2 of 5: So what sets how long a note lasts?

Tavi's guess was half right. At one tempo, every quarter note lasts as long as the next. But a note
value is a share of a whole note, and the tempo sets the clock. In Lesson 8, 60 BPM meant one beat a
second and 120 meant two. So in 4/4 at 60 BPM, a quarter note lasts 1 second and a half note 2
seconds. At 120 BPM, they last half a second and 1 second. Change the tempo and every note stretches
or shrinks together, while the page stays the same.

**Try:** play the bar at 60 BPM, then drag Tempo to 120 and play it again.
**Notice:** every stopwatch reading halves, and the notes never change.

> **Common mix-up:** "A note value is a length of time."
> **Why it seems right:** at any one tempo, every quarter note does last the same time.
> **The evidence:** the same written bar lasts half as long at 120 BPM as at 60. And two eighths and
> a quarter at 60 BPM last exactly as long as two quarters and a half note at 120: half a second,
> half a second, then a second. [Play both]
> **The better idea:** a note value is a share of a whole note. The tempo turns the shares into
> seconds.

_Toy: the written bar, two quarters and a half note, with Tempo, the stopwatch strip and Play both._

## Step 3 of 5: How do you count the short ones?

Lesson 8 counted the beats: 1 2 3 4. Shorter notes get words between the numbers. Eighth notes split
each beat in two, counted "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". Sixteenths split it in four: "1 e and a", with
"a" said "uh". A note longer than a beat holds its count. Counts you don't say are written in
brackets, so a half note on beat 3 counts "3 (4)". The second bar counts "1, 2 and, 3 e and a, 4".

**Try:** switch on Count, play the bars and say each count as it lights. Play them again: the counts
fade.
**Notice:** the numbers always land on the clicks. "And", "e" and "a" fall between them.

_Toy: two bars played in turn, eight eighths, then a quarter, two eighths, four sixteenths and a
quarter, with Count._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Takadimi.** Some teachers count with syllables instead: "ta" on each beat, "di" halfway
>   through it, and "ta ka di mi" for four sixteenths. The 1996 paper that set it out notes that the
>   syllables are like those of North Indian tabla playing.
> - **"1 e and a".** The same paper calls it the common system, one that grew up as music was brought
>   into American state schools.

## Step 4 of 5: What does a silence look like?

Every note value has a rest: a written silence just as long. There's a whole rest, a half rest, and
so on down to a sixteenth rest. The beat doesn't stop for a rest, so the count carries on in your
head, in brackets as before: quarter, quarter rest, quarter, quarter rest counts "1 (2) 3 (4)". A
half note on beat 1 counts "1 (2)" too. The difference is what you hear on beat 2: the half note
keeps sounding, and the rest is silent.

**Try:** tap a beat to turn its note into a rest, and listen.
**Notice:** the clicks carry on through every silence.

_Toy: the pads with Note · Rest on each beat and the bar written above them; under it, the five
rests, each played between two notes._

## Step 5 of 5: How do you write a note longer than its shape?

Three beats has no shape of its own: a half note lasts two, a whole note four. A dot after a note
makes it half as long again, so a dotted half lasts three beats and a dotted quarter one and a half.
A tie is a curve that joins two notes of the same pitch into one sound, as long as both together. A
tie can even cross a bar line. Pitch matters for a tie, so the bars move onto the staff from Lesson
15, with every note on A.

**Try:** press Dot, then Tie, and listen each time.
**Notice:** tied notes sound once, for both their lengths.

_Toy: two bars on the five-line staff, in your clef, with Dot, Tie and the stopwatch strip._

## Wrap

- Note values are shares of a whole note: halves, quarters, eighths and sixteenths. Each has a rest
  just as long.
- The tempo turns the shares into seconds. In 4/4 at 60 BPM a quarter note lasts 1 second; at 120
  BPM, half a second.
- Count the beats with numbers and the halves between them with "and". A dot makes a note half as
  long again, and a tie joins two notes into one sound.
