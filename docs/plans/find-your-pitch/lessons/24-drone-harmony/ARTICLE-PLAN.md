## Four words you'll meet

- **Drone**: one low note held under the music. [Play: A3 drone]
- **Beats**: a regular throb in loudness when two notes are close but not the same. [Play: 220
  against 222, two a second] (R1)
- **Pure**: an interval tuned to an exact ratio, so its harmonics meet and nothing beats: 3:2 for
  the fifth, 5:4 for the major third. [Play: pure fifth] (R3)
- **Equal-tempered**: the piano's tuning, every semitone the same size. [Play: the piano's fifth]
- Words: 90.

## Step 1 of 4: What is that throbbing?

- Point: two close notes beat; the rate is their difference; it slows as they meet, and stops in
  tune (R1). Sound first, then the picture: the glow breathes at the beat rate.
- Examples, on the A3 drone: 222 Hz (2 a second), 221 (1), 220.5 (one every 2 s), 220 (still).
  Then the octave: A4 at 441 against the drone beats once a second, through the drone's second
  harmonic, 440 (R2).
- Toy (signature, first state): the drone band with a draggable second tone; the glow at the exact
  beat rate; a readout "beats a second".
- Try: drag the top note until the glow stops. Notice: the slower it breathes, the closer you are.
- Facts: R1, R2. Words: 140.

## Step 2 of 4: Do notes that aren't the same beat too?

- Predict (written out): "Merc plays the piano's fifth above the drone, then the piano's major
  third. Which one beats faster?" [ The fifth ] [ The major third ]. Reveal: "The major third, much
  faster. Above A3 the piano's third beats almost nine times a second and its fifth less than once.
  Tune them pure, 3:2 and 5:4, and both go still (R3, R4). (The fifth is the wider leap, which made
  it a fair guess.)"
- Point: notes carry harmonics (Lesson 13). In a pure fifth, the top note's second harmonic and the
  drone's third land on the same frequency, 660 Hz; the piano's fifth puts them 0.75 Hz apart, so
  they beat slowly. The piano's third is 0.8 % wide (R3), so its harmonics beat fast. Only the pure
  intervals are still.
- Examples, on A3 (220 Hz): ET fifth E4 329.63 (0.745 a second); pure fifth 330.00 (still); ET
  major third C♯4 277.18 (8.73 a second); pure third 275.00 (still) (R4).
- Toy: do, mi and so beads over the drone; tap so or mi to hear it equal-tempered or pure; the glow
  keeps a slow pulse at the piano's fifth, a fast one at its third, and stills only at the pure ones.
- Go deeper: "The formula: beats a second = 2 × 329.63 − 3 × 220 for the fifth, 4 × 277.18 − 5 ×
  220 for the third. The rate grows with the drone, so on a higher drone everything beats faster
  (R4)."
- Facts: R2, R3, R4, R10. Words: 230.

## Step 3 of 4: Which third should I sing?

- Tavi: "So should I sing the piano's third, or the pure one?"
- Point: either lands in this lesson's band. A pure major third is 386 cents, 14 below the piano's
  400 (R3); the band reaches 35 either side (R10). One a cappella quintet, measured over four months,
  sang major thirds that averaged 392 cents, between the two, with examples of both (R8). The page
  does not choose for you.
- Examples: mi above the A3 drone at 400 and at 386 cents, both landing in the band.
- Toy: the band around mi, with two ticks, "piano" and "pure".
- What the app does: Drone Intonation scores against the piano's notes, so a pure third scores
  about 79 there (R11). Said once, plainly.
- Facts: R3, R8, R10, R11. Words: 130.

## Step 4 of 4: To hold my part, should I shut out the other one?

- Common mix-up card (shown half right):
  - Belief: "To hold your part, shut out the other voice."
  - Why it seems right: in one study, singers sang their own notes more accurately alone, and
    singing a different part from a partner was harder than singing the same one (R7).
  - The evidence: the same singers, hearing each other, adjusted "to mitigate a partner's error and
    preserve harmonic intervals"; together, their harmony was closer than when each sang alone (R7).
    The quintet's rehearsals included "encouraging each other to listen in a more focused way" (R8).
  - The better idea: listen to the other part. The beats tell you where you are.
- Point: listening needs something to hear. A vibrato smears the beats (R6), so against a drone a
  straight note shows them best. Lesson 22's point still holds: a vibrato is never wrong. **[coach]**
- Toy (signature, last state): a second singer's chrome line joins over the drone, Lesson 26's world.
- Facts: R6, R7, R8. Words: 180.

## Quick check

1. "Two notes, 220 Hz and 223 Hz, sound together. How many times a second do they beat?"
   - 3 **[correct]** · "Beats a second are the difference: 223 − 220."
   - 443 · "That's the two added. Beats come from the difference. Try Step 1 again."
   - None: they're too close to hear · "Close notes beat most clearly; only the same note is still."
2. "Above a drone, the piano plays a fifth, then a major third. What do you hear?"
   - Both beat, the third much faster **[correct]** · "The piano's third is 0.8 % wide; its fifth
     only slightly narrow."
   - Only the fifth beats · "The fifth beats slowly, under once a second; the third beats much
     faster. Try Step 2 again."
   - Neither beats: the piano is in tune · "In tune by the piano's own system, but only pure
     intervals are still."
3. "You hold a harmony note while another singer sings a different part. What helps keep the
   harmony together?"
   - Listening to the other part **[correct]** · "Singers who heard each other kept their harmony
     closer."
   - Blocking the other part out · "Alone, your own note may be closer; together, listening keeps
     the harmony. Try Step 4 again."
   - Singing louder than them · "Loudness isn't tuning: listening is."
4. "Listen. [Play] Is this pair beating or still?" (A3 with a pure fifth, 330 Hz.)
   - Still **[correct]** · "A pure fifth: the harmonics meet exactly."
