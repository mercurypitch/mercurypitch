## Four words you'll meet

- **Cadence**: the way a phrase ends, in its tune and its chords, like punctuation (R2). [Play: bars
  7 and 8]
- **Interrupted cadence**: V going to vi instead of I (American books: deceptive). [Play] (R1)
- **Plagal cadence**: IV to I, the "Amen" ending. [Play] (R1)
- **Form**: the order of a song's sections, written as letters: A A B A. [Show the letters] (R7)
- Words: 70.

## Step 1 of 4: What makes a phrase sound finished?

- Point: Lesson 14's two endings, now on the staff. V to I, landing on do, is a perfect cadence: it
  sounds final. A phrase that stops on V is an imperfect cadence: it sounds unfinished, so the song
  goes on (R1, R3, R6). In the lesson's song, bar 4 is imperfect (A over D) and bars 7 to 8 perfect.
- Names: American books say authentic for perfect and half for imperfect. Careful: an American
  "imperfect authentic cadence" is a kind of V to I, not the British imperfect cadence (R1, R3).
- Examples: bars 1 to 4, then bars 5 to 8.
- Toy (signature): the two-part staff; a tag appears at each phrase end naming its cadence.
- Try: play each phrase and watch the tag. Notice: the unfinished one ends on V.
- Go deeper: "Not every recording ends on a cadence: many fade out instead (R11)."
- Facts: R1, R3, R6, R11. Words: 170.

## Step 2 of 4: How can a song fake its ending?

- Common mix-up card (shown half right):
  - Belief: "When a phrase sounds finished, the song is over."
  - Why it seems right: a perfect cadence does sound final, and a song can end on one (R3, R4).
  - The evidence: cadences end phrases all through a song, not only its last (R2). And the
    interrupted cadence, V to vi, sounds like an ending and swerves: an encyclopedia calls it weak
    because of its "hanging" feeling (R1).
  - The better idea: a cadence is punctuation. A full stop may end a sentence, not the story.
- The fourth: IV to I, the plagal cadence, named the Amen cadence from hymns (R1). Play the song's
  plagal tag, sung "A-men".
- Examples: bars 7 to 8 three ways: V–I (perfect), V–vi (interrupted), then the IV–I tag.
- Toy: three buttons, Perfect · Interrupted · Plagal, each replaying bars 7 to 8 its way.
- Facts: R1, R2, R3, R4. Words: 200.

## Step 3 of 4: How is a song put together?

- Predict (written out): "Merc plays the whole song: A, A, then a new section, then A again. Where
  does the new section end?" [ On V, unfinished ] [ On I, finished ]. Reveal: "On V. The new
  section, the bridge, ends unfinished, and the song needs A to come back (R8, R10). (Every A ends on
  I, which made 'finished' a fair guess.)"
- Point: songs are built from sections, labelled with capital letters: A for a strophe, B for a
  bridge (R7). A A B A is so common in older songs that it has a second name, 32-bar song form: four
  sections of eight bars (R8). Another plan: verses change their words, and the chorus keeps its
  words and the title (R9).
- Examples: the 32-bar song, played with section blocks; then a verse-chorus diagram (V C V C, no
  sound needed).
- Toy: form letters gather above the sections as they play.
- Go deeper: "Listen to 'I Want to Hold Your Hand' (the Beatles, 1963): two As, a bridge, then A
  again (R8)." Named, not played.
- Facts: R7, R8, R9, R10. Words: 220.

## Step 4 of 4: How do I read my part?

- Point: a two-part staff: upper part in the treble clef, lower in the bass clef (Lesson 15). One
  sharp, F♯: G major, do on G (Lesson 21). Count "1, 2, 3, 4" (Lesson 17): two notes a bar, a whole
  note at each cadence. Read your own clef: higher voices the upper part, lower voices the lower.
  The two parts move a third apart, an octave away, like Lesson 26's harmony (R13).
- Examples: each part alone, then both; "see it on the pitch line" toggle.
- Toy: the staff with a cursor at crotchet = 72; tap a part to hear it alone.
- Tavi's re-explanation: "So my part is a tune of its own, and the other part makes it a harmony."
- Facts: R13; Lessons 15, 17, 21 and 26 (Arc C's packages). Words: 150.
