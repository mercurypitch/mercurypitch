## Where the sources and the card disagree (read first)

1. **"Songs usually end on a perfect cadence" was not found in any source read.** OMT says
   authentic cadences "sound conclusive" and the perfect authentic cadence is "the strongest kind of
   cadence" for its "sense of finality" (R3, R4); its popular-music chapters say many recordings end
   with a fade-out (R11). The plan says a perfect cadence sounds final and is the textbook way to
   close, and never "usually".
2. **Plagal and interrupted are not phrase endings in every classification.** The Wikipedia article
   Lesson 14 cites says "Typically, phrases end on authentic or half cadences, and the terms plagal
   and deceptive refer to motion that avoids or follows a phrase-ending cadence" (R1); OMT files
   plagal motion under prolongation (§ 4.10, title only read). The card's four named cadences stay,
   as British teaching names them, but the plan presents perfect and imperfect as the two phrase
   endings and the other two as "one that swerves" and "one that adds an Amen".
3. **British "imperfect" is not American "imperfect".** In British usage an imperfect cadence ends
   on V (American: half cadence); in American usage an "imperfect authentic cadence" is a V–I that
   misses one of two conditions (R1, R3). Lesson 14 wrote the British names only, never "imperfect
   authentic" (Lesson 14 FACTS 44). The plan leads with the British names and gives each American
   name once, with that warning.
4. **The benchmark's T22 and T31 sources were not re-read** (musictheory.net, Coursera M3 and M5,
   Ableton's Song structure). OMT's cadence chapter and three popular-form chapters, read in full,
   stand in for them; ABRSM's Grade 5 list of cadences was found only on tutoring sites and is
   **not verified**.
5. **The two-part checkpoint cannot be scored with today's kit**: the kit reads one sung note per
   take, plays one line per call, and records nothing (R14). The plan says what works today.

## Claims

### Cadences and their names

**R1. Four cadences, named in British and American usage: perfect (authentic) V–I; imperfect (half)
ending on V; plagal IV–I; interrupted (deceptive) V–vi.**
- Source: Wikipedia, "Cadence", revision 1373604555 (6 September 2026, the revision Lesson 14's
  FACTS cites), raw wikitext, § Nomenclature across the world, a table citing Grove Music Online
  (Rockstro 2001): "authentic cadence | perfect cadence | ... | V → I or V7 → I"; "half cadence |
  imperfect cadence | ... | I, II, IV or VI → V"; "plagal cadence | plagal cadence | ... | IV → I";
  "deceptive cadence | interrupted cadence | ... | V → vi or V7 → vi". § Common classifications:
  "Typically, phrases end on authentic or half cadences, and the terms plagal and deceptive refer to
  motion that avoids or follows a phrase-ending cadence." § Plagal cadence: "A plagal cadence is a
  cadence from IV to I. It is also known as the Amen cadence because of its frequent setting to the
  text "Amen" in hymns." § Deceptive cadence: "Also known as an interrupted or false cadence, the
  deceptive cadence is a cadence from V to any chord other than the tonic (I), usually the
  submediant (VI)" (citing Latham 2002, p. 193); "This is considered a weak cadence because of the
  "hanging" (suspended) feeling it invokes."
- Read: the article at that revision, in full; Grove and Latham not read. Strength: Moderate (an
  encyclopedia article citing Grove; consistent with OMT for the American names).

**R2. A cadence ends a phrase, like punctuation, and helps establish the key.** OMT § 4.01
"Introduction to Harmony, Cadences, and Phrase Endings", § Introduction to Cadences: "cadences—
melodic and harmonic patterns that create goals, kind of like punctuation marks in literary
sentences. They're important not only because they can help you determine phrase endings, but also
because they help establish a key." Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R3. Authentic cadences sound conclusive; half cadences sound inconclusive.** OMT § 4.01:
"Authentic Cadences (they sound conclusive!)": "An authentic cadence occurs when the harmonic
progression V–I (or V–i in minor) marks the end of a phrase"; a perfect authentic cadence needs "Do
... in the soprano over the tonic chord" and "Both V and I ... in root position"; otherwise it is an
imperfect authentic cadence. "Half Cadences (they sound inconclusive!)": "A half cadence (HC) occurs
when a phrase ends on V". Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R4. Cadences differ in strength.** OMT § 4.01, § Cadential Strength and the IAC: "PACs are the
strongest kind of cadence available to a composer because of the sense of finality they can create,
and HCs are the weakest kind of cadence because of their unfinished sound." Read: in full. Strength:
Strong (within this style). The plan uses "sounds final" and "sounds unfinished", not "strongest".

**R5. Hearing cadences: mark the points of rest, check the chords, listen for a new start.** OMT §
4.01, § Hearing Cadences: "Mark potential cadence points; Analyze the harmonies involved; Listen for
a sense of beginning after each potential cadence point"; "unless an excerpt's tempo is quite slow,
it's not common for phrases to be two measures long." Read: in full. Strength: Practitioner (a
method). Used for the toy's three-step "find the cadence".

### Song form

**R7. Sections are labelled with capital letters by function; phrases with lower-case letters by
their music.** OMT § 7.4 "Introduction to Form in Popular Music", § Analytical Notation: "A section
that functions as a strophe is labeled with an A; A section that functions as a bridge is B; A verse
is labeled V; A chorus is labeled C"; "Phrases are labeled with bolded lowercase letters according
to their musical content". § Sections within Pop Forms: "a section typically spans between 8 and 24
bars and includes 2–4 phrases". Read: in full. Strength: Strong (for this text's conventions; it
bases its terms on Summach 2012, not read).

**R8. AABA, or 32-bar song form: two strophes, a contrasting bridge, and the strophe again.** OMT §
7.5 "AABA Form and Strophic Form": "AABA form, also called 32-bar song form because in earlier
“Golden Age” songs that make use of this structure, each section is eight measures long"; its
example is the Beatles' "I Want to Hold Your Hand" (1963), whose bridge comes "at 0:52". § Strophe
(A): it provides "a point at which the song might satisfyingly end"; § Bridge (B): "Bridges are
contrasting sections"; "Bridge sections tend to emphasize non-tonic harmonies and commonly end on
dominant harmony." Read: in full. Strength: Strong. The Beatles song is named in Go deeper only, as
a listening suggestion, never played (rights).

**R9. Verse-chorus form: verses change their words; the chorus keeps its words and carries the
title.** OMT § 7.6 "Verse-Chorus Form": "Verse sections are lyric-variant and often contain lyrics
that advance the narrative"; "Chorus sections are lyric-invariant and contain the primary lyrical
material of the song (the title lyrics and/or lyrical hook)"; "A verse-chorus song may not have a
bridge at all." Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R10. A bridge's job is to make the return of A wanted.** OMT § 7.5, § Bridge (B), quoting Summach
(2012, p. 79): a bridge "“must be followed by [the primary section] in order for its function to be
satisfied”". Read: in OMT. Strength: Moderate. Used in the step on form: the bridge ends on V, an
imperfect cadence, and A comes back.

**R11. Many recordings end with a fade-out, not a cadence.** OMT § 7.5, § Outros: "Outros function
as a transition from song back to silence, and thus decrease energy. Often this is accomplished in
the recording studio by way of a fadeout." § 7.6: in "Livin' on a Prayer" "a fadeout ends the
song". Read: in full. Strength: Moderate. Used as the honest exception in Step 1's Go deeper.

## Interesting findings

1. **One word, two meanings.** A British "imperfect cadence" ends on V; an American "imperfect
   authentic cadence" goes V to I (R1, R3). Possible: Step 1's aside.
2. **The Amen cadence.** IV to I is called the Amen cadence for its setting of "Amen" at the end of
   hymns (R1). Possible: Step 2's example, sung "A-men".
3. **The swerve is the cadence that isn't.** The interrupted cadence goes to vi instead of I, and is
   "considered a weak cadence because of the "hanging" (suspended) feeling it invokes" (R1). Possible:
   the cold open's reveal.
4. **32 bars, four sections.** AABA got its other name, 32-bar song form, because in older songs each
   of its four sections is eight bars (R8): the lesson's own song can be built the same way.
   Possible: Step 3.
5. **Many songs never cadence at the end.** In the recording studio a song often fades out instead
   (R11). Possible: Go deeper, the honest exception to "ends on a perfect cadence".
