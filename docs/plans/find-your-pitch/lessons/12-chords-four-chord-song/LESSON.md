## Four words you'll meet

- **Diatonic**: made only from the notes of one key's scale. In C major, that's the white keys.
  [Play: C major's seven chords]
- **Roman numeral**: a chord's name from the scale degree it's built on: I on do, V on so. [Play: I,
  then V]
- **Diminished**: a triad of two minor thirds, 3 and then 3 semitones: B, D, F. [Play: B, D and F]
- **Augmented**: a triad of two major thirds, 4 and then 4 semitones: C, E, G♯. [Play: C, E and G♯]

## Step 1 of 5: Where do a key's chords come from?

Lesson 4 numbered the notes of the major scale 1 to 7, do to ti. Lesson 10 stacked thirds into a
triad. Take any note of the scale as a chord's root, its bottom note. Add the scale's notes a third
and a fifth above it, skipping a note each time. On do, that's C, E, G. On re it's D, F, A, and on
fa it's F, A, C. Seven notes give seven chords. In C major, every one uses only white keys: they're
the key's diatonic chords.

**Try:** tap each chip in turn, from do to ti.
**Notice:** no room ever reaches a black key. Every corner is a note of C major.

_Toy: the ring of rooms, in C major, with its seven chord chips._

## Step 2 of 5: How do Roman numerals name them?

Name each chord by its root's place in the scale, in Roman numerals. Capitals or small letters show
its quality: capitals for major, small letters for minor. C major's seven read I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi
and vii°. The small circle marks the one diminished chord, on ti. No chord built from a major scale
is augmented. The numerals belong to the key, not to the letters. In G major, I is G, B, D, and V is
D, F♯, A, with the key's one sharp.

**Try:** switch the key from C to G, then tap I, IV and V again.
**Notice:** every room turns round the ring together, and each keeps its shape and its numeral.

_Toy: the ring of rooms, with the key switch C · G._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Plain numbers.** Not every text writes capitals and small letters. Ableton's free lessons
>   call chords by plain numbers, such as "the 1 and 5 chords", and some theorists write every
>   numeral in capitals.

## Step 3 of 5: Why is the chord on la minor?

On la, the sixth degree, the chord is la, do and mi: A, C, E. A up to C is 3 semitones, and C up
to E is 4. That's Lesson 10's minor shape, and its numeral is vi: A minor. In Lesson 10, the minor
chord's root was do: do-me-so. Here its root is la, and we're still in C major. Do stays on C, and
all three notes are the scale's own. Compared with I (C, E, G), it keeps C and E and swaps G for A.

**Try:** switch the names to do-re-mi, then play I, then vi.
**Notice:** one corner slides from G to A. The new room is the old one's mirror image.

_Toy: the ring of rooms, with Play I, then vi, and the names switch._

## Step 4 of 5: Which four chords go round and round?

Play I, V, vi and IV, then go back to I and round again. In C major that's C, G, A minor and F,
built on do, so, la and fa. A series of chords played in order is a progression, and this one is a
loop. Ableton's free lessons call it 1-5-6-4. Open Music Theory, a free textbook, lists it among
its four-chord loops. Its four chords hold all seven notes of the scale between them. From V to vi,
all three notes move up a step.

**Try:** play the loop, then play its roots alone.
**Notice:** the roots go down to so, up to la, then down to fa.

_Toy: the ring of rooms, with Play the loop and Play the roots._

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Start on vi.** Open Music Theory gives the same loop starting from vi, vi–IV–I–V: the same
>   four chords in the same order. It calls the turn that starts on I "exceedingly common" too.
> - **Another loop.** The same four chords make other loops. The one the textbook calls doo-wop
>   runs I–vi–IV–V, and it says that loop was "common in 1960s pop music".

## Step 5 of 5: If the chords match, is it the same song?

Orange Merc: "Same chords, same song. Isn't that how it works?"

Back to the cold open's two tunes, over the same four chords. Tune 1 walks down, mostly by steps,
in even notes. Tune 2 leaps between corners in a long-short-long rhythm. Both land mostly on
corners, and they're still two tunes. Now hear tune 2 with even notes: the same notes over the same
chords, with only its rhythm changed.

> **Common mix-up:** "Songs with the same chords are the same song."
> **Why it seems right:** one open textbook calls I, IV, V and vi "probably the most common chords
> in all of pop music", and says the loops made from them "all sound somewhat similar".
> **The evidence:** a song has two building blocks, its chords and its tune. Ableton's lessons say
> melodies "define the ‘identity’ of the song in many styles of music": "if you ask someone to sing
> a particular song, they'll usually sing the melody". This lesson's two tunes share every chord,
> in the same order, and they're still two tunes.
> **The better idea:** the chords are the rooms; the song is the walk through them, its tune in its
> rhythm.

**Try:** play tune 1, then tune 2, then tune 2 with even notes.
**Notice:** the chords never change. Only the tune's path and its rhythm do.

_Toy: the ring of rooms with the tune toy: Tune 1 · Tune 2 · Even notes._

## Wrap

- Stack the scale's notes a third and a fifth above each degree of a major scale and you get its
  seven chords. In C major they're all on white keys.
- A Roman numeral names a chord by its root's degree. Capitals or small letters show its quality:
  I, IV and V are major, ii, iii and vi minor, and vii° diminished.
- I–V–vi–IV is C, G, A minor and F in C major. Two songs can share it and still differ: the tune,
  in its rhythm, can make the song.
