## Step 1 of 4: How do you build a seventh chord?

- Point: stack one more third in the key on any triad (R1). On do: C E G B; on so: G B D F. Name it
  by its triad and its seventh: C E G B is a major triad with a major seventh (C to B, 11 semitones),
  a major seventh chord; G B D F is a major triad with a minor seventh (G to F, 10), a dominant
  seventh chord (R2).
- Examples: C4 E4 G4 B4; G3 B3 D4 F4; each note by note, then together.
- Toy (signature): Lesson 12's rooms, each able to grow a fourth bead; the semitone count from root
  to seventh shown.
- Try: grow I, then V. Notice: both triads are major; the sevenths differ by a semitone.
- Facts: R1, R2. Words: 140.

## Step 2 of 4: Does an extra note just make a chord richer?

- Common mix-up card (half right):
  - Belief: "Adding a note just makes a chord richer."
  - Why it seems right: on I, it does: one textbook suggests hearing the major seventh chord as
    "happy and jazzy" (R3).
  - The evidence: on V, the same added third sounds "unresolved", "like they strongly need to move"
    (R3), because the seventh adds dissonance, which "strengthens the pull of the V chord toward I"
    (R4).
  - The better idea: what a seventh does depends on the chord it joins. On V, it pulls.
- Examples: C E G B held, then G B D F held, then G B D F to C E G.
- Toy: the I and V rooms side by side, each with its seventh.
- Facts: R3, R4. Words: 150.

## Step 3 of 4: Why does the dominant seventh pull home?

- Predict (written out): "In G, B, D and F, two notes are six semitones apart: Lesson 23's leaning
  interval. Which two?" [ B and F ] [ G and D ]. Reveal: "B and F: ti and fa, the tritone (R5).
  G to D is seven, a perfect fifth, which sits still. (G and D are the chord's outside notes, which
  made them a fair guess.)"
- Point: in V7 to I, ti rises a semitone to do and fa falls a semitone to mi: B3 and F4 close inwards
  to C4 and E4 (R5). The tritone resolves to a major third. In minor, V takes ti, not te, so the
  pull is the same (Lesson 25; R5).
- Examples: B3+F4 alone, then to C4+E4; the full V7–I.
- Toy: across Lesson 2's ring, the tritone's diameter lights between ti and fa and folds inwards to
  do and mi.
- Tavi's re-explanation: "So the note we added makes the tritone, and the tritone wants to close."
- Facts: R4, R5, R7. Words: 180.

## Step 4 of 4: Is a chord named after its bottom note?

- Predict (written out): "Merc plays C, E and G, then moves C up an octave: E, G, C, with E at the
  bottom. What is the chord called now?" [ Still C major ] [ E minor ]. Reveal: "Still C major. Its
  root is still C; only the bass changed. With its third, E, at the bottom it's in first inversion,
  written C/E (R6). (The bottom note is the one you hear first, which made 'E minor' a fair guess.)"
- Point: root position, first inversion (third at the bottom), second inversion (fifth at the
  bottom); a seventh chord has a third inversion, its seventh at the bottom (R6). The name stays;
  "the bass voice of the chord is NOT the same thing as the chord's root" (R6).
- Examples: C E G, E G C, G C E; G B D F with F at the bottom (G7/F).
- Toy: a chord's beads rotate bottom to top, the name staying; on the staff, the noteheads restack.
- Facts: R6. Words: 170.

> **Go deeper**
>
> - **Figures.** Older music writes inversions as numbers under the bass, the intervals above it: 6
>   for a first inversion, 6 over 4 for a second (R6). Musicians shortened them "to save time and
>   supplies (paper and ink were very expensive before the industrial revolution)".
> - **No fifth.** When every note of V7 follows its tendency, the I chord it lands on has "three
>   roots and one third—no fifth" (R5).
> - **Other names.** Open Music Theory calls the dominant seventh a "major-minor" seventh chord, a
>   major triad with a minor seventh (R2).
