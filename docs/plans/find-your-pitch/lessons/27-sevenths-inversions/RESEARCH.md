## Claims

### Building seventh chords

**R1. A seventh chord stacks one more third on a triad: root, third, fifth and seventh.**
- Source: OMT § 1.17 "Seventh Chords": "Like triads, the notes of a seventh chord can always be
  arranged in thirds, on adjacent lines or spaces of the staff"; "the lowest note of a seventh chord
  stacked in closed spacing is called the root, and the other notes are named for their generic
  intervals above the root ... the third, the fifth, and the seventh."
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

**R2. A seventh chord's quality is its triad's quality plus its seventh's. A major triad with a major
seventh is a major seventh chord; a major triad with a minor seventh is a dominant seventh chord.**
- Source: OMT § 1.17, § Seventh Chord Qualities and Nomenclature: "These qualities are determined by
  two factors: The quality of the triad ... The quality of the seventh from the root to the
  seventh"; "major-major seventh chord = major triad + major seventh"; "major-minor seventh chord =
  major triad + minor seventh"; "The major-major seventh chord is also often called the major
  seventh chord. The major-minor seventh chord is also often called the dominant seventh chord."
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong. Arithmetic: in C major, I with its seventh is C E G B (C to B 11
  semitones, a major seventh); V with its seventh is G B D F (G to F 10, a minor seventh). Stacking
  a third in the key on D gives F (Lesson 26: two letters up, in the key), so V7 in C is G B D F.

**R3. Textbooks hear the dominant seventh as unresolved.** OMT § 1.17, § Listening to Seventh
Chords: "You might think of major-major seventh chords as sounding “happy and jazzy,” major-minor
seventh chords as sounding “unresolved” (like they strongly need to move to another chord)". Read:
in full. Strength: Practitioner (a listening tip, as Lesson 10's "happy and sad" mnemonic was). Used
in the misconception's "why it seems right" and evidence, as a textbook's suggestion.

### Why V7 pulls

**R4. The added seventh, fa, makes V a dominant seventh, and its dissonance strengthens V's pull to
I.** OMT § 4.02 "Strengthening Endings with V7": "the V chord contains an extra note, fa, that
transforms it from a major triad into a dominant seventh chord. Since the seventh adds dissonance
(and therefore instability) to the chord, it strengthens the pull of the V chord toward I." Read: in
full. Strength: Strong.

**R5. In V7 to I, ti tends to rise to do and the seventh, fa, to fall by step to mi; a complete V7
resolving this way gives a I with no fifth.**
- Sources: Wharton & Shaffer (eds.), Open Music Theory 1st ed., § 2.12 (LibreTexts, last modified
  16 July 2023), read in full for Lesson 23 (its RESEARCH R9): "when ti appears it tends to be
  followed by do in the same voice ... this tendency is strongest when ti occurs in a
  dominant-functioning chord"; the dissonant degrees "include the seventh of every seventh chord",
  which have "a strong tendency to resolve down by step". OMT 2e § 4.02, § The Default Resolution of
  V7 to I: "When the notes of a complete V7 chord resolve according to their typical tendencies, we
  end up with a tonic triad that has three roots and one third—no fifth"; "If you are writing in
  minor, remember to use ti, not te, in V7 to make the chord major!"; § Leading-Tone Drop: in an
  inner voice ti may instead leap down to sol.
- Read: both in full. Strength: Strong. Arithmetic: ti and fa, B and F, are six semitones apart, the
  tritone (Lesson 23); B3 and F4 (a diminished fifth) moving to C4 and E4 close inwards to a major
  third.

### Inversions

**R6. A triad with its root in the bass is in root position; with its third in the bass, first
inversion; with its fifth, second inversion. A seventh chord adds third inversion, the seventh in
the bass. The bass is not the root.**
- Source: OMT § 1.18 "Inversion and Figured Bass", § Triadic Inversion and Figures: "when the third
  appears in the bass, we say the triad is in first inversion, and when the fifth appears in the
  bass we say the triad is in second inversion"; "It is important to note that the bass voice of the
  chord is NOT the same thing as the chord's root. The root of an A major triad is always A,
  regardless of whether the triad is in root position, first inversion, or second inversion"; in
  chord symbols an inverted A major with E in the bass is "A/E". § Seventh Chord Inversion and
  Figures: "When the chordal seventh of a seventh chord is in the bass it is in third inversion."
  Figures: "First inversion triads are abbreviated with the superscript number “6,” while a second
  inversion triad keeps its full figures" (a fourth and a sixth above the bass); seventh chords in
  full, "a third, fifth, and a sixth" (first), "a third, a fourth, and a sixth" (second), "a second,
  a fourth, and a sixth" (third), with a root-position seventh "abbreviated with just the number
  “7”" (the other short forms are images; the standard 6/5, 4/3 and 4/2 follow from the full
  figures but were not read as text).
- Read: in full. Strength: Strong.

## Interesting findings

1. **The pull is Lesson 23's tritone.** Add fa to G, B, D and the chord holds B and F, the six
   semitones that split the octave; they close inwards to C and E (R4, R5). Possible: Step 3's
   reveal.
2. **Resolving V7 the textbook way loses a note.** Each note following its tendency leaves the I
   chord with "three roots and one third—no fifth" (R5). Possible: Go deeper.
3. **One added note, two moods.** A textbook suggests hearing the major seventh chord as "happy and
   jazzy" and the dominant seventh as "unresolved" (R3): the same added third, a semitone apart.
   Possible: the misconception's evidence.
4. **A chord keeps its name upside down.** C, E and G with E at the bottom is still C major; OMT
   writes it C/E, and stresses that "the bass voice of the chord is NOT the same thing as the chord's
   root" (R6). Possible: Step 4's predict.
5. **Paper was dear.** OMT says musicians shortened figured bass "to save time and supplies (paper
   and ink were very expensive before the industrial revolution)" (§ 1.18). Possible: Go deeper.
