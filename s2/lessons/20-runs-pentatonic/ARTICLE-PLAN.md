## Step 1 of 4: What's left when fa and ti go?

- Point: take the major scale and drop fa and ti: do re mi so la. Both semitones went with them (mi-fa
  and ti-do), so no step is a semitone. But the steps aren't even: 2, 2, 3, 2 and 3 semitones, since mi
  to so and la to do are three tall.
- Signature: Lesson 4's staircase loses two treads and both its short risers (card).
- Examples: C major up, then C pentatonic up, C D E G A C, 0.5 s a note; the staircase drawn under
  each.
- Try: tap Remove fa and ti. Notice: the two short risers vanish, and two tall ones appear.
- Toy: Lesson 4's staircase, Major · Pentatonic, with the semitone counts.
- Facts: R20-1, R20-2. Words: about 110.

## Step 2 of 4: Is there a minor pentatonic too?

- Point: start the same five notes on la: A C D E G. That's the minor pentatonic. With A as do, in this
  series' do-based minor, it sings do me fa so te, climbing 3, 2, 2, 3, 2.
- Examples: C pentatonic C D E G A; then A minor pentatonic A C D E G A, 0.5 s a note; then C minor
  pentatonic, C E♭ F G B♭ C (do me fa so te do).
- Try: switch Major · Minor and play. Notice: the same five notes on the ring, a different home.
- Toy: staircase with Major · Minor and the house on do.
- Go deeper: the blues scale adds one note between fa and so (R20-5).
- Facts: R20-3, Lesson 10 FACTS 8. Words: about 100.

## Step 3 of 4: How do I make a run faster without smudging it?

- Point (shown half right): fast is the goal, but in the pianists' study what counted was how many tries
  were right. So: a tempo slow enough that every bead lands in its band; repeat it; nudge it up; drop back
  when notes start to blur. Singing teachers build agility with scales and arpeggios on open vowels,
  "ah" and "oo".
- Common mix-up card: Belief "Speed comes from practising it fast." Why it seems right: you want it fast,
  so practise it fast. The evidence: in one study of 17 pianists, practice time and the number of tries
  didn't predict the next day's playing; the share of correct tries did (Duke and colleagues, 2009).
  The better idea: practise as fast as you can sing it right, then a little faster.
- Examples: the run at 0.6, 0.45 and 0.3 s a note; the beads draw together as the tempo rises, each
  still in its band (card's signature).
- Try: drag the tempo up until the beads start to blur, then back one notch. Notice: that notch is
  your practice tempo today.
- Toy: beads on a string with a tempo slider (no score).
- Facts: R20-8, R20-10 (and R20-9 in Go deeper). Words: about 140 with the card.

## Step 4 of 4: How do short notes help?

- Point: staccato (Lesson 18's dot): each note short and separate. Singing a run's notes short and
  light, then joining them, is one way teachers make the notes distinct (Arneson's staccato; coach to
  confirm). Light, never pushed.
- Examples: do re mi so la, staccato (0.15 s notes, 0.45 s apart), then the same legato.
- Try: play both. Notice: the staccato beads each start clean.
- Toy: beads on a string, Staccato · Legato.
- Facts: R20-10, R18-6. Words: about 90. (Coach may cut this step.)

   - Yes: it has no semitones, so every step is a tone · "Mi to so and la to do are three semitones. Try
     Step 1 again."

## Wrap

- The major pentatonic is do re mi so la: the major scale without fa and ti, so without semitones, in
  steps of 2, 2, 3, 2 and 3.
- The minor pentatonic is the same five notes from la; from do, do me fa so te.
- For speed, practise accurately first, then nudge the tempo up.

## Go deeper

- The pentatonic isn't universal: in 304 recordings, uneven scales of seven or fewer notes nearly were
  (R20 finding 1, R20-7).
- Where it occurs, the pentatonic tends to share its interval shape (R20 finding 2).
- The blues scale's extra note, between fa and so; the app's ear drills call that degree "Fi" (R20-5);
  no syllable taught.
- Slowing down went with better playing among 60 wind players (R20-9, "one study").
- The pentatonic is four stacked fifths, C G D A E (R20-4): keep for Lesson 21, or say it here only if
